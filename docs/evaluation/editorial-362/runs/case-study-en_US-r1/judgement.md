## 2. F1 — source fidelity of the delivered draft: **PASS**

I re-derived every claim-bearing passage from the material rather than from either checker.

Correct and load-bearing:

- Actor. Elm Quay Housing and its maintenance team act throughout ("decided to try", "chose Svale Systems after testing", "The categories were the team's own"); Svale Systems appears only in the two roles the material gives it — "configured the log and trained six staff over two sessions". No supplier voice, no "we": the brief's third-person requirement holds in every sentence including the closing note.
- Figures. 640 flats, 31 repair reports, six staff, two sessions, eight weeks, two buildings, two working days against three in the preceding eight-week period, note dated 4 December 2025 — each matches the material exactly. No count is characterised as large, small, high or low, and no comparison is drawn that the material does not supply.
- The measure is never converted: "time from report to assignment" appears as such, and "time to completion" appears only in the list of what was not measured.
- Causation. The lead and the body both carry the non-attribution ("a difference the note does not attribute to the software" / "the note does not attribute the difference to the software"), and the body also carries the workload qualification. Nothing in the draft asserts, implies or hedges toward the software having caused the shorter assignment time.
- Whose assertion. The count and the medians sit with "Elm Quay's internal note" / "Elm Quay's internal trial note of 4 December 2025", never with the maintenance team; the workload difference is stated in the draft's own voice, as the material states it.
- The reservation survives in the reader's path: "Lind did not recommend Svale Systems to every housing company" stands immediately after the positive quotation, and "not yet expanded" plus "will decide once it has checked" keeps the future open.
- All three quotations are character-for-character from the material's bullets, each a whole bullet, none welded, no hedge added or removed ("I would", "helps us, but", "I would leave an extra week"). Attribution in place is correct for all three.
- Destination of the link is described as the material's brief describes it — "can read Svale Systems' implementation checklist" — with the URL exact and no booking or trial implied.
- Publisher stance is truthful and in the material's own terms: "Svale Systems publishes this account of its customer's work. It is not independent journalism."

Four items I tested and record as within support, the first two as the weakest points in the draft:

1. **Lead: "emergencies and earlier work excluded."** The material's criterion is "work ordered before the trial" — an ordering date, not an age. "Earlier work" is looser: a reader can attach it to when the fault arose. It does not assert an exclusion the material denies, and the body two sections later states the criterion exactly ("excluding emergencies and work ordered before the trial"), so the reader meets the precise version before the piece ends. A real imprecision, not an unsupported claim. This was seen (report-2 F1) and rejected; see §4.
2. **"Cost, resident satisfaction, and time to completion were not measured."** The material says "There are no cost, resident-satisfaction or completion-time measurements" — an absence of measurements. "Were not measured" asserts, marginally more strongly, that the measuring never happened. The gap between absent and unknown is real here but vanishingly small, and it moves in the safe direction: it cannot be read as a cost, satisfaction or completion claim, which is the hazard the material names. Neither checker treated this distinction; see §4.
3. **"The team chose Svale Systems."** The material's sentence is "It chose Svale Systems after testing…", whose nearest antecedent is "The team". The draft resolves the pronoun to the team rather than to the company. Both readings keep the customer as acting party and neither adds a fact.
4. **"Maya Lind, the maintenance supervisor."** The definite article is anchored to the maintenance team introduced two sentences earlier and asserts nothing about how many supervisors that team has; no claim in the piece rests on it. The source's bare apposition would have carried the same content.

No unsupported addition, invented event, invented personal attribute, changed date, changed subject, changed modality or dropped caveat was found. F1 passes.

## 3. G2 — **PASS**; L1 — **PASS**

**G2 (case study).** All four required jobs are performed and separated:

- *Customer situation*: 640 flats, telephone reports and emails stored separately, staff on different shifts without the same information.
- *Customer action*: the team decided to trial the log, designed its own categories, kept telephone reporting open, chose the supplier after a status test.
- *Results*: the section "What the note records", which gives the figures with their exclusions, the workload qualification, the non-attribution, and what was never measured — a results section honest enough to say what it cannot show.
- *Appraisal*: "After eight weeks" is Lind's own qualified assessment, attributed in place, followed by her non-recommendation and by the open decision.
- *Publisher stance*: the closing italic line, truthful and third-person.

One weakness, short of failure. The material's judging note asks whether quotations contribute experience beyond the surrounding narrative. Quotation 2 clearly does — the cost of agreeing categories appears nowhere else. Quotation 3 is the only appraisal in the material and is irreplaceable. Quotation 1 largely restates its own frame: "explains the aim and the division of work" announces exactly the two things the quotation then says, the aim is already in the lead and in the preceding paragraph, and the division of work is restated again in the next section ("The categories were the team's own… Svale Systems configured the log"). What it adds over the narration is the concrete morning/evening pair. The reader meets the same fact three times in four paragraphs — flat, not false.

**L1.** The prose reads as professionally written English: clean subject-first sentences, no translationese, no stacked nominalisations, headings that say something ("What the note records", "After eight weeks"). Rhythm varies; the lead's dash carries the caveat without a second sentence.

The one blemish is register rather than idiom: the resolved locale is en_US, but the lexis leans British — "flats" (AmE: apartments), "by telephone" (AmE: by phone), "report a fault" (AmE: report a problem). "Flats" comes from the material and changing it would be a term change, which is why I do not hold it against the draft; "by telephone" and "report a fault" are the draft's own wording and are the places an American operations manager would notice the seam. The run's second checker saw "flats" and set it aside as locale mechanics, which is the right disposal for that word but leaves the other two unexamined. Not enough to fail L1.
