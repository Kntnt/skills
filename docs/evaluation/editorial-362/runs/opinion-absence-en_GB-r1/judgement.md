**The one flagged ambiguity.** Paragraph five: "The wing stands empty today, and nothing I am proposing would displace the usual activities in the hall's main rooms." On the reading the writer defends — what she proposes is the deferral, the study and the comparison — the clause is trivially true and supported. But the clause sits immediately after the empty-wing sentence, which is about the wing as a rehearsal space, and a resident may well read it as a promise that rehearsal use would not disturb the main rooms. The material cannot support that: sound insulation is precisely what the study is to settle, and "the hall's main rooms continue to host their usual activities" is a statement of the present, not a prediction about a converted wing. Because a supported reading exists and is the writer's stated intent, I do not score this as an unsupported assertion — but it is the draft's single soft spot, and cutting the clause (as the writer offered) would cost the paragraph nothing.

One further mild addition, noted and cleared: "That is the weakest point in our own case." The source says Reed "accepts this gap"; ranking it as the weakest point is her evaluative judgement, native to a signed opinion piece and not a factual claim about the world.

## 3. G2 — genre parts (Opinion)

**Pass.**

- **Early position.** The headline ("Test the rehearsal case before letting the wing") and the second sentence of paragraph one both state the ask before any evidence arrives: establish what the wing could do on weekday evenings before committing it for three years.
- **Support.** Three distinct supports, escalating: the survey (nine of fourteen), the named scope of the study, and the symmetry argument that neither use is currently costed.
- **Relevant real objection.** Paragraph two is the trustees' own case, stated at its strongest and in their terms ("Predictable income would help meet the hall's maintenance costs, and that upkeep has to be paid for somehow"), with the explicit concession "Anyone asking the trustees to pause owes them a serious answer on money rather than a sentimental one" — and then the genuine concession "Commercial letting may still prove the better use of it." This is the brief's requirement to treat the financial concern seriously, met without hedging the position away.
- **Identifiable action and actor.** The closing names the actor (the trustees) and three concrete, dated actions (defer the three-year decision, commission the study, publish the comparison — eight weeks). The reader knows who must do what.

The piece also conceded its own weakest point before the objection could be used against it, which is what makes the argument credible rather than promotional. Length 276 words of body, inside the brief's 250–300.

## 4. L1 — language

**Pass**, and comfortably.

The register is native British English throughout, in the idiom of a local association chair writing to a hall's trustees, not translated or generic. Specifics: "letting"/"let" used in the British property sense; "costing" as a noun for a cost estimate; "pay its way"; "for its part"; "stands empty"; "dress those nine answers up as bookings"; "upkeep has to be paid for somehow". The date form "3 July" is British. Sentence rhythm varies deliberately — the short pivot "So here is ours, gaps included." lands between two longer paragraphs and carries the argument's turn.

No Swedish syntax or calques are imported; there is no participial pile-up, no "not only … but also" scaffolding, no abstract-noun chains. Spaced em dashes around the study's scope are conventional British practice. The first-person plural for the association and first-person singular for Reed are used consistently and for different work (the association's evidence vs. her own judgement and her own ask).

Reader effect: a trustee reading this hears someone who has counted what she has and admitted what she has not, which is the only register in which "give me eight weeks" is persuasive.
