## 2. Findings

### Finding 1 — "her email" asserts Ortiz's gender, which no supplied material establishes (source-support finding)

**Draft passage.** "Ortiz explained the distinction in her email: 'We kept the uncertain dates visible. …'"

**Source passages, in the material's own words.**

- "Collection coordinator Lena Ortiz sent the following complete quotations in an email headed 'Catalog pilot: notes for publication':"
- "The archive supplied that email and the pilot note for this supplier-published case. These are the only correspondence and reporting materials supplied. There is no question-and-answer transcript, observation of an archive visit, or account of Ortiz's manner or emotions."

**What differs.** The support that is missing is a statement of Ortiz's gender. The material names Ortiz by full name and role and refers to her — or him, or them — only by surname and by the possessive "Ortiz's". It never uses a personal pronoun for Ortiz and never characterizes Ortiz beyond name and role; it states explicitly that the two supplied documents are the only materials and that nothing describes Ortiz personally. Identifying the referent of "her" as the collection coordinator, which the draft does correctly, establishes person and number; it does not establish natural gender. The given name "Lena" is a conventional association, not a fact in the material, and an association of that kind is precisely the sort of form-completing detail the contract forbids supplying.

**Two-direction test.**

- A case in which the source statement holds and the draft's fails: Lena Ortiz is a man or a nonbinary person who uses "he" or "they". Nothing in the supplied material excludes this — the material never assigns Ortiz a gender and is visibly constructed to avoid doing so. Every sourced fact in the case study remains exactly true, and "her email" is false.
- A case in which the draft's statement holds and the source's fails: none. If Ortiz does use "she", the gender-neutral wording below is still true. The neutral wording is therefore correct in both worlds; "her" is correct only in one, and the material does not say which.

**Smallest supported repair.** Delete the pronoun:

> Ortiz explained the distinction in the email: "We kept the uncertain dates visible. …"

or, if the possessive is wanted to separate this email from the pilot note, "in Ortiz's email". Either repair is one word long, keeps the sentence rhythm, the reporting verb, the section structure and the quotation untouched, and removes the only unsupported attribute in the draft.

## 3. Considered and not reported

Recorded so the accounting is complete and the two-direction test is visibly applied. Neither is a defect that must be repaired.

### 3.1 The title's present tense — "volunteers share records"

The note's claim is modal and attributed: "volunteers could consult the same records". The title states it flat and in the present. The narrow reading would be a finding, but the two-direction test does not produce a case that an ordinary reader would recognize: in American journalism a headline in the simple present reports the recent event the story covers, and "share records" in context names the same state of affairs as having the same records available to consult, which is what the note reports. The lead sentence immediately below restores both the modality and the attribution ("Volunteers could consult the same records, according to the archive's pilot note"), and every body sentence is in the past tense, so no reader is left with an ongoing-practice claim. Defensible as written; preserving it respects the editorial choice.

### 3.2 The reporting verb "explained"

"Explained" is factive about the content of the quotation, and the material warns that there is "no … account of Ortiz's manner or emotions". But the quoted sentences do state a distinction and do give the reason for keeping uncertain dates visible, so "explained the distinction" reports what the supplied email itself contains rather than an observed manner. The draft never places Ortiz in a scene, never describes tone, and never invents a question she was answering. Defensible.

### 3.3 Absences, causation and length

The draft asserts no cause anywhere: it does not claim the shared catalog saved time, cut cost, improved public access, or caused the 47 donations to be cataloged, and it reproduces the note's three absences verbatim. It adds no evaluative characterization to the counts — 47, eight weeks, 11, one session, five volunteers, two meetings are all given bare, with the pilot's exclusion of existing collections stated. The brief's 250–300 word range is met without adding material beyond the source package, so the length constraint did not force invention.
