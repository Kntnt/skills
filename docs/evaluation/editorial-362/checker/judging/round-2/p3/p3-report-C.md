## Findings

### F1 (source support) — the draft attributes to the association's submission a silence the material records only of the supplied material

- Draft passage: "Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use; **it does not establish whether that consent exists.**"
- Source passages: "The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use." and, as a separate statement about the record rather than about the document, "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status."

What differs: whose knowledge or assertion is being described. The material makes two distinct statements. The first is about the submission and is narrow: the submission does not represent that consent *has been secured*. The second is about the supplied material as a whole: it does not state the consent's *actual status*. The draft's second clause takes the wide silence from the second statement and attributes it to the document named in the first — "it" can only refer to "Our submission".

Both directions tested. A concrete case compatible with the supplied material: the association's submission says "we wrote to the building owner on 12 June and are awaiting a reply." That submission makes no representation that consent has been secured, so the material's first statement holds; the supplied material still does not state the consent's actual status, so the material's second statement holds; but the submission does establish that consent does not yet exist, so the draft's clause fails. The converse case does not rescue it: there is no case compatible with the material in which the draft's clause holds but the material's statements fail, because the draft's clause is a strictly stronger claim about the document's contents than anything the material records. The material characterises the submission's contents only in respect of the one representation it does not make; the draft characterises them in respect of the whole question of the consent's status, which the material declines to describe.

Why this is not defensible as written even though it is cautious in tone: the clause reads as a further disclosure about a document the author controls and the reader does not have, and it is the kind of statement a reader would rely on. Its cautiousness lowers the strength of the claim about the *consent*, but it does not lower the strength of the claim about the *submission*, which is asserted flatly. The material's deliberate separation of the two sentences is exactly the qualification lost here.

Smallest supported repair — keep the sentence and the candour, move the silence back to the speaker's own knowledge:

> Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use; I cannot tell the trustees whether that consent exists.

If the author prefers not to speak to her own state of knowledge either, the clause can simply be cut, leaving "Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use." — which is the material's first statement in full, and loses nothing the material supports.
