## 1. My own reading of the focus passage

The draft sentence is: "Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use; it does not establish whether that consent exists."

The material carries two sentences with two different subjects:

- "The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use." — about **the submission**, and narrow: it denies only a representation of having *secured* consent.
- "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status." — about **the supplied package**, and wide: the status is nowhere given.

The draft's first clause is a faithful rendering of the first source sentence. The second clause is not a rendering of either. Its "it" has "Our submission" as its nearest and ordinary antecedent, so the draft asserts of the submission that it settles the consent question in neither direction. The material never describes the submission's contents beyond the one representation it does not make.

The two come apart. A submission reading "we have not approached the owner and hold no consent" leaves both supplied sentences true — it still makes no representation of having *secured* consent, and the supplied material still does not state the status, because the package describes the submission rather than reproducing it — while the draft's clause becomes false: that submission does establish whether the consent exists. The package is a summary throughout ("It says predictable income would help…", "Its treasurer's signed note explicitly confirms…"); no document is quoted in full, so "the supplied material" names the summary in front of the writer, not the underlying documents. That is why the second supplied sentence cannot be borrowed to cover the first's subject, and it is why the material troubled to write two sentences with two subjects rather than one.

So I read the clause as **unsupported**: a scope and subject shift, and the repair is available at no cost — delete the clause, which loses nothing the material supports and still leaves the consent unclaimed.

The contrary case is not frivolous, and it is the one reports D and E make: if the submission counts as part of "the supplied material", then a submission stating a refusal would itself be the material stating the status, and the draft's clause follows. The disagreement turns entirely on whether "the supplied material" denotes the package's own summarising text or the documents it summarises. On the package's evident practice of describing documents it does not reproduce, the first reading is the right one.

One note on repairs, which I do not count as findings: the deletion offered by A, B and F is clean. C's preferred repair ("I cannot tell the trustees whether that consent exists") replaces an unsupported claim about the document with an unsupported claim about the author's own knowledge, and sits oddly with her chairing the association that made the submission; C's fallback, to cut the clause, is the better of its two.
