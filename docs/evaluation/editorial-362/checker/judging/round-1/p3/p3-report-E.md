## Scope and method

Every factual and attributed claim in the complete draft — frontmatter, title, byline, body — is paired below with the source passage it rests on, quoted in the source's own words. For each pair I record what could differ (thing named or measured, subject, scope, time, modality, certainty, whose knowledge or assertion it is), and where the two are not identical I test both directions for a concrete case, compatible with the supplied material, in which one statement holds and the other fails. Passages that are the author's own argument or advocacy are read as an ordinary reader would read them in context: the facts, events and views they present are tested, not the reasoning or the transition.

**Translation findings are not possible in this pair.** The source material and the draft are both English; no term is carried across languages, and no meaning can be changed in crossing one. The draft also contains no quoted speech: Reed's supplied quotation is rendered as her own first-person prose in an article she signs, so there is no translated quotation to check for idiomatic target-language voice. Every item below is therefore a source-support judgement.

General editorial and mechanical review is outside this comparison and was not performed.

## Differences examined in both directions and found not to be findings

- **"a room" for "a rehearsal room" (item 13).** Direction one: could the groups have said they would consider a *rehearsal* room while it is false that they would consider *a room*? No — a rehearsal room is a room, so the source statement cannot hold with the draft's failing. Direction two: could the groups have said they would consider some room — for storage, or for committee meetings — without considering a rehearsal room? In the abstract yes, but the draft sentence stands in a paragraph opened by "Our survey gives a reason to examine rehearsal use" and closed by "The survey cannot promise that a rehearsal room would pay its way", so an ordinary reader in this context takes "a room" as the rehearsal room under discussion. The count is not inflated and the "would consider" modality is not upgraded, which are the ways this sentence could have overclaimed. Defensible as written; no repair proposed.
- **"it does not establish whether that consent exists" (item 20).** The draft is weaker than the material, not stronger: the material says nothing in the record settles the consent's status, and the draft says the association's own submission does not settle it. The only case in which the draft's clause could fail while the source's holds is a submission that positively recorded a refusal — which would make the submission establish the consent's non-existence while still making no representation of having secured it. Nothing in the supplied material describes such a record, and the material's closing sentence ("The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status") is a statement that the status is unsettled here, which is what the draft conveys. Reading the clause as an ordinary reader would, it takes no position on whether consent exists and invites none. Defensible as written; no repair proposed.
- **"We did not ask" for "The survey did not ask" (item 15).** A case in which the association asked some group about price outside the survey would make the draft false while the source stayed true. But the draft sentence sits between two sentences about the survey and is read as a statement about what the survey contained, which is what the material says. It also preserves the disclosure in the direction that matters — it admits an omission rather than hiding one. Defensible as written.
- **Length.** The brief sets approximately 250–300 words. The draft's body runs about 270 words, so the stated length exerted no pressure to add material; nothing in the draft fills space with unsupported detail, and no qualification appears to have been cut to meet a limit. Both of the material's explicit gaps — no cost estimate, no representation of owner's consent — are stated rather than omitted, as is the absence of evidence on the eight-week timetable.

## Absences correctly preserved

The draft asserts none of these, and equally does not convert their absence from the record into a claim that they do not exist: it says the paper "names neither a prospective tenant nor a rental figure" — a statement about the paper — and is silent on complaints, entitlement and funding. No unnamed study or unnamed expert is invoked anywhere; the two evidentiary documents cited are the trustees' paper of 3 July 2026 and the association's own treasurer's signed note, both named as such in the material.

## Causation

The survey result is expressly held back from implying viability ("expressions of interest, not booking commitments"; "cannot promise that a rehearsal room would pay its way"), and the income proposition in ¶2 is kept as the paper's assertion rather than as an established effect. Chronology (paper of 3 July 2026, then deferral, then study, then comparison, then choice) is preserved in the title, ¶1 and ¶6 alike.
