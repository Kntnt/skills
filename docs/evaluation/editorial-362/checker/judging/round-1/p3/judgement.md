## My reading of the focus passage

Draft, ¶4: "Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use; **it does not establish whether that consent exists.**"

The material makes two statements, and it scopes them to two different things on purpose:

1. "The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use." — subject: the submission; scope: one way of settling the question (that consent *has been secured*).
2. "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status." — subject: the supplied material as a whole; scope: the status, in either direction.

Read in context, "it" is the submission, and "whether that consent exists" is two-sided: the clause asserts that the submission settles the question in neither direction. That is stronger than (1), which rules out only one direction, and it is a different subject from (2), which speaks of the record supplied to the writer, not of a named in-world document whose text is nowhere reproduced. The two can come apart: a submission that reported the owner had refused consent would still "make no representation that it has secured" it, and the supplied material would still not state the actual status — yet the submission would then establish that the consent does not exist, and the draft's clause would be false. The entailment runs the wrong way, so the clause is not carried by the material.

The contrast with ¶2 is instructive and, I think, deliberate. There the draft narrows an agentless source negative — "No prospective tenant or rental figure is named" — to one document, "the paper names neither". That narrowing *is* entailed: the unrestricted negative cannot hold while the restricted one fails. The consent clause is the same surface move with the entailment reversed, and that is what separates a safe scope narrowing from an unsafe one.

Two things in the passage's favour, which I have weighed and do not find decisive. The error is conservative in direction: it keeps the consent's status open and cannot mislead a reader into thinking consent is in hand. And the clause reads naturally as an amplification of the clause before it. But the amplification is precisely what is unsupported — "whether" rather than "that" is what makes it two-sided — and the fix is the deletion of eight words, well short of any cost that would argue against repairing it before delivery. A checker that raises it is right; one that clears it has let a document-scope shift through.

Classification consequence: a finding against this passage is **supported**; clearing it is a miss, not a merit.

The reasoning inverts the test: a case the material does not describe is exactly a case the material leaves open, and an unsupported claim is not rescued by the material's failing to contradict it. E's other ground — "The draft is weaker than the material, not stronger" — is true of the consent's status but not of the claim about the document, which is where the clause overreaches. **Outcome: notices the problem, then accepts the passage anyway.**
