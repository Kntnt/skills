## Findings

### Finding 1 — the submission is made to report a silence that belongs to the supplied material

- Draft: "Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use; **it does not establish whether that consent exists.**"
- Source, first clause: "The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use."
- Source, second clause: "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status."

What differs is the subject and whose knowledge is being described. The source's second
sentence is a statement about the material's own coverage — the status is not on the record
available. The draft transfers that silence to a named in-world document, "our submission",
and asserts as fact what that document does and does not settle. The material supports only
one statement about the submission's contents: that it makes no representation of having
secured consent.

Testing both directions with a concrete case compatible with the material: suppose the
association's submission states plainly that the owner has refused consent, or that consent
has been sought and not yet given. Both source sentences still hold — the submission still
makes no representation that consent *has been secured*, and the supplied material still
does not state the consent's actual status. The draft's clause fails, because a submission
saying that would establish something about whether the consent exists. So the draft says
more about the submission than the material can support, however natural the sentence reads.

The direction of the error matters for the repair: the clause is not over-claiming the
consent, it is over-claiming the document. Deleting it loses nothing the material supplies,
because the first clause already prevents any reader from inferring that consent is in hand.

Smallest supported repair — end the sentence at the first clause:

> Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use.

If the author wants the ignorance stated rather than merely implied, the alternative that
stays inside what she can assert is a claim about her own assertion, not about the
submission's contents, e.g. "and I claim nothing either way about whether it exists" — which
is true by the draft's own wording. The paired-clause form as written is the defect.
