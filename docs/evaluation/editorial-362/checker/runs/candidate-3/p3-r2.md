## 2. Findings

### Finding 1 — The submission's silence about consent is widened beyond the material (source support)

**Draft passage:** "Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use; it does not establish whether that consent exists."

**Source passages, in the material's own words:**

- "The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use."
- "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status."

**What differs — the subject and whose document is silent.** The material makes two separate statements about two different things. The first is about the *submission*, and says only that it does not represent that consent has been *secured*. The second is about the *supplied material as a whole*, and says that the consent's actual status is not stated in it. The draft's second clause takes "it" — whose nearest and ordinary antecedent is "Our submission" — and attributes to the submission the total silence that the material attributes only to the evidence package. It also changes the predicate: from "does not state the actual status" to "does not establish whether that consent exists", that is, establishes it neither in the affirmative nor in the negative.

**Case in which the material holds and the draft's clause fails:** the submission states that the building owner has told the association he would refuse consent for rehearsal use. Both supplied statements remain true — the submission still makes no representation that it has *secured* consent, and the supplied material still does not state the consent's actual status, because it nowhere reports that part of the submission. But the draft's clause fails: on that case the submission does establish that the consent does not exist.

**Case in the other direction:** the submission is silent on consent in both directions. Both supplied statements hold and the draft's clause also holds. So the two are not equivalent; the draft's clause is strictly the stronger, and nothing supplied entails it.

**Statement that would set the case aside:** none. Neither supplied sentence excludes the submission's saying something about the consent's status; the second sentence is about the supplied material, not about the submission, so it cannot be quoted to close the gap.

**Missing support:** a supplied statement that the submission says nothing either way about whether the owner's consent exists.

**Smallest supported repair:** delete the second clause, leaving the sentence at what the material does support:

> Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use.

If the author's point in the clause is the stronger and better one — that her not claiming consent is not itself evidence that consent is missing — the supported way to keep it is to make the silence, not the submission, the subject, e.g. "…; that silence is no evidence either way." That reading of "it" (the absence of a claim, rather than the submission) is available but is not the ordinary reading of the sentence as written, which is why the repair is needed.
