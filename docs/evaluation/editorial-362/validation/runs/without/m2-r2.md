### 3.1 Pairings that required checking, and their outcome

- **C9 — “But the paper names neither a prospective tenant nor a rental figure.”** Source: “No prospective tenant or rental figure is named.” The source's passive names no naming document; the draft names the paper. Report §3 case A is sound: the source sentence sits in a consecutive account of the paper, between “It says predictable income would help meet the hall's maintenance costs.” and “The wing is currently empty”, and the material declares itself “the complete source package”. The draft also stops at “names neither”, asserting nothing about whether a tenant exists. **Pairing upheld; no defect.**
- **C16 — “The survey cannot promise that a rehearsal room would pay its way.”** Source (Reed): “The survey is a reason to investigate a rehearsal room, not a promise that it will pay its way.” The draft moves from “is not a promise” to “cannot promise”, a capability claim. The material supplies the ground independently: “The survey did not ask what they would pay or how often they would attend.” An instrument that asked neither what groups would pay nor how often they would attend cannot yield a pay-its-way promise. **Pairing upheld; no defect.**
- **C19/C20 — “Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner’s consent for rehearsal use; it does not establish whether that consent exists.”** Sources: “The association’s submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner’s consent for rehearsal use.” and “The supplied material does not state the consent’s actual status.” The draft shifts the subject of the second clause from the supplied material to the submission. That shift is safe here because the submission is a described part of the supplied package, so if the package does not state the consent's status, the submission does not establish it either. The draft asserts nothing about whether consent was in fact granted or refused, which is exactly the state the material leaves. **Pairing upheld; no defect.**
- **T1/C1 — “Bellwick Hall’s wing”, “its former storage wing”.** The material's “the building owner’s consent” leaves ownership of the building open. The possessives attach the wing to the hall as a part of it, matching “the former storage wing” of the hall whose trustees propose letting it, and the draft concedes at C19 that the owner's consent is an open question — a concession incompatible with reading the possessive as a claim of title. **Pairing upheld; no defect.**
- **C6 — “makes a serious case.”** An evaluative characterisation, made in the first person in a signed opinion piece and immediately marked as the author's judgement (“That concern deserves weight.”). It attributes nothing to the paper beyond “It says predictable income would help meet the hall's maintenance costs.” **Pairing upheld; no defect.**
