## Finding dispositions

### Finding 1 — "it does not establish whether that consent exists"

**Disposition: accepted.**

**Draft (paragraph 4, complete sentence):** "Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use; it does not establish whether that consent exists."

**Source passages:** "The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use." and "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status."

**What the draft asserts in full context.** The paragraph is a disclosure of gaps in the association's own case: the treasurer's note, then the submission. The nearest and only nominal antecedent for "it" is "Our submission", and the predicate — a document establishing something — fits that antecedent. So the sentence's ordinary reading is that the submission settles nothing about the consent either way. The load-bearing word is **"whether"**. "Does not establish *that* consent exists" would follow from the first clause by entailment and be fully supported; "does not establish **whether** consent exists" adds the second direction — that the submission also does not establish that consent is absent. Nothing in the material speaks to that.

**Why the alternative reading does not save it.** "it" can be strained to mean the preceding fact (the absence of a claim), on which reading the clause is a harmless reflection and supported. But that reading requires "that" or "which", not "it", and it competes with the nominal antecedent rather than displacing it. At best the draft is genuinely ambiguous between a supported reflection and an unsupported two-directional claim about a document whose contents the material describes in one sentence only. That is unresolved support in the draft, not a failure by the report to establish its allegation.

**The report's case holds and is not excluded.** The case — a submission that states the owner has declined consent — leaves both supplied statements true: such a submission makes no representation that consent *has been secured*, and the package, which describes the submission without reproducing it, still does not state the actual status. The draft's clause is then false. No supplied statement excludes the case; "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status" is a statement about the package's coverage, not about the submission's contents, so it cannot be quoted against it. The report also tests the other direction correctly: were the status stated elsewhere in the package while the submission stayed silent, the draft's clause would hold and the supplied statement would fail. The two are not two wordings of one fact.

**Repair.** The proposed repair — delete the clause and its semicolon, ending at "…consent for rehearsal use." — is the smallest one that removes the unsupported direction while keeping the disclosure, the paragraph's candour and the first-person voice. The report's warning that any wording having the submission, the association or the author settle the status in either direction reintroduces the defect is correct.
