## Finding 1 — the draft has the submission settle a question only the supplied material is said not to settle

**Disposition: accepted.**

**What the draft actually asserts, in full context.** Paragraph 4 reads: "We must be equally clear about conversion costs. Our treasurer's signed note confirms that we have not prepared an estimate. Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use; it does not establish whether that consent exists."

I tested the competing reading that would have made this defensible — "it" taken propositionally, i.e. *the absence of such a claim* does not establish whether consent exists, a remark about what may be inferred from silence rather than about the document. I reject that reading as the ordinary one. Across a semicolon, "it" takes the subject of the preceding clause, "Our submission"; a propositional antecedent in this position would ordinarily be carried by "that" or "this". The report's reading is the plain reading, not a merely conceivable stronger one, and the draft's subject in that clause is therefore the submission.

**Whether the material supports that proposition with subject and scope intact.** It does not. The material's two statements are:

- "The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use." — a one-directional negative about the submission: it does not assert consent *secured*.
- "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status." — a statement about the coverage of the package handed to the writer, not about the submission's contents.

The draft's clause is two-directional and predicated of the submission: it asserts the submission settles the question neither way. Only the first half of that is supplied. The report's identification of the mismatch — subject and document scope transferred from the material to the submission — is exact.

**The report's concrete case survives testing.** Suppose the submission contains a passage to the effect that the owner has declined consent and the trustees are asked to seek it afresh. Then "The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent" holds — declining is not securing. "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status" also holds, because the material describes the submission in a single sentence rather than reproducing it, and nowhere states the status itself. Yet the submission does establish whether consent exists: it establishes that it does not. The draft's clause is false in that case while both supplied statements stand.

**No supplied statement excludes the case.** I searched the material for one. "The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use" is the only sentence describing the submission's contents, and it constrains one direction only. "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status" is about the package's own coverage and is silent on what the submission contains; the material marks a wider scope explicitly when it means one, as that very sentence shows, and does not do so for the submission. Nothing in the brief, the trustees' paper, the survey passages or Reed's quoted position bears on the consent's status.

**The converse direction also holds**, as the report argues: were the status stated elsewhere in the package while the submission stayed silent, the draft's clause would hold and the supplied negative would fail. The two statements come apart both ways, so they are not two wordings of one fact.

**Not a wording preference.** The defect is an unsupported assertion, not confidence or a qualification present only in the source. The proposed repair — deleting the clause and its semicolon, ending at "…consent for rehearsal use." — is the smallest change that removes it, and preserves the paragraph's candour, the disclosure itself and the first-person voice.

**Status:** this is a source-support finding, correctly classed. The report's "Translation findings: none" is correct: material and draft are both English, the draft carries no direct quotation, and Reed's supplied line is rendered as her own first-person prose with both halves of its contrast intact ("Our survey gives a reason to examine rehearsal use"; "The survey cannot promise that a rehearsal room would pay its way"). No translation check arises.
