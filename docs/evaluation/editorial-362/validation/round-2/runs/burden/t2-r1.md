## Findings — dispositions and evidence

### Finding 1 — "it does not establish whether that consent exists" — **accepted**

**What the draft asserts in full context.** The sentence is "Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use; it does not establish whether that consent exists." The subject of the second clause is "it", whose antecedent is "Our submission" — the subject of the clause immediately before it, and the only singular noun phrase available. Read as an ordinary reader would read it, the clause asserts a two-directional negative about a document the association itself wrote: the submission settles neither that consent exists nor that it does not.

**What the supplied material supports.** Two separate statements, with two separate subjects:

- "The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use." — subject: the submission; scope: one direction only, that consent has been *secured*.
- "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status." — subject: the supplied material, i.e. the source package's own coverage; scope: the status in either direction.

Neither statement carries the draft's proposition with its subject intact. The first has the right subject but half the scope; the second has the right scope but the wrong subject. The draft welds the second's scope onto the first's subject, which is exactly the document-scope shift the report names.

**The counter-case stands.** The report's case — a submission that states the owner has *declined* consent and asks the trustees to seek it afresh — is compatible with the supplied material, which is all that is required. Under it: the submission makes no representation that consent has been secured, so the first supplied statement holds; the supplied material, which describes the submission rather than reproducing it, still does not state the consent's actual status, so the second holds; but the submission does establish whether consent exists, so the draft's clause is false. No supplied statement excludes the case. The material nowhere describes the submission's contents beyond the single quoted sentence, and "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status" is a statement about the package's coverage, not about what the submission contains — it is silent on the submission's contents and therefore cannot exclude the case.

**The report's opposite-direction test is also sound**, and matters here: if the consent status were stated somewhere else in the package (say the trustees' paper reporting that the owner has consented) while the submission stayed silent, the draft's clause would hold and the supplied statement would fail. The two propositions come apart in both directions, so they are not two wordings of one fact, and the draft's clause cannot be read as a paraphrase of either supplied statement.

**Grounds for rejection, tested and unavailable.** I can quote no supplied statement that carries the draft's claim with subject, scope and qualifications intact — the two candidates are set out above and each falls short on one of the two. Nor is there a mismatch between the report's reading and what the draft says in context: the only alternative antecedent for "it" would be an expletive or a reading in which "it" picks up the supplied material, and neither is available, since the material is not mentioned in the draft at all and "it" sits in a clause coordinated with one whose subject is "Our submission". Reading the second clause as a mere elaboration of the first does not save it either: "makes no claim to have secured" and "does not establish whether … exists" differ in scope, and the elaboration asserts the wider negative.

This is a genuine unresolved support gap in the draft, not a report that has failed to establish its allegation. The finding is a defect requiring repair before delivery. The proposed repair — deleting the clause and its semicolon, ending at "…consent for rehearsal use." — is the smallest one that removes the unsupported proposition while keeping the disclosure, the paragraph's candour and the author's voice; it is supported by the first quoted statement as written.
