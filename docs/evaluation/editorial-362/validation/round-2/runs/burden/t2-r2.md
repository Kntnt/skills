## Finding dispositions

### Finding 1 — "it does not establish whether that consent exists" — **accepted**

**What the draft asserts in full context.** The sentence is "Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use; it does not establish whether that consent exists." The subject of the first clause is "Our submission"; the semicolon continues that subject, so "it" is the submission. The clause therefore asserts a fact about the submission's contents: that the document leaves the existence of consent open in both directions — it neither establishes that consent exists nor that it does not.

I considered the one reading that would rescue the clause: "it" as the bare fact of making no claim ("our making no claim does not establish whether consent exists"). That reading requires an antecedent that the sentence does not supply — the preceding clause offers "Our submission" as a subject, not a nominalised fact — and the report's reading is the direct grammatical one, not a merely conceivable stronger one. So the report has not stretched the draft.

**Whether the supplied material carries that proposition with subject and scope intact.** It does not. The material's two relevant statements are:

> "The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use."

> "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status."

The first is a narrower negative about the submission: it does not claim consent *has been secured*. That is strictly weaker than the draft's clause, which also denies that the submission settles the question the other way. The second has a different subject and a different scope — it is about the coverage of the supplied package, not about the contents of the submission. The submission is described in that package, not reproduced in it, so a statement about what the package states is silent on what the submission says. Neither statement, and no combination of them, carries the draft's proposition with its subject and scope intact, so I cannot reject the finding on the first ground.

**The counter-case, and whether it is compatible with the material.** The report names a concrete case: the submission contains a passage to the effect that the owner has declined consent and the trustees are asked to seek it afresh. In that case both supplied statements hold — such a submission still makes no representation that consent has been secured, and the package, which describes the submission without reproducing it, still does not state the consent's actual status — while the draft's clause is false, because the submission does establish whether consent exists. Nothing supplied excludes the case: the material's only other remark on this point, "The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status," is an explicit statement about the package's own coverage and therefore cannot exclude a fact about the submission's contents. The case need only be compatible with the material, not likely, so the material's silence is enough for it to stand.

The report also tests the other direction correctly: were the status stated elsewhere in the package — the trustees' paper reporting the owner's consent — while the submission stayed silent, the draft's clause would hold and the supplied statement would fail. The two statements come apart both ways, which establishes that they are not two wordings of one fact.

**Not a case of the report failing to establish its allegation.** The finding does not rest on an alternative reading of the draft, on a preferred wording, or on a qualification present only in the source. It rests on a demonstrated difference of subject (submission vs. supplied package) and of scope (one-directional vs. two-directional negative), with a concrete case on each side. This is a genuine unresolved support gap in the draft, not a report overreaching.

**Repair.** The proposed repair — delete the clause and its semicolon, ending at "…the building owner's consent for rehearsal use." — is the smallest one that removes the unsupported proposition while keeping the disclosure, the paragraph's candour and the author's voice. The report's alternative ("…, and I make none here.") also stays inside the material, since it is a statement about her own claim-making rather than about the document's contents. Both are acceptable; neither adds a claim.
