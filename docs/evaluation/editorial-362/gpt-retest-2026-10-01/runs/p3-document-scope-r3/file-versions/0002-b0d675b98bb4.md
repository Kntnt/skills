## Contextual equivalence and two-direction tests

These checks address wording that could appear broader, narrower or stronger in isolation. A hypothetical that changes the ordinary contextual reading is not a counterexample to that reading. Only F1 yields a standing case compatible with the material.

1. **“A room” / “a rehearsal room”.** For source-true/draft-false, consider groups interested in a rehearsal room but not a room. A rehearsal room is a room, so no such case exists under the contextual reference. For draft-true/source-false, consider interest only in a room for meetings or storage. That would distinguish the isolated nouns, but it is not the draft's assertion: the draft introduces “rehearsal use” and concludes “a rehearsal room”, and the supplied statement is “Nine said they would consider using a rehearsal room on weekday evenings.” There is no standing mismatch in the complete paragraph. Neither version claims a purpose-built or exclusively musical room, a particular capacity or a booking.

2. **“An estimate” / “a cost estimate for converting the wing”.** A conversion-cost estimate would satisfy both statements in the draft's conversion-cost context. An association might have prepared a rental valuation or an unrelated budget but no conversion estimate; that is compatible with the source, and the draft still holds because “an estimate” follows “conversion costs”. Conversely, absence of a conversion estimate does not establish absence of other estimates, and the draft does not make that broader claim. No standing case distinguishes the statements as written in context.

3. **“We did not ask” / “The survey did not ask”.** The association could have discussed payment outside the survey. The source allows that, and the draft's survey paragraph still holds: its “did not ask” concerns the survey. In the other direction, a survey that did ask payment or frequency questions would make the contextual draft statement false as well as the source statement. The supplied exclusion is explicit: “The survey did not ask what they would pay or how often they would attend.” There is no invented claim about all conversations or all time.

4. **“No claim” / “no representation” of securing consent.** A submission could discuss access, or state the owner's attitude, without asserting that the association has secured permission. Those possibilities are allowed by both formulations. A submission that asserts secured permission makes both formulations false, regardless of whether that assertion is true. Neither term here names a legal proceeding, a request for compensation or a proved permission. No standing two-way difference in the relevant assertion. This equivalence does not warrant the additional document-wide inference in F1.

5. **Current empty wing and continuing activities.** A wing could have been occupied last month and be empty now; both source and draft hold. A main-room event could have changed its attendance while the room continues its usual activities; both hold. A presently occupied wing would contradict “The wing is currently empty”, and would also contradict the draft's present tense. Neither wording supplies a vacancy duration or unchanged attendance. “Continue their usual activities” is ordinary shorthand for the source's “continue to host their usual activities”, rather than a different kind of actor or event.

6. **A forecast attributed to the paper / a guaranteed financial result.** An eventual letting might fail to cover all maintenance costs while predictable income would still help meet some of them. Both source and draft preserve “would help”. A paper might advocate predictable income without naming a tenant or rental amount; that is exactly compatible with both. A guarantee of full coverage or a secured revenue stream is not asserted in either, and the draft's explicit omissions prevent that contextual reading. “Serious” remains the author's valuation of the concern, not proof of financial sufficiency.

7. **“Not a promise” / “cannot promise” financial viability.** A room might ultimately pay its way, or it might not. Either outcome is compatible with the material, and neither makes the draft's present argument about these survey responses false. An expression of interest without booking commitments or payment/frequency answers cannot itself be an assurance of the eventual outcome in the sense used here. The source says, “This is an expression of interest, not a booking commitment” and gives Reed's position as “not a promise that it will pay its way.” The draft's argument concerns that assurance, not impossibility of profitability. No standing case changes the contextual proposition; it is the author's supported reasoning, not an invented empirical finding.

8. **Possible better option and the comparison.** Commercial letting could prove better, or rehearsal use could prove better. Both are compatible with “She accepts that commercial letting may prove the better option”, and the draft preserves that open choice. The phrase “a comparison should help establish that” is reasoning for the comparison, not a report that it has established commercial superiority. No completed result, causal effect or certain answer is asserted. A comparison that eventually leaves uncertainties would not make the present advocacy an invented fact.

9. **Requested eight weeks / demonstrated completion capacity.** A study might be achievable within eight weeks, or might take longer. Neither possibility is excluded by “There is no evidence that a study can be completed within eight weeks; this is her requested timetable.” The draft disclaims the assurance and asks for the timetable; it does not assert either capacity or incapacity. A claim that the evidence guarantees completion would conflict with the quoted source and is not made in the draft, including its final sentence. The requests for commissioning, assessment and comparison describe the proposed process rather than completed events.

10. **Title and opening chronology.** Trustees might ultimately reject commercial letting after assessment, or choose it after assessment. Both are compatible with the proposal and the author's “before” request. Neither title nor opening says that a lease has already been executed or that a decision to let is unavoidable. An actual commitment already made would not be licensed by a proposal alone, but the draft makes no such assertion. This is prospective advocacy, not an invented transaction chronology.

## Source-support finding F1

**Draft passage:**

> Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner’s consent for rehearsal use; it does not establish whether that consent exists.

**Source passages:**

> The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use. The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status.

**What changes:** The first draft clause is supported. The second changes the subject and scope of the information gap. The source says that this supplied package leaves the actual consent status unstated. It reports only one particular absence from the external submission: an assertion that the association has secured permission. The draft's ordinary grammatical “it” makes the submission itself the document that does not establish whether permission exists. That additional document-wide limitation is not supplied.

This is an attribution/knowledge-scope defect, not an accusation that the draft falsely says there is no consent. The draft appropriately avoids that latter inference. The source also does not settle whether the submission establishes consent's status through some different statement.

**Source holds, draft fails — a standing compatible case:** The actual submission records the building owner's current refusal of rehearsal-use permission. It therefore makes no representation that the association has secured that permission, but it does establish a negative answer to whether permission exists. The supplied source package reports the absence of a secured-consent representation and omits the refusal. Both supplied statements remain true: that representation is absent, and this package does not state the actual status. On this case, the draft's second clause is false. This is a hypothetical distinction, not a claim that a refusal occurred. No supplied statement excludes the case: silence in the package about actual status does not establish silence in the submission.

**Draft holds, source comparison fails — reverse test:** A submission could leave consent's status unsettled, while a separate supplied document states the owner's actual consent status. The draft's document-specific clause would then hold while the package-level silence would fail. That reverse case is excluded for this fixture by the express supplied statement: “The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status.” There is no standing reverse case on that package-level proposition within the given material. The forward case is sufficient for the finding.

**Missing support:** A supplied statement that the submission itself does not establish consent's actual status, or sufficient supplied contents of the submission to establish that limitation. Identifying the submission as the pronoun's referent supplies neither.

**Smallest clear supported repair:** Replace “it” in the second clause with “the absence of that claim”:

> Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner’s consent for rehearsal use; the absence of that claim does not establish whether that consent exists.

This keeps the supported assertion about the submission and makes the second clause the author's valid inference about what that particular absence proves. It preserves the distinction between an absent representation and an unknown actual status, without asserting anything else about the submission's contents. No change to the first clause or the surrounding consent wording is needed.
