## Meaning and counterexample checks for the supported passages

The accounting distinguishes differences in wording from changes in the claim. No translation between languages occurred. These are the potentially material changes in expression outside S1:

- **“Wing” / “former storage wing”, and “letting” / “commercial letting” (C02, C04).** The body identifies both abbreviated referents. A case about another wing or a non-commercial use would require reading the title apart from that identification. The supplied brief identifies “the former storage wing” and “long-term commercial letting”. In the complete draft, both directions refer to that same wing and letting proposal; no compatible semantic counterexample remains.
- **“Weekday evening rehearsals” / “evening rehearsal use” (C05).** The draft advocates examining the weekday-evening use supported by the survey: “Nine said they would consider using a rehearsal room on weekday evenings.” Evening use could in general include weekend evenings, but neither passage claims an exhaustive operating timetable or attributes weekend interest to anyone. The draft's narrower recommendation rests on the supplied interest. It does not claim that Reed opposed investigation at other times. No factual counterexample yields a defect.
- **“Room” / “rehearsal room” (C16).** In isolation, a meeting room used only for committee meetings would fall under “room” without falling under the intended rehearsal use. That hypothetical cannot replace the actual survey response: “Nine said they would consider using a rehearsal room on weekday evenings.” The draft also expressly introduces “rehearsal use”. Conversely, a rehearsal room is a room. No compatible counterexample to the contextual claim stands.
- **Plural “responses” / singular “This” (C17).** The source's “This is an expression of interest, not a booking commitment” qualifies the nine groups' reported conditional consideration. The draft carries that qualification over to “Those responses”. A booking commitment among these nine responses would conflict with that quoted qualification. Neither formulation asserts what the other five groups said. No compatible counterexample stands in either direction.
- **“Cannot promise” / “not a promise” (C20).** Read in its paragraph, the draft gives Reed's assessment of this survey's evidential limits. A room becoming financially viable later would satisfy both passages: neither denies that outcome. A claim that these responses already guarantee viability is excluded by “This is an expression of interest, not a booking commitment” and “The survey did not ask what they would pay or how often they would attend.” The rhetorical wording does not assert a universal impossibility for all surveys. No unsupported factual modal strengthening stands.
- **“Estimate” / “cost estimate for converting the wing” (C22).** A survey of likely running costs or an estimate prepared by someone outside the association could coexist with both passages. A draft claim excluding those things would be broader, but the actual sentence is explicitly introduced by “conversion costs” and attributes non-preparation to “we”, the association. Conversely, an association-prepared conversion-cost estimate would conflict with “the association has not prepared a cost estimate for converting the wing”. No contextual counterexample stands.
- **“Claim” / “representation” (C23).** Both denote an assertion that the association has secured the specified consent. Merely knowing that consent exists, or receiving consent through another actor, need not assert that the association secured it. Neither wording equates this absent assertion with absent consent. No compatible difference in what is asserted stands.
- **“Currently empty” / “is empty”, and rooms “host” / “continue” activities (C25–C26).** Both draft clauses report present conditions. The source says “The wing is currently empty; the hall's main rooms continue to host their usual activities.” Past occupation of the wing and ordinary variation in the activities are compatible with both. Neither draft clause asserts a historical duration or literal agency by rooms. No contextual counterexample stands.
- **Commissioning, obtaining, publication and choice (C27–C33).** The source expressly says Reed “wants the trustees to commission the study before choosing between uses” and proposes they “obtain the feasibility study and publish a comparison of the two uses”. The draft selects these supplied actions and advocates their preparatory order. It does not assert that commissioning equals obtaining a completed study. Delay or failure to complete the study within eight weeks is compatible with both the source's requested timetable and the draft's express disclaimer. No event, guaranteed outcome or unsupported completion date is added.

## Source-support finding S1

**Draft:** “Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner’s consent for rehearsal use; **it does not establish whether that consent exists**.”

**Support:** “The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use. **The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status.**”

**What changes:** The first clause is supported. In the second clause, the subject shifts from the supplied material to the association's submission. The source's two limits are (1) no representation that the association secured consent and (2) no supplied statement of actual consent status. The draft's second clause asserts a broader absence of status-establishing information within the underlying submission. An identifiable singular pronoun does not supply evidence for that predicate. The source leaves actual consent status unknown.

**Source holds, draft fails — concrete compatible case:** The submission contains the owner's accurate, current signed statement that rehearsal permission has been refused and no such consent is in force. It makes no representation that the association has secured consent. The supplied package summarises the absence of that representation but does not reproduce the owner's statement or disclose actual consent status. Both supplied sentences remain true, while the submission itself establishes whether consent exists: it establishes that it does not. No supplied sentence excludes this case. The underlying submission is mentioned and summarised, not reproduced in full in the fixture. This counterexample is hypothetical, not an assertion that a refusal occurred.

An alternative compatible case also illustrates the changed actor: the submission contains the owner's accurate, current signed permission granted directly to the trustees, without claiming the association secured it. The supplied summary does not disclose that permission. Again, the source's two sentences hold but the submission establishes status. This is hypothetical and asserts no actual consent.

**Draft holds, source fails — reverse-direction test:** A submission could fail to establish actual consent status yet make an unsupported representation that the association secured consent. That would satisfy the draft's status clause but fail the source's no-representation clause. It is excluded here by: “The association's submission makes no representation that it has secured the building owner's consent for rehearsal use.” Alternatively, a submission could fail to establish status while the supplied package disclosed it independently. That would satisfy the draft's status clause but fail the source's package-silence clause. It is excluded by: “The supplied material does not state the consent's actual status.” These reverse cases are therefore set aside on explicit supplied statements. Neither exclusion removes the standing source-holds/draft-fails cases above.

**Smallest supported repair:** Replace “it” with “the absence of that claim”:

> Our submission also makes no claim to have secured the building owner’s consent for rehearsal use; the absence of that claim does not establish whether that consent exists.

This changes only the subject of the unsupported clause. It retains the submission's supported absence of a claim and expresses the valid argument that this absence alone settles neither actual consent nor actual lack of consent. It preserves the first-person voice and uncertainty without making a further claim about everything contained in the submission.
