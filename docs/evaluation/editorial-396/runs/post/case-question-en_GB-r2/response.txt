# Fenwick staff could see which instruments awaited parts

Fenwick Instrument Repairs spent six weeks trialling a shared bench schedule. Manager Priya Vale describes what the team had to settle first and what Vale would change, while the workshop's own summary records what the trial established – and what the summary does not measure.

By Thomas Barregren

Before the trial, jobs awaiting parts and jobs ready for work appeared on separate paper sheets at Fenwick Instrument Repairs, which services brass and woodwind instruments. In January 2026 its team began a six-week trial of one shared bench schedule, choosing Benchline after checking that it could tell those two statuses apart.

## Fenwick defined its own job statuses

Benchline configured the workshop's choices, including who would update each status, and trained four staff in one online session. Collection arrangements were still discussed with customers by telephone.

Asked by email what the team had to settle before the schedule was useful, workshop manager Priya Vale replied: 'We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.'

## The summary leaves repair speed unmeasured

The workshop's trial summary, dated 23 February 2026, records 38 jobs entered in the schedule and says staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts. It does not measure repair duration, revenue or customer satisfaction.

## Vale looks ahead to the backlog

Vale approved continued use for new jobs but held back the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions. Asked about choosing again, and what would change, Vale wrote: 'I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.'

A manager planning a similar trial can start with the question Fenwick had to settle: what does 'ready for work' mean in your workshop?
