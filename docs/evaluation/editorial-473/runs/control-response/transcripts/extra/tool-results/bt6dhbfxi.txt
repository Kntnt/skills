# Press release

An announcement written for the editor deciding whether there is a story here and for the journalist who may take facts from it and publish them. It is not written for the public: it is written for the person standing between an organisation and the public, and it works when they can do their job from it without ringing to ask what something meant.

Write the headline as [Headlines](../headlines.md) says, within the limit below.

## What this genre asks for

**A recipient meets the parts in the order they expect them.** An editor works through many releases in a day and reads each one by habit, finding the next thing where the last release had it, so a release that rearranges the parts spends that habit before its facts get a hearing. A headline and a summary come first and work as a news item together, so that a recipient who reads no further has still been told the news. A short quotation follows the summary, saying what somebody makes of the news. The body comes next and can be facts alone, because the comment has already been made, and a second quotation after it carries one of the most important messages the sender wants the recipient to take away. Then comes what serves the recipient rather than the story: background they may use or leave, the links and attachments that go with the release, the contact details, and last of all a standing description of the organisation. A release owes no heading announcing each part: a recipient finds each one by where it stands.

**The headline states the news on one line.** The headline is the release's level-1 heading (`h1` in HTML, `#` in Markdown). The headline is at most 70 characters, spaces included. It interests an editor by the news it states and never by a word louder than that news: a figure that carries the news belongs in it, and a word of praise, excitement or urgency does not. A time from which the release may be published, where the material states one, stands on a line of its own above the headline, never between the headline and the summary.

**The summary tells the news in full.** The summary is the first paragraph after the headline. The summary is at most 60 words. It names the organisation sending the release and says what has happened or will happen, so that a journalist can copy the headline and the summary into a news item and publish it. A dateline, where the release carries one, opens that paragraph rather than standing as a paragraph of its own. The summary restates the headline's news in full, in its own words, and adds the sender and the particulars: a reader who has just read the headline meets the same news told fully, and never the headline's wording said again. The rule in [Headlines](../headlines.md) that a headline and what follows say different things in different words therefore holds here for the words and the detail and not for the news, since the two together are the notice.

**The news is in the first sentence.** The summary opens with what has happened or will happen, who is doing it, and when. That sentence and the headline above it are the only ones an editor is certain to read, and an opening that describes the organisation, sets a scene, or announces that an announcement is being made has spent them on nothing.

**Every fact is in a form that can be printed.** Dates in full, times and opening hours as they will appear, amounts with their currency and the period they cover, counts as counts, names with the roles that make them relevant, and places spelled as the recipient spells them. A recipient should be able to lift any fact straight into their own text and be right.

**What the announcement excludes is said.** Where a reader would reasonably assume something the material rules out — that a service does a thing it does not do, that a figure covers more than it covers, that a trial is a permanent arrangement — the release says so plainly and once. A sentence costs less than the correction it prevents.

**A quotation is a person saying something only they could say.** Where the material carries an attributed quotation, it appears with the speaker's name and the role that entitles them to what they are saying. Where the material carries none, none is written; a manufactured sentence of enthusiasm is the first thing an editor deletes and the first sign that the rest may be padded too. A quotation carries what somebody makes of the news — an interpretation, a conclusion, a view, a message — and never a fact or a figure of its own: a fact the news depends on stands in the summary or the body, where a journalist can lift it without the quotation marks.

**Each quotation has its place.** The first stands directly after the summary: it is short, comments on the news, draws a conclusion from it and takes a view. The second stands after the body and carries one of the most important messages the sender wants a recipient to take away, and no new fact or figure. Where the material carries one quotation, the release carries that one, directly after the summary, and it carries the message where the material gives it one. Where the material carries two and neither carries a message, each stands in its place as the material gives it, and no message is written into either. Where the material carries none, the release has no quotation, and the gap is the material's: said, never filled.

**The body is one or two short paragraphs.** They complement and deepen the summary, and they are neutral enough that a journalist can lift them straight into their own text without rewriting the substance.

**It reads as information rather than as selling.** The recipient's job is to be sceptical of it, and prose that argues for its subject invites exactly the scepticism the facts then have to survive. What survives is what the material supports and the recipient can check.

**What the release ends with is the material's.** The background carries what a journalist may use and may just as well leave out, so nothing the news depends on is left to it. The links and attachments are what accompanies the release — images, pages on the web, documents — named or linked, and nothing written about them. The contact is a named person a journalist can turn to with further questions: their name, their title, a telephone number and an email address, as far as the material gives them; a name by itself is not a contact. The standing description is a short, factual and neutrally worded account of what the organisation does, written for a journalist's use, and it stops there. Where the material gives nothing for one of these parts, or gives only some of a contact's details, the release goes without what is missing rather than with something written to fill the place.

**It is short.** A release is a page and not a document. Detail beyond what the news needs goes into the background or is left for the recipient to ask about.

## What this genre does not do

It does not write the journalist's article for them, and it does not narrate. Background, colour, and interpretation are theirs to add or leave out; what the release owes them is the news, the facts in a usable form, and somebody to ask.

It adds no requirement the base contract already holds. Register, structure, sentences, words, claims, openings, endings, formatting, and consistency are stated there once, and a rule repeated here would be one rule made into two things to keep true.

## The technique this genre is ordinarily written with

None. The order of the parts above is the whole of a release's structure, and it is a recipient's habit rather than an arc: the news, what somebody makes of it, the facts to lift, and the message the sender stands behind. An arc built over it would take the news off the first sentence, which is the one thing a release cannot spend.
# Press release — review guidance

Diagnostics for the requirements the press release genre's base half states, for a Skill reviewing a draft written against them. Nothing here is a requirement: every target a draft has to meet is in the base half, where the Skill that wrote the draft could see it, and a finding raised here is raised against a rule stated there. The examples are English and are patterns — apply what one does, in the language of the draft, rather than looking for its words.

## The shape

**Test.** Read the draft for its parts alone, ignoring how well each one is written, and check the sequence against the one the base half states — headline, summary, the first quotation, the body, the second quotation, the background, the links and attachments, the contact and the standing description. A part standing somewhere else in that sequence is a finding, and so is a part the material supplies and the draft leaves out.

**Failure.** A paragraph about what the organisation does before the news; the standing description at the top, or worked into the body as though it were background; contact details between body paragraphs; a comment placed before the recipient has been told what it is a comment on; the only comment standing between body paragraphs; background spliced through the body, so a journalist cannot lift a passage without carrying it along; detail kept below the release as notes where the background is its place; a publication time between the headline and the summary.

**Edge.** A part the material cannot supply is absent rather than invented, and that is a finding against the material rather than against the order: a release with one quotation or none is a whole release, and a release with no links, no attachments or no background is too. The parts are what a recipient expects to find and not sections to be labelled: a release carrying them in order owes no heading announcing each one, and a part is not missing because it runs to a single paragraph. A publication time on its own line above the headline is where the base half puts it, and no part out of order.

**Minimum safe correction.** Move the misplaced part whole and change nothing inside it — a part in the wrong place is rarely also badly written, and rewriting it while moving it turns one finding into two. Where a part is missing and the material carries what it needs, assemble it from the material. Where the material carries nothing for it, leave it out and raise the finding against the material. Never write a quotation the material does not have to fill a place in the sequence.

## The headline

**Test.** Count the headline's characters, spaces included, before reporting its length against the limit the base half states, and give the count. Then read the headline alone and ask whether it states the news on one line — what has happened or will happen — so that an editor who reads nothing else knows what the release announces.

**Failure.** A headline over the limit; a headline naming the organisation or the subject and nothing that happened; two news items joined into one headline; a headline that promises interest with a word — *exciting*, *major*, *big news* — where it should state what makes the news.

**Edge.** Whether it has an active verb, the present tense and a figure is what the headline guidance diagnoses, not this section. A plain headline stating the news is working; plain is no defect, and a headline with no figure where no figure carries the news is working too.

**Minimum safe correction.** Rewrite the headline alone, from the news the summary states and at its strength, and measure it again. Shorten by dropping what the summary already carries in detail, never by dropping the news; never make it louder to make it interesting.

## The summary

**Test.** The summary is the first paragraph after the headline. Count its words before reporting its length against the limit the base half states, and give the count. Then read that paragraph for the sender organisation's name and for the news, told in full enough that the headline and this paragraph would stand together as a news item.

**Failure.** A summary over the limit; a summary that never names the sender; a summary that opens on the organisation's history and reaches the news in its last sentence; a dateline standing as a paragraph of its own, so the paragraph after the headline is not the summary.

**Edge.** A dateline that opens the summary belongs to it and is counted with it. A summary exactly at the limit is inside it.

**Minimum safe correction.** Keep the news, the sender and the particulars a notice needs, and move the rest whole into the body, where a journalist still finds it. Move rather than delete: a fact cut from the summary to meet the limit is a fact the release no longer carries. Measure again after the move.

## The headline and the summary

**Test.** Read the summary against the headline above it. The base half asks the summary to restate the headline's news in full, in its own words, adding the sender and the particulars; read whether it does that, or whether it says the headline again in the headline's own wording.

**Failure.** A summary whose first sentence is the headline's sentence with a date added; a summary built from the headline's own phrases in the headline's own order.

**Edge.** A summary restating the headline's news in its own words, and adding the sender and the particulars, is the notice this genre asks for and no finding under the headline guidance's item on a heading that says again, in substance, what stands under it — the news is meant to stand in both. Shared names, figures and the one word for the thing announced are not the headline's wording said again; a summary that repeats the headline's wording is still a finding.

**Minimum safe correction.** Reword the summary's opening sentence in its own words at the same strength, keeping every fact and figure it carries, and measure the summary again.

## The first sentence

**Test.** Read the summary's first sentence alone and ask whether an editor could tell from it what happened, who did it, and when. Anything they would have to read on to learn is a finding.

**Failure.** *Fagervik City Library is delighted to announce a new initiative* — an announcement about an announcement. *For many households, a broken appliance means an expensive choice* — a scene that could open anything. A first sentence naming the organisation and its values before it names the news.

**Edge.** Where the material genuinely has no date — a thing that has already happened and is being announced now — the *when* is that it has happened, and the sentence says so rather than manufacturing a schedule.

**Minimum safe correction.** Find the sentence in the draft that does carry the news, move it to the front of the summary, and delete the opening it replaces. Where no sentence carries it, assemble one from the material's own facts and nothing else.

## Printable facts

**Test.** Take each fact in the draft and ask whether it could be typed straight into somebody else's article. A fact that would prompt a phone call is a finding.

**Failure.** *This spring* where the material gives a date; *evenings* where it gives opening hours; *substantial municipal funding* where it gives an amount; a name without the role that makes the person relevant; an amount without its currency or its period.

**Edge.** A fact the material itself leaves vague stays vague in the release and is not sharpened. Precision that the material does not carry is invention, whatever it does for the release.

**Minimum safe correction.** Replace the vague form with the material's own value. Where the material has no value, leave the vague form and raise the finding against the material rather than filling it in.

## Exclusions

**Test.** Read the draft as a busy journalist would and note what you now believe. Check each belief against the material, especially the ones the material explicitly rules out.

**Failure.** A release about a repair workshop that never says visitors repair their own devices, leaving a reader to write that the library repairs them; a trial year presented without the word that makes it a trial; a service described in terms that imply parts are sold.

**Edge.** A release does not list everything it is not. The exclusion belongs in the release where a reader would otherwise assume the opposite, and nowhere else.

**Minimum safe correction.** Add one plain sentence carrying the material's own exclusion, placed where the misreading would occur. Do not restructure the release around it.

## Quotations

**Test.** For each quotation, ask whether the named person is the only sort of person who could have said it, and whether the material carries it.

**Failure.** *We are excited to offer this service to our community, said the library director* — attributable to anybody, informative to nobody. A quotation assembled from a paraphrase in the material. A quotation carrying a fact that appears nowhere else and nowhere in the source.

**Edge.** A quotation may be shortened or its spoken syntax repaired within the boundary the writing Skill works under; what a review finds here is a quotation the material does not support at all, or one that says nothing.

**Minimum safe correction.** Replace it with what the material has the person saying, or remove the quotation. Removing it is always safe; writing a better one is not.

## Where the quotations stand

**Test.** Find each quotation and name the part on either side of it. With two, the first stands directly after the summary and the second after the body. With one, it stands directly after the summary. With none, there is nothing to place.

**Failure.** Both quotations after the body, so the news reaches the body with nobody having said what it means; the only quotation after the body or at the end; the first quotation between two body paragraphs; a quotation standing inside the summary, so the notice a journalist copies carries it; the two quotations standing side by side, with no body between them.

**Edge.** A release with one quotation, or none, because the material carries one or none, is placed correctly and owes nothing further; the gap is the material's, and a review writes no quotation into it. Where the material carries two and neither carries a message, each is placed as the base half says and neither is rewritten to carry one.

**Minimum safe correction.** Move the quotation whole, with its attribution, to its place, and change nothing inside it. Never write a quotation the material does not have, and never split one quotation into two to fill both places.

## The second quotation

**Test.** Read the second quotation for what it carries. It is there for one of the sender's most important messages; mark every fact and figure in it and check whether the release states each one outside the quotation.

**Failure.** *"We will have 180,000 kronor for the year and five volunteers on Tuesdays and Thursdays," says the head librarian* — the body's facts put in a speaker's mouth; a second quotation carrying the only mention of an amount, a date or a count; a second quotation repeating the first one's comment on the news with no message of its own.

**Edge.** A figure the quotation uses to make its message, and which the release states outside it, is part of the message rather than a new fact. A second quotation that the material gives without a message stays as the material gives it, and that is a finding against the material rather than a place to write one.

**Minimum safe correction.** Where the material carries the fact outside the quotation, state it in the body and shorten the quotation to what the speaker says beyond it, within the boundary a quotation may be shortened. Where the material carries the fact only in the speaker's words, leave the quotation as it stands and raise the finding against the material. Never add a message the material does not carry.

## The body's length

**Test.** Count the body's paragraphs between the first quotation, or the summary where there is none, and the second quotation, or the background where there is none. More than two is a finding, and so are two paragraphs long enough that a journalist cannot lift either as a passage.

**Failure.** Three or four body paragraphs working through every detail the material holds; a body carrying the organisation's history, which is background; a body that repeats the summary before it deepens it.

**Edge.** A single paragraph is a body. A body that is short because the material is short owes no second paragraph.

**Minimum safe correction.** Keep the paragraphs that complement and deepen the summary, and move the rest whole into the background. Move rather than delete: a fact moved is a fact a journalist can still use.

## Information rather than selling

**Test.** Mark every adjective and adverb of praise, novelty, or scale, then check each against the material.

**Failure.** *Unique*, *revolutionary*, *market-leading*, *long-awaited*, and *proud to* where the material establishes none of them; a benefit asserted for visitors that the material never claims.

**Edge.** A superlative the material states and supports — the first of its kind in the region, where the material says so — stays, with whatever qualification the material gives it.

**Minimum safe correction.** Delete the unsupported modifier and leave the noun. Deletion changes no fact, where rewriting the sentence around the claim often does.

## The close

**Test.** Read the end and ask what a recipient does next. Background they may use, the links and attachments, a contact with a name, a title, a telephone number and an email address as far as the material gives them, and a short standing description where there is one; and above the headline, a publication time where the material states one.

**Failure.** A contact name with no way to reach them; a contact's telephone number or email address in the material and absent from the release; an embargo stated in the material and absent from the release; links to images described in prose instead of given; three paragraphs of organisational history at the end of a page-long release.

**Edge.** A missing contact, or a contact detail the material does not give, is a finding against the material, reported rather than filled, and the same holds for an embargo the material never states.

**Minimum safe correction.** Restore the material's contact details or embargo verbatim, the embargo above the headline, and cut the standing description back at the point it stops being of use to a recipient.

## Length

**Test.** Count. A release running past a page is a finding, whatever else is right about it.

**Failure.** Background the news does not need; the organisation's history; an explanation of why the subject matters in general.

**Edge.** Where a stated length exists, that is the measure. Where none is stated, a page is the shape.

**Minimum safe correction.** Move the surplus into the background, or cut it. Facts are moved rather than deleted where a journalist might want them.
