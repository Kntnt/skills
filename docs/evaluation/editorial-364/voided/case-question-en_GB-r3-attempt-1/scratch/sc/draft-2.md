# Instrument workshop keeps shared schedule for new jobs

Fenwick Instrument Repairs gave a shared bench schedule six weeks and 38 jobs. Manager Priya Vale sets out what the team had to agree before it was useful, and what would get another week next time.

By Thomas Barregren

In January 2026 Fenwick Instrument Repairs, a workshop servicing brass and woodwind instruments, began a six-week trial of a shared bench schedule. Jobs awaiting parts and jobs ready for work had been on separate paper sheets, and the workshop chose Benchline after checking that it could distinguish the two.

## Benchline configured what the team had already decided

The workshop defined its statuses and settled who would update them. Benchline configured those choices and trained four staff in one online session. Collection arrangements were still discussed with customers by telephone.

Asked what had to be settled before the schedule was useful, Vale wrote in an email interview: “We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.”

## What the six weeks did not settle

Thirty-eight jobs were entered in the schedule, according to the workshop's summary of 23 February 2026, which says staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts. It does not measure repair duration, revenue or customer satisfaction.

The manager approved continued use for new jobs but postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions.

## Settle the status names before moving the backlog

Would the workshop choose the same way again? “I would use it again for new jobs,” Vale wrote. “I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.” For a workshop already weighing the same move, that is the order Vale's account supports: agree what each status means before any job goes on a shared schedule.
