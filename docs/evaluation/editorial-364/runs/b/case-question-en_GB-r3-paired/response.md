# Fenwick keeps its shared schedule for new jobs

Jobs split across separate paper sheets went onto one shared bench schedule at Fenwick Instrument Repairs, in a six-week trial with the supplier Benchline. What had to be settled first was what the statuses meant — and the backlog was still waiting on that.

By Thomas Barregren

Fenwick Instrument Repairs services brass and woodwind instruments, and kept its jobs on separate paper sheets. In January 2026 its team began a six-week trial of a shared bench schedule from Benchline, a scheduling software supplier, chosen after checking that it could distinguish jobs awaiting parts from jobs ready for work.

## Fenwick set the job statuses before Benchline configured them

The workshop defined the statuses itself and decided who would update them. Benchline configured those choices and trained four staff in one online session. Collection arrangements stayed on the telephone.

In an email interview, Benchline's case writer asked workshop manager Priya Vale what the team had to settle before the schedule was useful. Vale answered: ‘We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.’

## The trial evidence does not measure repair speed

A workshop summary dated 23 February 2026 records 38 jobs entered in the schedule, and says staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts.

It does not measure repair duration, revenue or customer satisfaction. The summary and the interview carry the team's reported experience, not a measured improvement in repair speed.

## Vale would allow an extra week for the status names

The manager approved continued use for new jobs, and postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions. Asked whether the choice would be made again, and what would change, Vale answered: ‘I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.’

**The findings**

1. **The body never said what the schedule replaced.** The situation the trial answered — jobs kept on separate paper sheets — sat only in the standfirst, which is often shown apart from the body or skipped, and a reader starting at the lead was never told what the workshop had been doing before. *Repaired:* the lead now names it in passing and moves on, from the text's own material.

2. **First subheading, ‘The team agreed the terms before Benchline configured them’.** ‘The terms’ named nothing the reader could resolve there, and in British usage *agreed the terms* reads as settling a contract, which is not what the section is about. *Repaired:* rewritten as ‘Fenwick set the job statuses before Benchline configured them’; the paragraphs under it are untouched.

3. **‘The summary and the interview’ in the second section.** *The interview* was a definite first mention: the exchange it points back to was introduced only as a question asked by email and never called an interview, so the reader had to reconstruct which exchange carried the experience. *Repaired:* the exchange is named as an email interview where it first appears.

4. **Second subheading, ‘Neither document measures repair speed’.** It presupposed two documents the reader had not been given, and of the two sources the section actually weighs one is an interview rather than a document. *Repaired:* rewritten as ‘The trial evidence does not measure repair speed’; the paragraphs under it are untouched.

5. **The ending carries no call to action — unresolved.** The anatomy requires the ending to call the reader to action, and this genre says that action may use only supplied offers, links and contact routes. The account supplies none, so a next step would have to be invented. The ending stands as it was, and this is for a person to settle with the material the text does not carry.

**The claim account.** No claim was removed, and none was left standing with its scope, certainty, attribution, chronology, causality or meaning moved. The four repairs touched the lead's first sentence, two subheadings and one bridge clause; both quotations came through word for word, and so did the two sentences that bound what the text asserts — that the summary does not measure repair duration, revenue or customer satisfaction, and that the two sources carry reported experience rather than a measured improvement in repair speed.
