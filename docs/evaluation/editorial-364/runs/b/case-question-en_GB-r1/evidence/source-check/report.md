## 2. Findings

### F1. Three pronouns assert a natural gender the material never supplies (source support)

Draft: "She replied:" (body, under the first heading); "Asked whether she would make the same choice again, she wrote:" (final paragraph).
Source: the material calls her subject "workshop manager Priya Vale", "Vale" and "The manager", and the interview addresses her as "you". No pronoun for Vale occurs anywhere in the brief or the material.

Identifying the referent of "she" as Priya Vale does not establish the person's natural gender; that is separate information the draft adds. Concrete case, compatible with the supplied material: Priya Vale is a man, or is non-binary and referred to as "they". Nothing in the package excludes it — the material records a name, a role and two email answers, and no supplied statement settles gender. Under that case the source statements all hold and the draft's "she" fails. In the other direction, if Vale is a woman the draft holds and the source is merely silent, which is why the defect is the assertion rather than the reading.

Smallest supported repair, which leaves sentence shape, voice and rhythm intact:
- "She replied:" -> "Vale replied:"
- "Asked whether she would make the same choice again, she wrote:" -> "Asked whether the workshop would make the same choice again, Vale wrote:"

### F2. The paper sheets are counted, and allocated one per status, where the source says only "separate" (source support)

Draft, deck: "after keeping jobs awaiting parts and jobs ready for work on two paper sheets".
Draft, opening: "Two paper sheets used to carry the work at Fenwick Instrument Repairs: one for jobs awaiting parts, one for jobs ready for work."
Source: "Previously, jobs awaiting parts and jobs ready for work appeared on separate paper sheets."

The source establishes that the two kinds of job were kept apart on paper. It fixes neither the number of sheets nor a one-to-one mapping of sheet to status. Circumstantial detail is a claim too.

Concrete case, compatible with the supplied material: the awaiting-parts jobs ran to three sheets clipped together and the ready-for-work jobs to one, four sheets in all. The source sentence holds — the two kinds still appeared on separate sheets — and the draft's "two paper sheets: one for ..., one for ..." fails. No supplied statement excludes it.

Smallest supported repair:
- deck: "on separate paper sheets"
- opening: "Paper sheets used to carry the work at Fenwick Instrument Repairs: jobs awaiting parts on one set, jobs ready for work on another." Or, shorter and closer to the draft's line: "Two kinds of paper sheet used to carry the work at Fenwick Instrument Repairs: one kind for jobs awaiting parts, one for jobs ready for work."

### F3. The approval and the postponement are placed at the end of the trial (source support, chronology)

Draft: "It ended with a summary, an approval and a postponement."
Source: "The workshop's trial summary, dated 23 February 2026 ..." and, in a separate sentence with no date, "The manager approved continued use for new jobs but postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions."

The summary's date is given; the decision's timing is not. The draft's "ended with" asserts that all three fell at the trial's close.

Concrete case, compatible with the supplied material: in the trial's second week the manager approved continued use for new jobs and postponed the backlog, and the trial then ran its remaining weeks to the 23 February summary. Every supplied sentence still holds; the draft's sentence fails, because the trial did not end with the approval and the postponement. No supplied statement fixes the decision's date.

Smallest supported repair: "It produced a summary, an approval and a postponement."

### F4. "notes that" presents the summary's statement as established fact (source support, certainty)

Draft: "The summary, dated 23 February 2026, counts 38 jobs in the schedule and notes that staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts."
Source: "It says staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts."

The material is careful here, and closes by saying what the documents do and do not establish: "The summary and interview establish the team's reported experience, not a measured improvement in repair speed". "Notes that" is factive in ordinary English — to note something is to remark on what is so — where "says" reports a claim. The attribution to the summary is preserved either way, but the endorsement is added.

Concrete case, compatible with the supplied material: the summary states that staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts, and in fact two bench staff never opened the schedule and went on asking at the counter. The source sentence holds, because the summary still says it; "notes that staff could see" fails. Nothing supplied excludes the case — the summary is the workshop's own account, and the material says so.

Smallest supported repair: "and says staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts".

### F5. The heading names a review of the names where the stated condition is a review of the definitions (source support)

Draft heading: "The backlog waits for a review of the names".
Source: "postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions."
Also in source, Vale: "I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog."

The heading states the actual condition on the backlog. The condition the material records is a review of the status definitions. The word "names" comes from Vale's answer, which is a statement of what she would do, not of the standing condition; the draft's own body sentence directly below the heading says "definitions".

Concrete case, compatible with the supplied material: the team keeps the labels "awaiting parts" and "ready for work" exactly as they are and spends the week rewriting what each one means. The source sentence holds — the definitions have been reviewed, the backlog can go in — and the draft's heading fails, since no review of the names occurred. Nothing supplied makes the names and the definitions the same object; the quotation's "status names" and the summary's "status definitions" are distinct things in this material, and the draft uses both.

Smallest supported repair: "The backlog waits for a review of the definitions".

## 3. Considered and not reported

- Title "keeps" for "approved continued use" (C1): headline compression, restored in the deck and body.
- "spent six weeks trialling" for "began a six-week trial" (C2): completion is settled by the 23 February summary and the approval.
- Deck's "counts 38 jobs" without "entered in the schedule" (C4): the sentence is about what the summary counts; the body carries the full scope.
- "wrote its own definitions" for "defined the statuses" (C13): ordinary usage for formulating a definition; the section is about who settled them.
- Heading "The trial recorded visibility, not speed" (C22): attributed in the paragraph below it and limited by the line that follows.
- "Vale approved" for "The manager approved" (C25): the material names exactly one manager.
- Lead-in dropping "and what would you change" (C26): understates the question, does not misstate it.
- The draft adds no scene, no spoken exchange, no expression and no emotional reaction, all of which the material explicitly excludes; no recommendation for other workshops is made; no causal claim about repair speed is made or hedged. Nothing to report on any of these.
