# Fenwick keeps shared bench schedule for new jobs

Fenwick Instrument Repairs spent six weeks trialling a shared bench schedule, after keeping jobs awaiting parts and jobs ready for work on two paper sheets. Its summary counts 38 jobs and does not measure repair time. Manager Priya Vale sets out what the team had to agree first.

By Thomas Barregren

Two paper sheets used to carry the work at Fenwick Instrument Repairs: one for jobs awaiting parts, one for jobs ready for work. In January 2026 the brass and woodwind workshop began a six-week trial of a shared bench schedule from the scheduling software supplier Benchline. It ended with a summary, an approval and a postponement.

## The team wrote its own definitions first

The workshop chose Benchline after checking that it could distinguish the two statuses. It defined them and decided who would update them; Benchline configured those choices and trained four staff in one online session. Collection arrangements stayed on the telephone.

Benchline's case writer asked workshop manager Priya Vale by email what the team had to settle before the schedule was useful. She replied: “We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.”

## The trial recorded visibility, not speed

The summary, dated 23 February 2026, counts 38 jobs in the schedule and notes that staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts. It does not measure repair duration, revenue or customer satisfaction.

That is the workshop's own account of six weeks, not a measured improvement in repair speed.

## The backlog waits for a review of the names

Vale approved continued use for new jobs, but postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions. Asked whether she would make the same choice again, she wrote: “I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.”
