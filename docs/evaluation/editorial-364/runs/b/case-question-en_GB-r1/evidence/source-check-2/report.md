## 4. Passages considered and set aside

Each was tested in both directions for a concrete case, compatible with the material, in which one
statement holds and the other fails.

**4.1 Title: "Fenwick keeps shared bench schedule for new jobs" vs "The manager approved continued
use for new jobs".** Candidate case: approval given but the schedule not in fact retained. The
material's approving party is the workshop's own manager, whose approval of *continued use* is the
decision that the use continues; the material records no event after 2 March 2026 that would separate
the two, and Vale's "I would use it again for new jobs" on that date shows the intention standing. The
title also carries the limiting scope "for new jobs" rather than dropping it. Defensible as written.

**4.2 Dek: "spent six weeks trialling" vs "began a six-week trial".** Candidate case: a trial billed
as six weeks that was cut short. Set aside by two supplied statements: "In January 2026 the workshop
team began a six-week trial" fixes the trial's length as six weeks, and "The workshop's trial summary,
dated 23 February 2026" places a completed trial summary at a date consistent with six weeks from a
January start. The material records no truncation. Defensible as written.

**4.3 Heading: "wrote its own definitions" vs "The workshop defined the statuses".** Candidate case:
definitions agreed orally and never written down. In ordinary editorial English "wrote its own
definitions" states authorship of the definitions rather than a medium, and the material has the
definitions ending up as configured choices and later as reviewable "status definitions" and "status
names" — a written form in the schedule. The heading's load-bearing claim, that the definitions were
the workshop's own and came before the configuration, is exactly the material's. Defensible as
written; recorded here as an editorial question, not a defect. A writer wanting the material's own
verb could use "The team defined the statuses itself" at no cost to the heading.

**4.4 "Asked whether the same choice would be made again" vs the two-part question "Would you make the
same choice again, and what would you change?"** Candidate case: a reader takes the second quoted
sentence as volunteered rather than prompted. The draft misattributes nothing — the question was put
by Benchline's case writer, as stated, and the quoted answer is complete and unaltered. Whose
assertion each part is remains correct. Defensible as written; an editorial question at most.

**4.5 "It produced a summary, an approval and a postponement."** Candidate case: the manager's
approval was settled independently of the trial, so the trial did not "produce" it. The material
presents the summary, the approval and the postponement as the trial's outcomes in a single
consecutive account, and the postponement is expressly conditioned on the status definitions the trial
exposed. No causal verb about repair speed or any measured effect is introduced. Defensible as written.

**4.6 Absence and omission.** The draft omits the interview date (2 March 2026), the approval of
publication, and the material's second caveat clause ("or a recommendation for every workshop"). None
of these omissions changes a claim the draft does make, and the draft advances no recommendation for
other workshops. Omission alone is not a source-support finding.
