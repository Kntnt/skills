## 4. L1 — language: **pass**

The prose reads as written by a British professional, not translated into one. The material is English and no quotation crosses a language boundary, so there is no imported phrasing to find; what is left is whether the English is idiomatic, and it is. "Trialling" as a verb is native BrE usage and correctly doubled. "Collection arrangements stayed on the telephone" is a crisp, wholly idiomatic compression. "Separate paper sheets used to carry the work at Fenwick Instrument Repairs" opens on a concrete image with natural stress. The subheadings are short declarative English of the kind a trade title runs, and "The trial recorded visibility, not speed" earns its antithesis. Register is steady throughout and suits independent workshop managers: plain, unhurried, no salesmanship.

Two wrinkles, neither enough to fail:

- **"Asked whether the same choice would be made again, Vale wrote:"** The supplied question was second-person ("Would you make the same choice again"), and the agentless passive is stiffer than any journalist would write unprompted. It reads as the cost of avoiding a pronoun; "Asked whether the workshop would do the same again" would have been natural and no less careful. The reader feels a small catch in an otherwise fluent paragraph.
- **"It produced a summary, an approval and a postponement."** Three nominalisations in a row is a deliberate cadence and mostly works, but "an approval and a postponement" is more abstract than the sentences around it.

Locale mechanics (spelling, the 23 February 2026 date form, curly quotation marks) are consistent BrE and are not counted here either way.

## 5. Bridges into quotations

Two quotations, in order of appearance.

**Q1.** Bridge: *"Benchline's case writer asked workshop manager Priya Vale by email what the team had to settle before the schedule was useful. Vale replied:"* — Quotation: *"We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench."*

**Class (b).** The words that put it there: "Benchline's case writer" (attribution), "by email" (occasion and medium), "workshop manager Priya Vale" (attribution), "what the team had to settle before the schedule was useful" (the question put, i.e. the occasion). Two readings are available. Read as (a), "what the team had to settle" could be said to pre-announce the quotation's content. I reject that reading: the bridge poses the question and stops there; the quotation supplies the answer the bridge withholds — *which* thing had to be settled ("what ready for work meant") and why ("a part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench"). A reader who has read the bridge does not already have what the quotation delivers, which is the test. It is not (c) either: every fact in it — the asker, the medium, the role, the question — is occasion and attribution, not a fact competing with the quotation's own.

**Q2.** Bridge: *"Asked whether the same choice would be made again, Vale wrote:"* — Quotation: *"I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog."*

**Class (b).** The words that put it there: "Asked whether the same choice would be made again" (the question put) and "Vale wrote" (attribution). Two readings, and this one is genuinely close. Read narrowly — the clause immediately before the quotation, which is what a bridge is — it names only the question and the speaker, so (b). Read to include the preceding narrative sentence, *"The manager approved continued use for new jobs, but postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions,"* it slides towards (a), because that sentence has already delivered both halves of what the quotation then delivers: continued use for new jobs, and the backlog waiting on a review of the statuses. I class it (b), because the definition fixes the bridge at the clause immediately preceding and that clause adds nothing of the answer. But the redundancy is real and belongs on the record: the reader arrives at Vale's answer having just been told its substance in the reporter's words, so the quotation's only fresh content is the "another week" and the first person. Neither checker raised it.

Neither quotation stands with no bridge.

- **Counts:** two bridges — nil of class (a), two of class (b), nil of class (c).
- **Interviewer's mouth:** the draft puts nothing in the interviewer's mouth that the material does not carry; both questions appear only in indirect speech and each renders a supplied question ("What did the team have to settle before the schedule was useful?" and the first half of "Would you make the same choice again, and what would you change?"), with no invented question, prompt or aside.
- **Unsupported bridge assertions:** none — "Benchline's case writer", "workshop manager Priya Vale", "by email", "replied" and "wrote" all rest on "a complete extract from an email interview on 2 March 2026 between Benchline's case writer and workshop manager Priya Vale" and "The interview took place by email".
