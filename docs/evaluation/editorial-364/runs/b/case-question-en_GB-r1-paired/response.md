# Fenwick keeps shared bench schedule for new jobs

Fenwick Instrument Repairs spent six weeks trialling a shared bench schedule, after keeping jobs awaiting parts and jobs ready for work on separate paper sheets. Its summary counts 38 jobs and does not measure repair time. Manager Priya Vale sets out what the team had to agree first.

By Thomas Barregren

In January 2026 the brass and woodwind workshop at Fenwick Instrument Repairs, which had kept jobs awaiting parts and jobs ready for work on separate paper sheets, began a six-week trial of a shared bench schedule from the scheduling software supplier Benchline. It produced a summary, an approval and a postponement.

## The team wrote its own definitions first

The workshop chose Benchline after checking that the schedule could distinguish the two statuses. The workshop defined them and decided who would update them; Benchline configured those choices and trained four staff in one online session. Collection arrangements stayed on the telephone.

Benchline's case writer asked workshop manager Priya Vale by email what the team had to settle before the schedule was useful. Vale replied: “We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.”

## The trial recorded visibility, not speed

The summary, dated 23 February 2026, counts 38 jobs in the schedule and says staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts. It does not measure repair duration, revenue or customer satisfaction.

That is the workshop's own account of six weeks, not a measured improvement in repair speed.

## Vale would give the status names another week

The manager approved continued use for new jobs, but postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions. Asked whether the same choice would be made again, Vale wrote: “I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.”
