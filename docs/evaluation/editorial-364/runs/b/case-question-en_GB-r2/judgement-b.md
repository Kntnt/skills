## 2. F1 — **fail** (narrowly; two dropped attributions, no invented matter)

What is clean first, because it bears on how narrow the failure is. Every name, date, figure and event in the draft is in the material: Fenwick Instrument Repairs and brass and woodwind (verbatim), January 2026, six weeks, Benchline as "scheduling software supplier" (from the brief), the check that it "could distinguish these statuses", the workshop defining statuses and deciding who updates them, Benchline configuring and training "four staff in one online session" (verbatim), the telephone continuing for collection arrangements, the summary "dated 23 February 2026", 38 jobs, the three non-measurements, and the manager's approval-and-postponement sentence (verbatim). No causal verb and no hedged causal verb appears anywhere; nothing is measured that the summary does not measure; no count is called high, low or modest; no scene, visit, expression or emotional reaction is portrayed, which matters because the material expressly excludes them; no unnamed study or expert is invoked; no quotation is welded, paraphrased-as-speech or repaired. Both quotations are character-for-character verbatim (checked mechanically).

Critically, **the draft asserts no gender for Priya Vale**. `draft-1.md` did, three times ("what **she** would change", "**Her** verdict", "**her** order of work"); the material names her only as "Priya Vale", "Vale", "workshop manager" and "the manager", and the interview was by email, so nothing supplies the feminine feature. The delivered draft uses "Vale" in all three places. That is a real personal attribute invented and then removed.

Against that, two passages leave the material.

**F1-a (clear). Section heading: "Staff could see the waiting jobs; the summary measures no repair time".** The material says "**It says** staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts", and closes by saying the summary and interview "establish the team's **reported experience**, not a measured improvement in repair speed". The heading's first clause drops that attribution and states the visibility as the article's own. The second clause of the same heading names the summary, which makes the omission in the first clause read as deliberate contrast — the reader takes clause one as established and clause two as the document's limit. The body sentence one line down attributes correctly ("says staff could see"), so the heading is the only place the qualification is lost; a qualification has to survive wherever the claim appears, headings included. The unavailable case is ordinary: the summary says staff could see the waiting jobs while some of the four trained staff never opened the schedule. Every supplied sentence still holds; the heading does not.

**F1-b (weaker, and genuinely contested). Standfirst: "A six-week trial ... replaced two paper sheets with one shared bench schedule".** The material says only that "**Previously**, jobs awaiting parts and jobs ready for work appeared on separate paper sheets", that 38 jobs were entered, and that the manager "postponed adding the backlog". "Replaced ... with" asserts that the sheets gave way; the postponed backlog has to live somewhere, and the material never says the sheets stopped. The word "Previously" is a real pull the other way — it is the material's own framing of the sheets as prior practice — which is why checker 1 set the case aside and checker 2 raised it. My reading: a mild overstatement, not on its own disqualifying, and the run was right to hand it to the user as a disagreement rather than to assert it settled. Note that the same fact is handled correctly in the lead: "**Until** January 2026 its jobs sat on two paper sheets" claims nothing about afterwards.

Two smaller items I record and do **not** count as failures:
- "The workshop **wrote** the definitions" for "The workshop defined the statuses". The material supplies the definitions as an object of their own ("its status definitions"), so authorship is supported; only the medium is unstated and the heading does not turn on it.
- "**two** paper sheets" is inferred from "separate paper sheets" for two named categories. The inference is the natural one and the rest of the draft depends on it consistently.

One item deserves naming even though both checkers considered and dismissed it. The closing "**What travels is** Vale's order of work" stands one clause after "The trial cannot say whether the same schedule would suit another workshop", and asserts that something *does* transfer — in a piece whose material says the summary and interview are not "a recommendation for every workshop". The content transferred (agree the status names, then enter the jobs) is Vale's and is doubly supported, and the sentence is marked as hers, so this stays on the right side of the line; but it is the one place where the draft leans on the material rather than resting on it, and it leans immediately after acknowledging the limit.

Unknown is kept apart from absent throughout, with the single exception of F1-a: the draft nowhere converts "the summary does not measure X" into "X did not happen", and `draft-1`'s "no repair time was measured" — which did exactly that — was repaired out.

## 3. G2 — **pass** (with a real redundancy)

All required parts are present, in order, each doing its own job: headline ("Fenwick keeps the shared schedule for new jobs"), standfirst (three sentences: the change, the numbers, what Vale supplies), byline ("By Thomas Barregren"), lead (the business, the prior state, the trial, the supplier and why it was chosen), three sections, and an ending that sets the limit and hands the reader the transferable part.

The customer account carries all four elements. **Situation**: two paper sheets, one for jobs awaiting parts, one for jobs ready for work. **Action**: the workshop settled which statuses and who updates them; Benchline configured and trained; the telephone stayed. **Results**: 38 jobs, staff could see which instruments were waiting, and — stated, not hidden — no repair duration, revenue or satisfaction measured. **Appraisal**: Vale would use it again for new jobs, with another week on the status names first. The publisher stance is truthful and visible without being confessed: Benchline is named as the supplier in the lead and as the employer of the interviewer at the bridge ("Benchline's case writer"), so the reader knows whose case this is and who asked the questions. The customer is unmistakably an acting party, not a recipient — the workshop "settled itself" what the statuses were, chose the supplier after a check, and its manager approved and postponed. There is no call to action, which is correct: the brief supplies no offer, link or contact route, and the reply says so plainly rather than inventing a next step.

The weakness is repetition between display parts. The standfirst and the lead both carry "six-week trial", "two paper sheets" and "one shared bench schedule" — a reader who has read the standfirst meets a third of the lead as already known, and the lead has to earn its place on what it adds (brass and woodwind, the supplier, the selection check). Separately, the headline "Fenwick keeps the shared schedule for new jobs" and the third section heading "Vale would use the schedule again for new jobs" are near-paraphrases: two of the draft's five display lines make the same point, which costs the third section the chance to announce anything new. Distinct enough to pass, not as distinct as three sections and a headline should be.

## 4. L1 — **pass**

The prose sounds professionally written in British English, with no source-language interference (the material is English throughout, so the risk here is register rather than transfer). Idiom is native and often good: "Collection arrangements stayed on the telephone" does in six words what a weaker draft would do in twenty; "chosen after checking that it could tell the two apart" is plain journalistic English; "Vale's verdict comes with a condition and a delay" and "What travels is Vale's order of work" are both confident, unpadded constructions. The fronted object in "Which statuses the schedule would carry, and who would update them, the workshop settled itself" is mannered but grammatical and does real work — it puts the workshop's agency in the stressed position, which is the sentence's point. Semicolon and comma use is correct; the em-dash-free, short-sentence rhythm suits the genre.

One blemish, below the threshold: "**Asked by email by** Benchline's case writer" stacks two *by*-phrases with different functions two words apart, and the ear catches it. "Asked over email by Benchline's case writer" or "Asked, by email, what had to be settled first, Vale answered" removes it. Locale mechanics are consistent (curly quotation marks, "23 February 2026", no Americanisms) but are not L1 and did not enter this verdict.

## 5. Bridges into quotations

**Quotation 1.**
- Bridge, in full: *"Asked by email by Benchline's case writer what had to be settled first, Vale answered:"*
- Quotation, in full: *"We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench."*
- **Class (b).** The words that put it there: "by email" (occasion), "by Benchline's case writer" and "Vale answered" (attribution), "what had to be settled first" (subject). The bridge names the topic and withholds every part of the answer — what had to be settled, that it was the meaning of *ready for work*, and the speaker's own image of a part in the building that has not reached the right bench. Second reading, and why I rejected it: "what had to be settled first" could be read as (a), since it appears to announce the quotation's content. It does not; naming the subject a quotation goes on to speak about is not (a), and here the reader learns from the quotation everything the quotation exists to deliver.

**Quotation 2.**
- Bridge, in full: *"Vale's verdict comes with a condition and a delay:"*
- Quotation, in full: *"I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog."*
- **Class (b)**, chosen over a live (a) reading. The words: "Vale's" and "verdict" (attribution, and the occasion — an answer to whether she would choose the same again); "comes with a condition and a delay" is a structural preview, and it is the reason this one is arguable. Reading one, (b): the bridge tells the reader the endorsement is twice qualified but says nothing about *what* the qualification is, so the quotation still delivers the restriction to new jobs and the length of the delay. Reading two, (a): the bridge pre-announces both of the quotation's two moves, leaving the quotation to supply only their values. I choose (b) because the substance — "for new jobs", "another week", "before adding the backlog" — arrives only in the quotation, and because the bridge adds a reading of the answer (that it is a verdict, qualified) that the answer does not state about itself. **But note**: the section heading two lines above, "Vale would use the schedule again for new jobs", *does* restate the quotation's first sentence almost word for word. It is not the bridge under the definition given — the bridge is the clause immediately before the quotation — yet its effect on the reader is class-(a): by the time the quotation arrives, half of it has been said. That is the strongest bridge-adjacent criticism available against this draft.

- **No quotation stands without a bridge.** Both are introduced.

**Counts:** class (a) 0, class (b) 2, class (c) 0, across two quotations.

**Interviewer's mouth:** the draft quotes no interviewer utterance and invents none; the single rendering is indirect — "Asked ... what had to be settled first" for the supplied "What did the team have to settle before the schedule was useful?" — which compresses the question by replacing its endpoint ("before the schedule was useful") with an ordinal ("first"), a change of emphasis rather than of content, and the second supplied question ("Would you make the same choice again, and what would you change?") is not attributed to anyone, its substance surfacing only as the writer's word "verdict".

**Unsupported bridge assertion:** none — "by email", "Benchline's case writer" and "Vale answered" are each supplied verbatim by the material ("a complete extract from an email interview on 2 March 2026 between Benchline's case writer and workshop manager Priya Vale"), and "a condition and a delay" is a characterisation whose two referents are both inside the quotation that follows.

## 6. Quoted speech

Both quotations are **verbatim**, checked by string match against `work/source.md`, and both survive intact in every respect the guidance protects. Quotation 1 keeps the hedge "did not **always** mean" — the single most droppable word in the draft, and the one whose loss would have manufactured a flat claim Vale did not make — and keeps her own image, "reached the right bench", rather than smoothing it into the article's register. Quotation 2 keeps both conditional "would" modals (not "will"), the scope limit "for new jobs", the figure "another week", and the ordering "before adding the backlog". First person ("We had to agree", "I would") is untouched, no two answers are welded into one sentence, no paraphrase is presented as speech, and the material contains no self-correction to mishandle. Stance — willing, qualified, not triumphant — is exactly what the surrounding prose presents.

**Nothing offered is missing.** The material says the extract is complete and that publication was approved by Vale; the extract contains exactly two answers, and the draft carries both, whole. There is no third quotation to be had, and the draft does not pretend otherwise or reach for scene or reaction that the material expressly says is not recorded.

## 8. Stop or delivery

**A valid delivery** — correct in process, imperfect in product.

Why valid: the run used both comparisons, repaired every finding it could reach in the round where repair was allowed (four of four from report 1, including the invented gender, which was the most serious defect in either draft), and then, faced with two findings arriving after the last comparison, chose to deliver the exact prose a checker had read rather than ship an unchecked edit. It disclosed both findings above the draft, quoted the smallest repair for each, marked which one the two checkers disagreed about, and said which is the user's to settle. It also stated plainly what the material could not support — no measured outcome, no call to action, no reportage colour, nothing known about where the backlog is kept — instead of filling those holes. The reply's length note (337 words whole, 282 from the lead, against a brief of approximately 250–300; my count confirms both) says the overage will be closed by cutting, not that it was padded to reach a number. That is the honest disposition of a run that has run out of comparisons.

The reservation: what shipped carries F1-a, a real dropped attribution in a section heading, for which an exact one-line repair was already written down. The user receives a draft they must edit before publication, and the reply tells them so. A reader who took the delivered text at face value would come away believing the article vouches for the visibility claim, which the material does not let it do. So: a delivery that should have happened, of a draft that should have had one more heading changed — and the run's own reply is the reason the user can make that change.
