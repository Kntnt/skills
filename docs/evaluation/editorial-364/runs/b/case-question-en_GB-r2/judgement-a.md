## 2. F1 — source support

**Verdict: fail, narrowly.** Nothing in the draft is fabricated. There is no invented event, no invented personal attribute, no scene, no expression, no emotional reaction, no causal verb open or hedged, no evaluative characterisation of any count, no unnamed study or expert, no date moved, no figure altered, no misquotation. The draft is unusually disciplined about the material's stated limits and says out loud, twice, what the trial does not establish. It fails only on attribution and scope, in four places — one of which the run concedes in its own reply.

**Defects.**

1. **Section heading: "Staff could see the waiting jobs; the summary measures no repair time".** The material carries the first clause only as the summary's say-so — "**It says** staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts" — and expressly limits the whole package: "The summary and interview establish the team's **reported experience**." The heading's first clause states it flatly as the article's own. The second clause of the same heading names the summary, which sharpens the contrast: the reader is taught that the attribution matters here, then handed the first clause without one. The body paragraph beneath it attributes correctly, which makes the heading the single place the caveat is dropped, and a qualification has to survive wherever the claim appears. Case in which the draft fails and the source holds: two of the four trained staff never opened the schedule and went on asking at the bench; "the summary says staff could see" stays exactly true, "staff could see" does not. Seen by checker 2 (Finding 2), conceded by the writer, delivered anyway.

2. **Standfirst: "A six-week trial at Fenwick Instrument Repairs **replaced** two paper sheets with one shared bench schedule."** The material says only that jobs "**Previously** … appeared on separate paper sheets", that 38 jobs were entered, and that the manager "**postponed adding the backlog**". "Replaced … with" asserts the sheets gave way. The postponed backlog points the other way: those jobs were deliberately not entered, so something still holds them, and the sheets are the only place the material names. The lead's own "Until January 2026 its jobs sat on two paper sheets" is the safe form of the same fact and does not carry the defect — which is what makes the standfirst's stronger verb an addition rather than a reading. Checker 1 set this aside on the word "Previously"; checker 2 raised it as Finding 1. Checker 2 is right: "Previously" fixes when the sheets held the jobs, not that they stopped.

3. **Byline: "By Thomas Barregren".** The brief states "No author name or call to action is supplied." The draft asserts an authorship the material does not carry. Both checkers excluded it from findings by instruction, and the reply flags it and tells the user to change it before publication, which is the right handling — but as a matter of F1 it is an attribution from outside the material, and I record it as one.

4. **Ending: "What travels is Vale's order of work: agree the status names, then enter the jobs."** The order itself is Vale's and is stated twice in the material. What is not supported is that anything travels: the material says the summary and interview are "not … a recommendation for every workshop", and the draft's own preceding sentence concedes the trial "cannot say whether the same schedule would suit another workshop" — then asserts a transfer in the next breath. Mild, and hedged as Vale's rather than as a finding, but it is the writer's own advocacy reaching past the supplied limit. Both checkers saw it and cleared it.

**Checked and clean.** Chronology (January 2026 start, six weeks, 23 February summary, the March interview implied only as "by email"): intact, and the interview date's omission drops no qualification. Author perspective: the draft never claims a visit or a spoken exchange, and says "Asked by email", which matches "No workshop visit, spoken exchange, expression or emotional reaction is recorded". Publisher stance: Benchline is named as the supplier *and* as the interviewer's employer, so the reader is told whose writer asked the questions. Causal limits: "chosen after checking" keeps the material's sequence without cause; repair duration, revenue and satisfaction appear only as things the summary does not measure. Unknown kept apart from absent everywhere except defect 1 above. Modality: "would use it again", "approved continued use", "postponed" all as supplied. Counts: 38 jobs, four staff, one session, six weeks — all exact, none characterised as large or small, and no count-size judgement is made anywhere, so no supplied comparison is needed.

**Terms, checked both ways.** "tell the two apart" ↔ "distinguish these statuses": in this material the two paper-sheet categories are the two statuses, and nothing could fall under one term and not the other. "waiting jobs" ↔ "instruments … waiting for parts": the material's own category is "jobs awaiting parts", and a job here is the repair of an instrument; no gap either way. "set up the software" ↔ "configured those choices": "set up" is the wider term and could cover installation or migration the material does not report, but the body narrows it to the source's own sentence in the next line, so nothing unsupported reaches the reader. "wrote the definitions" ↔ "defined the statuses": authorship is supported and the material supplies the definitions as an object of their own ("its status definitions"); only the medium is unstated, and nothing turns on it. "two paper sheets" ↔ "separate paper sheets" for two categories: an inference, and the only available one. "stayed on the telephone" ↔ "continued to use telephone calls … with customers": the dropped "with customers" changes no claim; the exclusivity reading is available but the draft adds no claim that the schedule was barred from the subject.

## 3. G2 — required parts

**Verdict: pass.**

Headline, standfirst, byline, lead, three sections, ending — present, in that order, each with its own job. The headline carries the decision ("keeps the shared schedule for new jobs"), not a benefit. The standfirst does three different things the headline does not: the shape of the change, the size of the evidence ("counts 38 jobs and no repair times"), and what the manager is here to say. The lead gives the situation and the action. The three sections divide cleanly: who did what (workshop defines, supplier configures), what the summary shows and does not show, and the manager's appraisal. The ending states the limit and leaves the reader with the transferable sequence.

**Customer account.** Situation: two paper sheets, two statuses, no shared view. Action: the team chose Benchline after checking it could distinguish the statuses, defined the statuses itself, decided who would update them. Results: 38 jobs entered, staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts, nothing measured about duration, revenue or satisfaction — the absence is stated, not hidden. Appraisal: Vale would use it again for new jobs and would allow another week on the status names.

**Publisher stance: truthful, and well handled.** Benchline is introduced as "scheduling software supplier Benchline", the publisher's role in the interview is disclosed ("Benchline's case writer"), and the piece claims no measured improvement anywhere. A reader is never misled about whose article this is.

**The customer is left an acting party, strongly.** "Which statuses the schedule would carry, and who would update them, the workshop settled itself"; "The workshop wrote the definitions; Benchline set up the software"; "The manager approved continued use … but postponed adding the backlog". The supplier configures and trains; every decision in the piece is the workshop's.

**Call to action.** Absent, and correctly so: the brief supplies no offer, link or contact route, and the genre builds the ending only from supplied ones. The reply names the gap and says what would close it. Not a G2 failure.

Reader effect, and the one weakness: the standfirst and the lead both carry "two paper sheets … one shared bench schedule", so the second paragraph re-treads ground the reader has just covered. It costs the lead a little of its lift. Not enough to fail the criterion.

## 4. L1 — language

**Verdict: pass.** This reads as British English written by someone who writes for a living, not as a translation or a machine register.

Native idiom, unforced: "its jobs **sat on** two paper sheets"; "checking that it could **tell the two apart**"; "Collection arrangements **stayed on the telephone**"; "postponed adding the backlog". No Americanism, no calque, no source-language syntax showing through. Sentence rhythm varies deliberately — the long chronological lead against the three short declaratives of the second section.

One sentence takes a risk and earns it: "Which statuses the schedule would carry, and who would update them, the workshop settled itself." The fronted object is a real English construction, and it puts the workshop at the end of its own sentence, where the emphasis falls — which is the point the paragraph is making. A reader feels the agency land.

Two small blemishes, neither disqualifying: "Asked **by** email **by** Benchline's case writer" repeats the preposition within five words and reads as a stumble; and "The workshop's summary **counts 38 jobs and no repair times**" yokes a count to an absence in one verb, which is neat on the second reading and slightly strained on the first.

Locale mechanics (spelling, curly quotation marks, the 23 February date form) are consistent with en_GB and are not judged here.

## 5. Bridges into quotations

**Quotation 1.**
- Bridge, in full: "Asked by email by Benchline's case writer what had to be settled first, Vale answered:"
- Quotation, in full: "We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench."
- **Class (b).** The words that put it there: "Asked by email" (the occasion), "by Benchline's case writer" (who asked), "Vale answered" (the attribution), "what had to be settled first" (the question the quotation answers, not its answer). The bridge names the subject the quotation goes on to speak about and stops. A reader who has read it knows a question was asked and by whom, and does not yet know that the thing to be settled was the meaning of *ready for work*, or that a part could be in the building without reaching the right bench — both of which the quotation delivers. Second reading considered and rejected: one could call "by email" and "Benchline's case writer" facts the quotation does not carry, pushing it to (c), but class (b) expressly allows the occasion and the attribution, which is all those two are.

**Quotation 2.**
- Bridge, in full: "Vale's verdict comes with a condition and a delay:"
- Quotation, in full: "I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog."
- **Class (b), with a second reading given.** The attribution is "Vale's"; "verdict" names the occasion, which the material supports — the answer was given to "Would you make the same choice again, and what would you change?". The extra freight is "comes with a condition and a delay", which characterises the *shape* of what follows without supplying its content: the reader does not learn from the bridge that the limit is "for new jobs", that the delay is "another week", or what the week is for. **Second reading, toward (a):** the bridge does tell the reader in advance that the endorsement is qualified and that a wait is attached, so the quotation's two moves are announced before they arrive and land as confirmation rather than as news. I chose (b) because the quotation still carries everything that makes those two moves worth reading — the scope, the duration and the reason — and a bridge that announces a shape has not delivered a substance. **A third reading, toward (c), considered and rejected:** if "condition" is taken to assert that Vale attached a proviso to re-use, that is a fact the quotation does not carry — she stated a scope limit ("for new jobs"), not a condition on it. I did not class it (c) because "condition" is ordinary loose usage for a qualified endorsement and the quotation follows immediately, so the reader corrects it in the same breath. Both checkers logged the same wobble as an editorial question; checker 1's "a limit and a delay" would track the speech more closely.

**No quotation stands without a bridge.** Both are introduced.

**The three answers.** Counts: class (a) 0, class (b) 2, class (c) 0, out of two quotations and two bridges. Interviewer: the draft puts no question or utterance in an interviewer's mouth that the material does not carry — the one question it renders ("what had to be settled first") is an indirect compression of the supplied "What did the team have to settle before the schedule was useful?", quoting nothing, and the second supplied question is not rendered at all. Bridge support: neither bridge asserts anything the material does not support — "by email" and "Benchline's case writer" are the material's own ("an email interview … between Benchline's case writer and workshop manager Priya Vale"), and "verdict" is what the material makes the second answer; the nearest thing to an unsupported bridge word is "condition" for a scope limit, treated above.

One dropped nuance worth recording: "what had to be settled first" loses the supplied question's endpoint, "before the schedule was useful", and can be misread as asking for the first item in a queue rather than for everything that had to precede. The draft's closing sentence restores the sequence, so the reader is not left wrong; it is a compression, not a defect.

## 6. Quoted speech

Both quotations are **character-for-character identical** to the supplied extract. I compared them word by word against `work/source.md`.

- Quotation 1: meaning, stance and certainty intact. The hedge "did **not always** mean" survives — removing it would have manufactured a confidence Vale did not offer. The speaker's own image, "reached the right bench", is not smoothed into the surrounding register, and it is the most distinctive thing she says.
- Quotation 2: both "would" modals kept (not "will"), the scope limit "for new jobs" kept, the figure "another week" kept, and the ordering "before adding the backlog" kept. Stance is willingness with a reservation, and it stays that.
- No welding: the two answers are given separately, to their separate questions, and are never joined into one sentence.
- No paraphrase is presented as speech, and no quotation is assembled from fragments.
- Quote approval ("Publication of the extract was approved by Vale") is a human arrangement; the draft neither claims nor needs to mention it, and its silence is correct.

**Missing quotations: none.** The material offers exactly two Vale answers and permits both; the draft uses both, in full, in the material's own order. There is no third utterance in the package to omit.

## 8. Stop or delivery

**A valid delivery, with one reservation.**

Valid, because the thing delivered is sound where it matters most: two verbatim quotations with hedge, modality, scope and voice intact; no fabricated fact, figure, scene or emotion; no causal claim; the material's own limits stated three times inside the article (no measured duration, revenue or satisfaction; the backlog postponed; the trial cannot speak for another workshop); and the delivered prose is exactly the prose the last comparison read, so nothing unchecked reached the user. The reply does not launder the defects — it opens with "Delivered with known defects", names both, quotes the smallest repair for each, and reports honestly that the two comparisons disagreed about the standfirst and that this one is the user's to settle. A user reading that reply can fix both in under a minute and knows precisely what they are fixing.

The reservation: defect 2-2 should not have shipped. The writer agreed it was a support defect and left it in, on a rule about unchecked prose that this particular repair cannot violate — adding "The summary says" to a heading subtracts a claim and adds none, and no comparison is needed to know that. The honest disclosure converts a delivery failure into a delivery with a flaw, and that is the right description of this run: repair discipline good on what it was told, judgement one step short on what it had already worked out for itself.
