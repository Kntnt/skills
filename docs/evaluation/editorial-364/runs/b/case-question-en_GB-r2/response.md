# Fenwick keeps the shared schedule for new jobs

A six-week trial at Fenwick Instrument Repairs replaced two paper sheets with one shared bench schedule. The workshop's summary counts 38 jobs and no repair times. Manager Priya Vale says what had to be agreed first, and what to allow more time for.

By Thomas Barregren

Fenwick Instrument Repairs services brass and woodwind instruments. Until January 2026 its jobs sat on two paper sheets, one for those awaiting parts and one for those ready for work; that month the team began a six-week trial of a shared bench schedule from scheduling software supplier Benchline, chosen after checking that it could tell the two apart.

## The workshop wrote the definitions; Benchline set up the software

Which statuses the schedule would carry, and who would update them, the workshop settled itself. Benchline configured those choices and trained four staff in one online session. Collection arrangements stayed on the telephone.

Asked by email by Benchline's case writer what had to be settled first, Vale answered:

> “We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.”

## Staff could see the waiting jobs; the summary measures no repair time

The workshop's summary, dated 23 February 2026, records 38 jobs entered in the schedule and says staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts. It does not measure repair duration, revenue or customer satisfaction.

The manager approved continued use for new jobs but postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions.

## Vale would use the schedule again for new jobs

Vale's verdict comes with a condition and a delay:

> “I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.”

The trial cannot say whether the same schedule would suit another workshop. What travels is Vale's order of work: agree the status names, then enter the jobs.
