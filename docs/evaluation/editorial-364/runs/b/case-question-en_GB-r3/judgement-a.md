## 2. F1 — source support

**Pass**, with three cautions and one addition that the run itself declares.

Everything in the judged draft that reports the workshop, the trial, the documents or the interview traces to a supplied sentence. I checked each claim against the material independently of both reports.

Supported and exact: the trade ("services brass and woodwind instruments", verbatim); the date and duration ("In January 2026 ... a six-week trial"); the prior arrangement ("separate paper sheets", the count now absent); the reason for the choice ("chosen after checking that it could distinguish jobs awaiting parts from jobs ready for work" — the material's own anaphor "these statuses" expanded into the material's own two terms, with nothing in this context falling under one term and not the other); the division of labour ("The workshop defined the statuses itself" against "Benchline configured those choices"); the figures ("four staff", "one online session", "38 jobs", all uncharacterised — no count is called high, low or modest anywhere); the document's date ("23 February 2026"); the three unmeasured things, verbatim; the conditional postponement ("until the team had reviewed its status definitions"); and the reporting verbs that keep the visibility claim the summary's own ("records", "says").

Chronology, modality and causation hold. The draft contains no causal verb and no causal hedge: nothing in it says the schedule shortened, improved or caused anything. "chosen after checking" keeps a sequence a sequence. The conditional in the heading "Vale **would** allow an extra week" keeps an intention an intention and attributes it by name. The publisher's stance is truthful throughout: Benchline configured and trained, and did not define.

The material's own limiting sentence is carried into the draft's own voice — "The summary and the interview carry the team's reported experience, not a measured improvement in repair speed" — which is the single most important caveat in the package and is not dropped.

### Cautions (none of them a fail on its own)

1. **Dropped exclusion.** The material's limiting sentence reads "not a measured improvement in repair speed **or a recommendation for every workshop**". The draft keeps the first exclusion and drops the second. It does not breach it — the piece recommends nothing to workshops generally, and the audience-facing framing is absent — so no claim changes. But the draft is addressed to independent repair workshop managers, which is the readership that second exclusion exists to protect, and one clause would have kept it.
2. **"the backlog was still waiting on that."** "That" points back to "what had to be settled first was what the statuses meant". In the material, what the backlog waits on is a *review of the status definitions*, while Vale reports the meaning of "ready for work" as something the team *had to agree* — settled, in the past. The draft therefore lets a reader take the meaning question as still open. The material does support the status question being not yet closed (the review is outstanding and Vale would give it another week), so this is imprecision rather than an unsupported claim.
3. **"Jobs split across separate paper sheets went onto one shared bench schedule."** Read as migration, this fails: the jobs already on the sheets are the backlog, which the material says was expressly *not* added. Read as a statement of what the trial changed for jobs of those two statuses, it holds, and the summary's "staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts" shows both statuses living in the one schedule. The very next clause tells the reader the backlog was outstanding, which blocks the migration reading. Defensible as written, but the sentence is doing more work than the material comfortably bears.

### The one addition

**"By Thomas Barregren."** The brief states "No author name or call to action is supplied," and the material names no author. The byline is an attribution that does not come from the supplied material. Both checkers set it aside as coming from the invocation rather than the material, and the response declares it plainly and tells the user to change it before publication if the piece is to run unsigned or under Benchline's name. Declared and correctable, so I do not fail F1 on it; but it is the draft's only assertion with no source, and a reader of the published piece would have no way to know that.

### Correctly kept apart: unknown from absent

The draft never says the trial produced no results, that the schedule did not speed repairs, or that customers noticed nothing. It says the documents do not measure those things. That is the distinction this material is built to test, and the delivered text holds it — including in a heading, which is where it is easiest to lose. The first draft did lose it there and was repaired (see §6).

## 4. Bridges into quotations

Two quotations, in order.

### Quotation 1

- **Bridge (in full):** "Benchline's case writer asked workshop manager Priya Vale by email what the team had to settle before the schedule was useful. Vale answered:"
- **Quotation (in full):** "‘We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.’"
- **Class: (b).** The words that put it there are "Benchline's case writer" (attribution), "by email" (occasion), "workshop manager Priya Vale" (subject and role), and "what the team had to settle before the schedule was useful" — which names the *question*, i.e. the subject the quotation goes on to speak about, without delivering the answer. Nothing in the bridge tells the reader what the team had to settle; the quotation alone supplies "what ready for work meant" and the bench distinction.
- **Second reading considered:** it could be read as (c), since "by email" and the interviewer's identity are facts the quotation does not itself carry. I chose (b) because those facts are precisely the attribution and the occasion, which (b) covers by name; a (c) reading would make every attributed quotation class (c).

### Quotation 2

- **Bridge (in full):** "Asked whether the choice would be made again, and what would change, Vale answered:" — preceded in the same paragraph by "The manager approved continued use for new jobs, and postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions."
- **Quotation (in full):** "‘I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.’"
- **Class: (b).** The bridge proper is "Asked whether the choice would be made again, and what would change" — the occasion and the question put — plus the bare tag "Vale answered". It delivers no content of its own.
- **Second reading, and it is a strong one:** read at paragraph level this is close to **(a)**. The preceding sentence has already told the reader that continued use was approved *for new jobs* and that the backlog was *postponed until the team had reviewed its status definitions*; the subheading above it has already told the reader that *Vale would allow an extra week for the status names*. Vale's two sentences then say, in her own words, continued use for new jobs and another week on the status names before the backlog. A reader arriving at the quotation has been given its content twice already. **I chose (b)** because the clause standing immediately before the quotation carries nothing but the occasion, and because the quotation still adds two things the bridge does not: the shift from the manager's recorded decision to Vale's own conditional appraisal ("I would use it again"), which is the customer's verdict the genre needs in the customer's voice, and the specific quantity "another week". But the redundancy is real and is the clearest editorial defect in the piece: the subheading in particular should not have pre-spent the quotation's own figure.
- No quotation stands with no bridge.

**Counts:** class (a) 0, class (b) 2, class (c) 0.

**Interviewer's mouth:** no. Both indirect questions render questions the material itself carries — "What did the team have to settle before the schedule was useful?" becomes "what the team had to settle before the schedule was useful", and "Would you make the same choice again, and what would you change?" becomes "whether the choice would be made again, and what would change". No question, remark or reaction is put in the interviewer's mouth that the material does not supply, and no interviewer utterance is placed inside quotation marks at all.

**Unsupported bridge assertions:** none. "Benchline's case writer", "workshop manager Priya Vale" and "by email" are all in the material ("an email interview on 2 March 2026 between Benchline's case writer and workshop manager Priya Vale"). Neither bridge asserts a scene, a manner of speaking, an expression or a reaction — which matters here, because the material explicitly records that none exists. No "Vale explains", "she recalls with a smile", "sitting in the workshop" or similar appears anywhere.
