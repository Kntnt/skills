## The quotation guidance that governs quoted speech in this draft

> Read this before putting quotation marks around anything somebody said. It applies to interview transcripts, recorded remarks, meeting notes, and any other material where the words are a person's rather than a document's.
>
> Speech is not written prose, and a transcript quoted raw reads as carelessness towards the speaker rather than fidelity to them. So a spoken quotation may be repaired — cautiously, within a boundary that is easier to state than to feel your way to.
>
> When translating permitted quotations, preserve meaning, stance, certainty and distinctive voice in idiomatic target-language speech, not source-language syntax. The restrictions below protect that substance; they do not require word-for-word translation. Make an implicit referent explicit only when the supplied context settles it. Where the meaning remains ambiguous, paraphrase what is established rather than inventing a fluent interpretation.
>
> **What may be repaired.** The syntax of speech: a sentence abandoned halfway and restarted, a subject left behind by its verb, a clause that never closes. Fillers — *you know*, *sort of*, *liksom*, *alltså* — where they carry nothing. Repetition that is the speaker searching for a word rather than insisting on one. False starts, and the small disfluencies a person does not hear themselves make. That is the whole list. Everything on it is noise the speaker did not intend to produce, and removing it leaves what they meant to say.
>
> **What may not.** *Meaning.* Not narrowed, not widened, not sharpened. A speaker who said *most of them* has not said *all of them*, and one who said *we looked at it* has not said *we rejected it*. *Stance.* Approval stays approval, reluctance stays reluctance, and a speaker who was careful not to blame anybody is not made to blame somebody by a tidier sentence. *Certainty.* *I think*, *probably*, *as far as I know*, and *we are not sure yet* are the claim, not padding on it. Removing a hedge manufactures a confidence the speaker did not have; adding one takes away a confidence they did. *Distinctive wording.* Where the speaker reached for an unusual word, an image, a piece of slang, or a phrasing that is theirs, it stays. Smoothing it into the register of the surrounding article is the most tempting of these failures and the one a reader can least detect. *A self-correction.* Where a speaker gives a number or a fact and then corrects it, the correction is what they said. *Anything additive.* Add no new meaning: no fact, stance, certainty, name, figure or connection that the speaker did not supply. A permitted repair or translation may change words only to render that same meaning.
>
> **When to paraphrase instead.** Where fidelity is uncertain, paraphrase. The cases that should turn into paraphrase: material that is a summary of what somebody said rather than their words; a transcript too broken to repair inside the boundary above; several remarks that would have to be welded together to make one sentence; and any moment where you find yourself choosing between two readings of what the speaker meant. Signal what the material actually is.

## The composition guidance for the target language, en_GB

> British general-interest prose is understated. It states a strong claim plainly and lets the claim carry the emphasis, where American register would reach for an intensifier. Irony and litotes are available and are not read as evasion.
>
> Collective nouns take plural agreement when the members are acting and singular when the body is acting as one: *the team are arguing about it*, *the committee has published its report*. Both are correct; what matters is that the choice is consistent within a passage.
>
> The present perfect covers a recent past that still bears on now: *I've just seen the figures*, *have you eaten?* The simple past in those positions reads as American.
>
> *Have got* is ordinary British usage for possession and obligation — *I've got the file*, *you've got to see this* — and is not an informality to be edited out of general prose.
>
> Prepositions differ in a small, fixed set of places: *at the weekend*, *in hospital*, *different from* or *different to*, *write to somebody*, *meet somebody* rather than *meet with*. None of these is a preference; each is the British form.
>
> Spelling is *-ise* or *-ize* by house style — both are correct British English, *-ise* being the majority convention and *-ize* the Oxford one — but a document uses one throughout. The *-yse* group is invariant: *analyse*, *paralyse*, *catalyse*, in Oxford spelling as in any other.
>
> Quotation marks are single as the outer mark in most British house styles, with double inside; some publications invert this. Follow the document's own choice and make it consistent.
