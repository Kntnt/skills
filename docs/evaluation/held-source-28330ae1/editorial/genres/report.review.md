# Report — review guidance

Diagnostics for the requirements the report genre's base half states, for a Skill reviewing a draft written against them. Nothing here is a requirement: every target a draft has to meet is in the base half, where the Skill that wrote the draft could see it, and a finding raised here is raised against a rule stated there. The examples are English and are patterns — apply what one does, in the language of the draft, rather than looking for its words.

## The shape

**Test.** Read the draft for its parts alone, ignoring how well each is written: an opening beneath the title, a summary above the body where the length calls for one, and the body under those. Then read the opening by itself and ask what the report covers, what it was written to do, and what period it speaks to. Anything you cannot answer from it, and any part standing somewhere else in that sequence, is a finding.

**Failure.** A draft that begins with its first body section, the scope arriving three sections in and the period never; an opening that has become the first body section, so that the reader is into the material before the report has said what it covers; a long report with no summary above it, so a reader who will read one section has no way to take in the rest; a summary in a report short enough to read in one go; a summary at the end, which is a recap of what the reader has just read rather than the part this shape places.

**Edge.** The parts are what the reader expects to find and not sections to be labelled: an opening owes no heading announcing itself, and a report below the threshold whose reader asked for a summary carries one. A report that falls short of the threshold because the material carries no more is at the length it should be, and is not padded up to earn a summary.

**Minimum safe correction.** Where the opening is missing, write it from the material's own statement of scope, purpose and period and from nothing else. Where a summary is missing, assemble it from what the report already says; a summary carrying a claim the body does not is a second report to keep true. Where one stands in a report too short for it, remove it, first checking that nothing it holds is held only there.

## The question

**Test.** Read the first paragraph alone and write down what the report was asked. If that cannot be done without reading further, the question is not stated where the reader meets it.

**Failure.** An opening that describes the arrangement under review, the history of how it came about, or the method the report used, with the question implied somewhere behind it. Also a question restated in the writer's vocabulary rather than the reader's: *an assessment of monitoring redundancy* for *whether we should keep paying for the second service*.

**Edge.** A report may take up a question the reader asked imprecisely and say so, naming what it is answering and what it is leaving aside. That is stating the question, not drifting from it.

**Minimum safe correction.** Lift the question out of wherever the draft states it and put it first, in the material's own words. Where the draft never states it, take it from the request and add one sentence; do not rewrite the report around a question you have inferred.

## The answer first

**Test.** Cover everything after the first two paragraphs. What is left should tell the reader what to do or what is the case. Then read it beside the question the report states: what stands at the top has to answer that question, and not the narrower one a section took up.

**Failure.** A report that reasons through the evidence and reaches its recommendation in the final section; a first paragraph that says the question is difficult, that the evidence is mixed, or what the report will do, rather than what it found. Also an opening carrying what one section settled — the cost comparison's result, the twelve-month alert history's finding — where the report's own answer belongs, so that the reader is handed a part of the case as though it were the verdict.

**Edge.** Where the material genuinely settles nothing, the answer at the top is that it settles nothing, stated as plainly as any other answer, together with what it would take to settle it. That is an answer, not a missing one. A section that reaches its own result at its own end is not a finding here either: what this rule fixes is where the report's answer stands, and a draft that gives it at the top meets the rule however the sections below arrange their own work.

**Minimum safe correction.** Move the existing finding to the top unchanged and delete whatever it replaced there. Do not strengthen it in the move: a recommendation stated more firmly at the top than it was at the bottom is a new claim. Where the top carries a section's result instead, the result goes back to the section that earned it and the report's answer takes its place; where the draft states no such answer anywhere, that is a finding about what the report concluded rather than a sentence to compose for it.

## Figures

**Test.** For each number in the draft, ask what it counts, over what period, and where it came from. A number failing any of the three is a finding.

**Failure.** *Alerts have fallen sharply* with no count and no period; a monthly cost given once and then used as if annual; a figure the material calls an estimate presented as a measurement; a percentage whose denominator is nowhere in the text.

**Edge.** A figure whose basis was given in full a paragraph earlier need not repeat it. Repetition of a basis is not the requirement; availability of it is.

**Minimum safe correction.** Restore the missing period, unit, or source from the material. Where the material does not carry it, remove the number rather than approximating it, and say in the text that the figure was not available.

## Density, tables and charts

**Test.** Put each passage that establishes something beside the material behind it. Every figure the material gives that the draft turned into a description of itself is a finding. Then take each table and chart and ask which sentence introduces it and which says what the report takes from it.

**Failure.** *The cost came down over the year* where the material holds both costs and the months; a comparison of four services across three measures written out as four paragraphs; a chart dropped between two paragraphs that neither mentions; a caption naming the source instead of what is shown; a table the prose never draws anything from, left for the reader to interpret.

**Edge.** Density is what the material affords and never a target to reach: a number the material does not carry is not supplied to raise it, and three numbers a sentence carries comfortably are not made into a table. A caption saying what is shown and prose saying what the report takes from it are doing two jobs, and neither is a finding for failing to repeat the other.

**Minimum safe correction.** Put the material's figures back where the description stands. Where a table or a chart stands unintroduced, add the sentence saying what it shows and what the report takes from it, and leave the table as it is. Where a table holds what one sentence would carry, move it into the sentence.

## The objection

**Test.** Find the counter-argument in the material and find it in the draft. Then check whether the draft's version is one its holder would recognise.

**Failure.** The objection omitted; the objection stated and then answered by an assertion the material does not support; the objection stated in a form easy to dismiss — *the on-call lead prefers having two dashboards* for *the two services fail independently, and a monitoring outage during an incident is the case the second exists for*.

**Edge.** An objection the material leaves unanswered stays unanswered in the report, said so plainly. Reporting that the material does not resolve it is answering it honestly.

**Minimum safe correction.** Replace the draft's paraphrase with the objection as the material states it, and let the existing response stand or say that the material does not resolve it. Do not construct evidence for or against an objection to close it.

## Limits

**Test.** List what the material says it does not know. Then find each item in the draft.

**Failure.** A survey caveat present in the source and absent from the report; a conclusion whose conditions are stated nowhere; a report whose limits section says only that further work is recommended.

**Edge.** Limits belong wherever the reader meets the claim they qualify, which is not necessarily a section of their own. A short report may carry them in the sentences they attach to.

**Minimum safe correction.** Restore the dropped caveat in the material's own terms, beside the claim it qualifies. Dropping a caveat asserts a confidence the material refuses, so a restored caveat is never traded for a softened claim elsewhere.

## The recommendation

**Test.** For each recommendation, ask what is to be done, by whom, by when, and at what cost or risk. Then ask which of those the material actually supports.

**Failure.** *We should consider consolidating our monitoring* — no action, no owner, no date; or a recommendation carrying an owner and a deadline that appear nowhere in the material.

**Edge.** A report that recommends nothing because the material supports nothing is complete when it says what would have to be known first. Absence of a recommendation is a finding only where the material supports one.

**Minimum safe correction.** Add only the elements the material carries, and say plainly which of the others are not established. Never supply an owner, a date, or a cost that the material does not give.

## Sections

**Test.** Read the headings alone and ask whether a reader returning for one figure would know where to go, then read them in sequence and ask what the report found. Then look at the levels: a thematic area above, the section settling part of it below, and nothing beneath those two.

**Failure.** *Background*, *Analysis*, *Discussion*, *Conclusion* over a report whose sections hold a twelve-month alert history, a cost comparison, an access-log fact, and a recommendation. Numbered headings, which locate a reader in a scheme rather than in the report. A heading that withholds — *What the access logs showed* over a section that says what they showed. A contents list naming eight areas and not one thing the report established, where every one of those sections reached something.

**Edge.** A report short enough to read at one sitting needs no headings at all, and adding them is not an improvement. A section that reached no finding — the material, the method, what was measured — is named for what it holds, which is the floor rather than a defect.

**Minimum safe correction.** Rename the heading to the finding the section states, in the section's own words; where the section states none, name it for what it holds. Do not derive a finding from a section in order to give its heading one. Unnumber a numbered heading without moving it, and leave the sections where they are: reordering a report is a larger change than the finding asks for.

## Reading a section alone

**Test.** Take each section in turn, read it without the ones above it, and mark every sentence you could not have followed: a pronoun with nothing to attach to, a term used as though it had been introduced, a figure referred to and not given, a comparison to a baseline that stands somewhere else.

**Failure.** *This confirms what the previous section established*; *the higher of the two figures*, where both are three sections up; a section arguing from a definition the report gave once at the top and never again; a conclusion resting on a count the reader would have to go and find.

**Edge.** A section may point at another by name for what it does not repeat, and a reader can follow that. What breaks the rule is resting on the other section silently. Availability of a figure's basis within the section that carries it is the figures rule and not this one; what this one asks is that a reader who read no other section is not left short.

**Minimum safe correction.** Name the referent where the section needs it, taking the term or the figure from the section that holds it. Where a section cannot stand without an argument made elsewhere, raise that as a finding rather than copying the argument in: the same paragraph in two places is two paragraphs to keep true.

## Register

**Test.** Read the draft for its sentence boundaries alone, ignoring what the sentences say. Every fragment, every one-word sentence, and every sentence opening on a conjunction is a finding.

**Failure.** *Which is the whole problem.* *Twice.* *And that is what the logs show.* A verbless phrase standing as its own paragraph for emphasis.

**Edge.** A heading, a caption, a table cell, and a list item are not sentences and are not read for this. A fragment inside quoted material is what somebody said and stays as it is.

**Minimum safe correction.** Rejoin the fragment to the sentence it was cut from, or give it the verb it lacks. Where a sentence opens on a conjunction, drop the conjunction if what precedes it already carries the relation, and otherwise fold the sentence into its predecessor. Change nothing else: a fragment repaired into a claim the draft did not make is a new claim.
