# Writing brief: Why the evening repair sessions run over

## 1. What is the assignment?

- **Working title:** Why the evening repair sessions run over
- **Topic:** the evening repair sessions
- **Genre:** report
- **Channel:** a PDF for the board of the Alder Tool Library
- **Language:** en_GB is the source language. A Swedish version will be translated from it afterwards by the library's volunteer translator.
- **Length:** provisionally 800 to 1,200 words
- **Search phrase:** none, deliberately, since the report goes to the board only.

## 2. Which brand is the sender?

The Alder Tool Library. Its platform is in a document not to hand (see the open questions under question 12).

- **Promise:** [MISSING: What does the Alder Tool Library promise its audience and want to be known for?]
- **Image:** [MISSING: How does the board perceive the library and its volunteer coordinators today, and is there trust to build on or distrust to handle?]
- **Mandate:** the volunteer coordinators may not recommend spending, since the budget is the treasurer's mandate.
- **Tone of voice:** plain and factual.
- **Tone in this text:** sober and neutral. The brand's tone of voice and the report's conventions pull the same way.

## 3. What does the sender want to convey with this text?

The volunteer coordinators have the sessions under control and can fix the overruns themselves.

## 4. Who is the reader?

The seven board members, who asked for the report. They know the sessions exist but not how they run. They care about the volunteers' wellbeing and about the terms on which the library rents its premises. They read what they asked for before the November board meeting, where the session schedule is decided; that meeting is what makes the report pressing now.

## 5. What is the text's angle?

What the September sign-in sheets show about where the overrun time goes.

## 6. What is the hook?

The sessions are booked for two hours but ended late on every evening in September, and nobody has known why.

## 7. How should the text be structured?

The report's own form, chosen as such. For the report genre that form is PAC, so the sketch uses PAC's one-sentence form. The report genre advises against ABT: a report told as a complication and its consequence hands a story to readers who came for a result they can act on and argue with.

**The whole text in one sentence:** [SUGGESTED: The evening repair sessions are booked for two hours but ran over on every evening in September without anyone knowing why, and the September sign-in sheets show that most of the overrun comes from repairs started in the last half hour, which means that a cut-off for new repairs at 20:30 would end the sessions on time and the coordinators can fix the overruns themselves.]

**Sketch** (about 1,000 words; below 5,000 words a report carries no separate summary):

1. **Opening (about 150 words).** What the report covers (the evening repair sessions), why it was written (the board's question ahead of the November meeting, where the schedule is decided) and the period it speaks to (September). The question in the board's terms: why do sessions booked for two hours run late every evening? The answer follows before the evidence: where the time goes, that the coordinators can fix it themselves, and the proposal for a trial cut-off.
2. **The overrun in figures (about 150 words).** How many evenings, how late each ended and the total overrun, from the sign-in sheets, probably as a table. Every figure says what it counts and where it comes from.
3. **Where the overrun time goes (about 350 words, the core of the analysis).** The overrun time broken down by where the sheets place it, in one or more subsections whose headings state the finding. What the sheets record is kept apart from what the report makes of it.
4. **The strongest objection (about 100 words).** The objection the board is most likely to raise, put at its strongest, and what the sheets do and do not do to it. It may touch the premises only through the 21:30 limit.
5. **What the sheets cannot settle (about 100 words).** One month's data, and only what the sign-in sheets record; the conditions under which the answer would differ.
6. **The proposal and the ending (about 150 words).** A proposal for a board decision: a trial cut-off for new repairs at 20:30 from the first session in December, measured by the share of December sessions that end on time. No spending is recommended, since the budget is the treasurer's mandate. The ending ties back to the hook: the reason for the overruns is now known, and the board has what it needs for the November decision.

## 8. What should be left out?

- No recommendation to hire staff or spend money.
- No names of individual volunteers.
- Nothing on the rental terms beyond noting that the premises must be empty by 21:30.

## 9. What should the reader have understood afterwards?

Most of the overrun comes from repairs started in the last half hour, so a cut-off for new repairs at 20:30 would end the sessions on time.

## 10. What should the reader feel, think or do afterwards?

- **Main effect:** the board approves a trial cut-off for new repairs from the first session in December.
- **The way there:** the board first needs to see that the overrun has one main cause.
- **Call to action:** direct, a proposal for a board decision.
- **Measure:** the share of December sessions that end on time.

## 11. What makes the text RIV for the reader, and does the brief hold together?

- **Relevant:** the board asked for the report and decides the session schedule at the November meeting. The overruns touch what the board cares about: the volunteers' wellbeing, and premises that must be empty by 21:30. Found in the opening (why now) and in the figures in step 2.
- **Interesting:** the hook: sessions booked for two hours ended late on every evening in September, and nobody has known why. Stated as the question in the opening.
- **Valuable:** the conclusion: the overrun has one main cause, repairs started in the last half hour, and a cut-off at 20:30 would end the sessions on time without any spending. Carried by the analysis in step 3 and the proposal in step 6.

**Chain check:**

- *Does the reader follow from the sender and the channel?* Yes: the board members asked for this PDF report.
- *Does the angle lie close to the reader, and does the hook carry the angle?* Yes: what the sign-in sheets show about where the time goes answers the hook's "nobody has known why".
- *Does the structure resolve the hook's tension, and does it fit the length?* Yes: step 3 resolves the why and step 6 ends on the proposal; about 1,000 words fits 800 to 1,200.
- *Is the conclusion what the structure actually lands in, does it carry the message, and does it lie within the brand's mandate?* Partly: the structure lands in the cut-off, and a cut-off involves no spending, so it lies within the mandate. But the message says the coordinators can fix the overruns themselves, while the main effect needs the board to approve a trial. Unresolved: can the coordinators introduce the cut-off on their own, or does "themselves" mean they need nothing beyond the board's approval of the trial?
- *Does what the reader understands lead to the effect, and does that effect serve the brand's goals?* Partly: one main cause leads naturally to approving a trial, but whether that serves the brand's goals cannot be fully checked while the brand's promise is missing.
- *Does anything in the structure conflict with what should be left out?* No conflict: nothing is spent or hired, no volunteers are named, and the premises appear only through the 21:30 limit.
