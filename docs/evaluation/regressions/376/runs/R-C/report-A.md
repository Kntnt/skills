## 1. Claim accounting

Each row records a draft passage beside the supplied passage it rests on, quoted in the source's own words, with what differs between them.

### 1.1 "Nordvik Logistik AB does not pretend to be neutral about this."

- **Draft:** "Nordvik Logistik AB does not pretend to be neutral about this."
- **Source (Commissioner's own starting points):** "Nordvik Logistik AB, which commissioned this article, states its own position as follows, and asks that it be carried as the company's view: ... 'we are not neutral about it.'"
- **Thing named:** the company's own declared stance. Same entity, same stance.
- **Subject:** Nordvik Logistik AB in both. Third person singular; company as institutional actor, grammatically neuter in the draft ("its view") and first person plural in the source ("we"). The shift from the source's first-person plural to the draft's third-person singular is a change of speaking position only, not of referent or of person/number information about any human being: no natural-gender feature is asserted in either text, and none is introduced. The draft's "its" is a grammatical possessive on a corporate name, not a natural-gender assertion.
- **Scope / time / modality:** unrestricted present in both. The draft's "does not pretend to be" and the source's "are not" both assert the stance flatly rather than hedging it.
- **Whose assertion:** the company's, in both, and the draft makes that visible by naming the company as grammatical subject of the disclaimer.
- **Verdict:** supported. The brief expressly asks that this be carried as the company's view, and the draft carries it as such. Nothing external is needed for a party's own declared non-neutrality to be reported as that party's.

## 2. Findings

Four findings. Each is a defect to repair before delivery.

### 2.1 "Residents agree." — an unsupported claim about an actual population

**Draft:** "Residents agree."

**Missing support:** no supplied passage reports any view held by residents. Material B's "less disturbance for residents at night" is the company's own asserted link between its cycles' low noise and night-time disturbance; it attributes no opinion to residents and says nothing about vans.

**Why it is not carried by the preceding standpoint:** the sentence does not continue the company's voice. It is a new sentence in the draft's own voice, and its function in context is to corroborate the company's stance from outside — an ordinary reader takes "Residents agree" as the writer reporting that a real group shares the view just quoted. That the brief establishes that the company asserts something establishes nothing about whether residents share it. A claim about what an actual population thinks is a factual claim whoever supplied the surrounding standpoint.

**Test, draft holds and source fails:** residents of Nordvik are canvassed and are indifferent to delivery vans, caring only about kerbside parking availability. Nothing supplied excludes this; Material B's sentence about night-time disturbance remains true as the company's own linkage, and every other supplied statement is untouched. The draft's sentence is then false while the material stands.

**Test, source holds and draft fails:** with the material exactly as supplied and no canvass ever run, the material asserts nothing about residents' agreement — yet the draft asserts it. The draft outruns the material in this direction too.

**No supplied statement excludes either case.** The case stands; this is a finding.

**Smallest supported repair:** delete the sentence. If a bridge is wanted in that position, the supported alternative is the company's own linkage, attributed: "The company links the cycles' low noise to a calmer street environment and to less disturbance for residents at night." That keeps the company as the party standing behind it and adds no claim about what residents think.

### 2.2 "Delivery vans are unpopular wherever they park." — unattributed, and wider than the standpoint it echoes

**Draft:** "Delivery vans are unpopular wherever they park."

**Missing support:** nothing supplied concerns the popularity of delivery vans anywhere. The sentence echoes the company's standpoint from §1.2 but strips the attribution the brief requires and widens the scope from "in a city centre" to "wherever they park".

**Two separate defects, both in one sentence.**

*Attribution.* In §1.2 the same sentiment is safe because "the company says" marks it. Here, following "Residents agree", the tag is gone and the reader takes the generalisation as the draft's own report of fact about vans and the people near them. A rhetorical generalisation is supported as the commissioning party's standpoint only "wherever the brief carries it" — as theirs. Detached from that marking, it is an unsupported assertion about an actual population.

*Scope.* Even restored to the company's voice, "wherever they park" is not what the brief supplies. The brief's sentence is confined to "a city centre".

**Test, draft holds and source fails:** the draft's sentence, taken at face value, commits to vans being unpopular in an out-of-town industrial estate, a motorway service area and a depot yard. Nothing in the material speaks to any of these, and a case in which vans parked at a freight depot are entirely unremarked is fully compatible with every supplied statement — including the company's own sentence, which is about city centres. The draft then asserts what the material does not.

**Test, source holds and draft fails:** the brief's statement, carried as the company's view about city centres, holds exactly as the brief gives it while the draft's unrestricted, unattributed version fails at the first parked van nobody minds.

**No supplied statement excludes either case.** The case stands; this is a finding.

**Smallest supported repair:** fold the sentence back into the attributed standpoint and restore the supplied scope, or delete it as redundant with §1.2. If it is kept: "In the company's view, vans in a city centre are ugly and intrusive." Note that this makes it a near-repetition of the opening paragraph, which is why deletion is the smaller repair of the two.

### 2.3 "through improved routing" — a dropped conjunct narrows the programme's stated means

**Draft:** "a 25 % reduction in kerbside dwell time through improved routing".

**Source (Material A):** "a 25 % reduction in kerbside dwell time through improved routing and loading".

**What changed:** the objective's second named means, loading, is dropped. "Improved routing and loading" is a conjoined modifier governing the whole reduction; both conjuncts qualify the same target, and carrying one across while dropping the other changes what the programme says the reduction is to come from.

**Test, draft holds and source fails:** the programme achieves its 25 % reduction with 20 points coming from redesigned ground-level loading and 5 points from routing. The material's objective is met as published. The draft's sentence — which tells the reader the target is set on routing — misdescribes the objective's stated basis, and a reader who went on to attribute the result to routing would be relying on the draft. Nothing supplied excludes this split; Material C's "flat, ground-level loading" makes loading a live mechanism in this pilot rather than a hypothetical one.

**Test, source holds and draft fails:** conversely, a reader asking "what does the programme say will produce the reduction?" gets a complete answer from the material and an incomplete one from the draft. The draft's narrower sentence is not entailed by the wider objective in the way a summary of it must be, because the reader cannot recover the dropped conjunct.

**No supplied statement excludes either case** — nothing in the material singles routing out or subordinates loading. The case stands; this is a finding.

**Smallest supported repair:** restore the conjunct — "through improved routing and loading". One word plus a conjunction. Keep "put the target at", which correctly preserves the objective as a target rather than an outcome, and keep the figure and the measured quantity as they are.

### 2.4 "40 % more than the vans managed on the same streets" — a comparison the operator expressly declined to make

**Draft:** "412 deliveries were made by cycle — 40 % more than the vans managed on the same streets."

**Source (Material D):** "Over the pilot's first eight weeks, 412 deliveries were made by cycle. The operator states that it makes no claim about how this compares with van deliveries over the same period, because the routes were not matched."

**Missing support, on three counts.**

*The figure.* No van delivery count appears anywhere in the material, so no ratio can be computed from it. "40 %" has no supplied basis of any kind.

*The comparison itself.* The material does not merely omit the comparison — it records the operator declining to make it, and the draft asserts precisely what the operator declined to assert. The state of knowledge supplied is: the cycle count is known, the comparison is unclaimed. What is unclaimed is not thereby established in either direction, and the draft converts an explicit non-claim into a specific quantified claim.

*The stated reason, and "on the same streets".* The operator gives its reason: "the routes were not matched." The draft's "on the same streets" asserts the very matching the material says did not hold. This is the clause that most directly contradicts a supplied statement, rather than merely outrunning it.

**Test, draft holds and source fails:** none is needed in this direction to establish the finding, but note that even a case in which cycles did outperform vans by 40 % would leave the draft unsupported, because the comparison would still rest on routes the material says were not matched.

**Test, source holds and draft fails:** the cycle routes covered a compact central district while the vans ran a dispersed suburban round over the same eight weeks. Every supplied statement holds — 412 cycle deliveries, the operator's refusal to compare, the unmatched routes — and the draft's "40 % more than the vans managed on the same streets" is false twice over: the streets were not the same, and no ratio was established. Nothing supplied excludes this case; Material D's stated reason positively invites it.

**The case stands, and this finding is the least defensible of the four**, because the material anticipates and refuses the exact claim the draft makes.

**Smallest supported repair:** delete the comparison, leaving the supported sentence and its supplied qualification: "Over the pilot's first eight weeks, 412 deliveries were made by cycle. The operator makes no claim about how this compares with van deliveries over the same period, because the routes were not matched." Strictly, deleting the em-dash clause alone is the minimum repair; carrying the operator's qualification across is recommended alongside it, because the bare count in a persuasive article invites the reader to supply the comparison the operator refused. Do not substitute a hedged form — "suggests that cycles outperformed vans" still adds the comparison and its attribution, and is equally unsupported.

## 3. Checks run that produced no finding

- **Causal language.** The draft contains no causal verb and no causal hedge. "Through improved routing" reports the programme's own stated means for its own target, not an established cause of an observed outcome, and "put the target at" keeps it as an objective. Subject to §2.3, no causal overreach is present. In particular the draft nowhere claims that the pilot shortened dwell time.
- **Evaluative characterisation.** The count of 412 is given without being called high, low or modest. The only evaluative comparison in the draft is the "40 %" clause, reported at §2.4 as a source-support finding rather than as an evaluative one.
- **Chronology and scope.** "Over the pilot's first eight weeks" is carried verbatim. The programme's March-to-November run is not used and is nowhere contradicted.
- **Invented apparatus.** No study, expert, quotation, scene or personal experience is invented to complete a form. No unnamed study or unnamed expert is invoked. The planned-but-unwritten evaluation report is nowhere treated as existing or as having found anything.
- **Pronoun features.** Every pronoun in the draft — "its" (twice, of the company), "they" (of delivery vans), "it" (of the operator, in the repair text only) — has a referent settled by the supplied context, and none asserts a natural-gender feature. The draft introduces no person, number or natural-gender information beyond what the material conveys. The company's shift from source first-person plural to draft third-person singular is a speaking-position change only; no purely grammatical gender is read as a natural-gender assertion anywhere.
- **Length.** The draft is well within any conceivable limit and no length pressure is visible; nothing in the draft reads as padding added to reach a length, and no qualification appears to have been cut to meet one. The dropped conjunct at §2.3 and the qualification lost at §2.4 are not attributable to a length constraint.
- **Translation.** Vacuous. Source and draft are both English; no term crosses a language boundary and no quotation is translated. No translation findings exist, and none of the four findings above is a translation finding — all four are source-support findings.
