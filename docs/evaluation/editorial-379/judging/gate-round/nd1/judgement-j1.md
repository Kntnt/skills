# Judgement J1 — nd1-narrowed-denial

Fixture: `docs/evaluation/editorial-379/fixtures/nd1-narrowed-denial.md`
Reports judged: A, B, C, D, E, F (six, under neutral names; nothing is known or assumed about how any was produced).

Throughout, the operative test for a source-support defect is the one the brief sets: read in its full context, does the draft passage assert something the material does not carry, such that the passage could be false while every supplied statement holds? Where the answer is no, the passage is supported however much an editor might prefer other words.

---

## 1. My own reading of the focus passage

**Draft:** "The questionnaire does not ask about participants' mental health."
**Material:** "Enkäten frågar inte om deltagarnas hälsa." Plus the questionnaire's content: "frågade vad de gjorde på gården, hur de tog sig dit och om de ville att gården skulle vara öppen fler kvällar."

The denials differ in scope: the material denies health questions of any kind, the draft denies only mental-health questions. The narrowing runs in the safe direction. A questionnaire that asks nothing about health asks nothing about mental health, so the draft's sentence is entailed by the supplied statement: there is no state of affairs compatible with the material in which the draft's sentence is false. It asserts strictly less than the material carries, and nothing the material does not carry.

The only real case against it is the implicature one — that naming the narrower category invites a reader to suppose health was covered in some other form. Read in context, that inference does not survive. The paragraph is about one sentence in the staff recommendation, "motverkar psykisk ohälsa bland unga i Nyhamn", and a denial pitched at the recommendation's own topic is the relevant denial, not a concession about other topics. The draft then lists the questionnaire's three questions in full — "what they did at the center, how they got there, and whether they wanted it open more evenings" — so no reader finishes the piece believing a health question of any kind was asked. Nothing in the draft becomes false, no figure is misreported, and no claim in the argument depends on the difference.

What is left is a rhetorical preference: the material supports the wider denial word for word, and the wider denial is the stronger sentence for this argument. Swapping one word would improve the piece. That is an author's choice about force, not a repair the material compels before delivery.

**Conclusion: a finding against the focus passage is not correct.** The strongest contrary argument — made most fully by D and E — is that a narrowed denial in this position "conveys" a contrast the file does not support. I reject it because the contrast is defeated within the draft itself, by the enumeration of the three questions, and because a writer who under-claims against their own interest has not misstated the source.

| # | Draft passage targeted | Report's allegation | Class | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| — | — | No item is presented as a finding. | — | §4.1–§4.4 record four divergences examined and set aside; §6 records "None". |

C is right about the focus passage and about "does not know", and misses the one item I hold supported: at §4.1 it sets aside "decides on March 3" on the ground that the sentence "says that the question is before the committee at that meeting, which is exactly what the material supplies". That reading underweights the verb: "decides on March 3 whether X" asserts when the deciding happens, and the material's own boundary calls the outcome a *möjligt* beslut. Missing a finding is not itself a finding, so it does not enter the table, but it is the one place C differs from my reading of the fixture.

Focus passage: **cleared.** Decisive sentence: "Widening the denial to 'participants' health' would track the source wording more closely, but that is a preference about the draft, not a defect in it. Not a finding."
