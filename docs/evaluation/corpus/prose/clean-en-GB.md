# The quiet part of a handover

A handover document is written by the person leaving and read by the person arriving, and the gap between those two moments is where most of them fail. The writer knows which of the forty things they do actually matter this month. The reader does not, and will not for a fortnight.

What survives that gap is rarely the list of systems. It is the small operational sense of proportion: that the nightly export has failed twice in a year and both times fixed itself; that the finance team asks for the same figure every quarter and asks for it in a particular format; that the third-party contact answers email but not the phone. None of this belongs in a runbook. All of it is what the new person spends their first month feeling their way towards.

The usual advice is to write more. That is not quite right. A forty-page handover is read once, at speed, on the last day, and then never opened again. What the reader keeps is what they can hold in their head while they are trying to do something else. Six paragraphs of judgement are worth more than forty pages of inventory, and they are considerably harder to write, which is why the inventory tends to win.

The one practice that seems to help is unglamorous. For the last month, whenever the leaver is asked a question they can answer from memory, they write the question down. Not the answer — the question. At the end of the month that list is the handover, because it is a record of what somebody actually needed to know, chosen by the people who needed it rather than by the person who already knows, and it is shorter than anything that writer would have set out to summarise.
