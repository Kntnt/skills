# Fenwick says staff could identify instruments awaiting parts

For new jobs, Fenwick Instrument Repairs approved continued use but postponed adding its backlog. This fictional case shows workshop managers the setup choices and the limits of the evidence.

By Thomas Barregren

Fenwick Instrument Repairs services brass and woodwind instruments. Jobs awaiting parts and those ready for work appeared on separate paper sheets before the team began a six-week trial of a shared bench schedule in January 2026.

## The team shapes the setup

The team chose Benchline after checking it could distinguish the two statuses. The workshop defined them and decided who would update them; Benchline configured those choices and trained four staff in one online session. Customer collection arrangements continued to be discussed by telephone.

In an email interview on 2 March 2026, manager Priya Vale explained what the team had to settle: ‘We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.’

## The summary sets limits on the findings

The trial summary, dated 23 February 2026, records 38 jobs entered in the schedule. It says staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts.

The summary does not measure repair duration, revenue or customer satisfaction. The manager approved continued use for new jobs but postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions.

## Vale reflects on the choice

In the same email interview, Vale said: ‘I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.’
