======== base.md
# The base contract

Write with the clarity and factual precision of a well-edited newspaper or quality magazine. Genre, audience, and purpose override that register: a letter, a speech and an advertisement keep their own character. The selected genre governs craft and form; the resolved language governs idiom, syntax and typography. Examples here illustrate meanings, not English phrases to translate.

## Reader and progression

Give the intended reader what they need to understand, judge or do the thing the text is for. Keep a clear focus, introduce unfamiliar concepts before the reasoning needs them, and order material by that reader's needs rather than the writer's discovery process. Relevant technical detail belongs; unexplained jargon and unrelated background do not.

Make the relations between passages real. A transition may explain a connection, but cannot invent one. Keep paragraphs coherent and give supporting material a clear place beside what it supports. The body and its sections remain intelligible without treating a title, standfirst or subheading as their opening sentence.

Open on something specific to the subject. End with what the job calls for: an earned understanding, conclusion, recommendation or next step. Remove introductions and conclusions that add only an announcement or a generic flourish.

## Expression

Use concrete words, useful verbs and a natural, varied cadence. Keep terminology and reference clear; introduce an unfamiliar abbreviation before using it alone. Choose ordinary words where they suffice and established specialist terms where they give this reader precision.

Let tone and rhetorical devices serve the genre and the meaning. A useful analogy, question, image, fragment or humorous turn can clarify or carry a voice; decoration and manufactured drama cannot. An imagined comparison is recognisable as a comparison, never presented as an event or someone's experience. Criticism addresses the supported issue without inventing motives or patronising the reader.

Each passage earns its place. Repetition may establish an independent entry point, explain a hard idea or give a deliberate rhetorical return; merely saying the same thing again does none of these. Keep formatting useful: headings for orientation, lists for coordinate items, tables for comparisons, emphasis for what merits it. Hold voice, terminology and presentation consistent unless a visible purpose calls for a change.

## Claims

Every claim is supported by the text or its material, with exact attribution and proportionate strength. Preserve the state of knowledge: what is unknown or unclaimed is not thereby known not to have happened. Keep uncertainty where it exists and certainty where it is warranted. A fact, quotation, source, scene, personal experience or opinion attributed to somebody is never invented to complete a form. Name the actual source of an attributed claim rather than invoking unnamed studies or experts.

Circumstantial detail is a claim too: duration, manner, motive, absence and background all need support. So does an evaluative characterisation: calling a count high, low or modest needs a comparison, target, capacity or speaker assessment. Otherwise give the count with its exclusions, without that assessment.

A sequence or correlation establishes no cause. A causal verb such as *shortened* needs causal support; a causal hedge such as *suggests that it shortened* still adds that attribution. This boundary holds in the title, summary, headings and body alike. Preserve chronology, scope and qualifications wherever a claim appears.

A stated length constrains the use of material and is never a licence to add to it. When the material is insufficient, deliver the length the material supports and name what further material would close the gap. A supplied publication limit is binding; meeting it cannot justify invention or loss of a qualification that changes a claim.
======== base.review.md
# Reviewing the base contract

Diagnose failures of [the base contract](base.md) in the text in front of you. A finding identifies the passage, the requirement and what the reader loses. Count a loss as the reader who meets the passage in its place would suffer it, not as the strictest reading of a single word could construct it; that holds for every passage, the headline, the standfirst and each subheading as much as the body. A writer's own judgement that the text supports, such as calling a survey small where the text says it reached forty people, is not a claim the text does not carry. Another successful choice, a house-style preference and a mechanical error are not editorial findings; mechanics belongs to the separate final pass. Repetition of whole sentences or passages is an editorial matter, even when the duplication is verbatim; Proofread's duplicate-word correction does not own it.

Read for the intended reader's missing step: an unexplained term, a transition asserting a relation the surrounding sentences do not establish, a section whose place obscures the argument. Cover the paratext to find unresolved references in the body. A heading may carry a fact that the section under it does not repeat: a time, a price or a condition a subheading states has been stated to the reader, and is not missing from the body. What is a finding is a body that needs the heading to be understood — a pronoun, a definite form or an ellipsis that only the heading completes — repaired by naming the referent where the body first needs it rather than by saying the heading's facts again. Judge rhythm by its effect, not sentence counts; purposeful repetition, a personal rough edge and a useful digression may be doing essential work.

Compare the strength of a claim with the support visible in this text. A number contradicting its stated denominator, a causal conclusion following only a sequence, or a certainty contradicted by a nearby qualification is diagnosable. A plausible quotation or experience is not disproved by the absence of its original source. Review no unseen material, request none, and add no source-verification caveat.

Use [the anti-slop catalogue](anti-slop.md) to recognise empty performance, not to prohibit a working figure, contrast or ending. Test suspected filler by what its removal costs; preserve any fact, implication or voice it carries beside the defect. Strong supported criticism needs no generic softening.

A sentence can do its work by bounding what the text asserts: that something is not measured, not claimed, not witnessed or not general, or that what follows is an observation of a document rather than a scene from an occasion. Such a sentence is where *Preserve the state of knowledge: what is unknown or unclaimed is not thereby known not to have happened*, *Keep uncertainty where it exists and certainty where it is warranted* and *Preserve chronology, scope and qualifications wherever a claim appears* are carried, and the text alone shows which sentence it is: read it out, and the claims around it assert more than they did. That cost is what the removal test above answers, so recognise one by what it bounds and by which claim loses that bound, never by its form. A finding lies inside such a sentence or nowhere: name a limit another sentence already states in full, or a limit contradicting another passage of this text, or leave the sentence exactly as it stands. Naming the whole of it as the defect names nothing inside it.

Make the smallest correction that removes a finding. Move or clarify existing material where it supplies the missing connection. Introduce no new evidence, experience, attribution or outcome. If repair would change a claim or a quotation's meaning, or requires information absent from the text, leave the passage and report the unresolved finding. A claim removed because the finding named that claim itself remains part of the claim account, and so does every claim a correction left standing with its scope, certainty, attribution, chronology, causality or meaning moved, and every claim a correction added — one the corrected text asserts and the text before it has no counterpart for, an inference drawn from what that text already said among them where it asserts something that text did not say. A claim that has a counterpart in the text before is changed rather than added. A headline, a standfirst and a subheading carry claims like any other passage, and a changed claim is accounted for by what it now asserts rather than how far its wording moved; a correction that took a limit, a connective or a bounding clause out of the text is accounted for by what the reader no longer has, however little of the text went with it. The account is of the text delivered rather than of the text received, and nothing a reviewing Skill says about the claims as a whole contradicts a finding the same reply states.

Compare each correction with its input, including claims beside the defect, then reread the surrounding passage for broken references or transitions. Compare it difference by difference and not by its claims alone, and trace each one to a finding or to what repairing that finding required: a difference answering neither is not a correction, whatever it improves, and the passage it touched belongs back as it was. A correction is a candidate until that comparison and the review after it have run: where the review establishes a defect the correction introduced and its input did not have, the correction is not accepted and its input stands. Preserve functioning wording, voice and formatting outside the finding; concision is not permission to summarise the text away.
======== genres/general.md
# General

The default genre: the contract a text gets when nobody named a content type. It is a complete contract rather than the absence of one, and it is what a note, a memo, a letter, an explanation, or a piece of prose whose kind has no name is written against.

## What this genre asks for

**The shape follows the material and the job.** No convention is imposed here — no lede, no summary paragraph, no boilerplate, no mandatory heading, no obligatory recommendation at the end. A named genre brings its reader's expectations with it; this one brings only the reader's need, so the text takes the shape that need implies and no other.

**The kind of text is recognisable from the text.** A reader should be able to tell within a sentence or two whether they are holding an explanation, an argument, an account of what happened, a request, or a description, and the text should then behave like that thing throughout. A draft that is an argument for two paragraphs and a chronology for the next two has changed kind without telling anybody.

**The register is the baseline's, moved by the audience and the purpose.** With no genre convention to override it, what moves the register is who the text is for and what it is for. A note to a colleague, a public explanation, and a letter of complaint are all this genre, and they are not written the same way. Where the request states or implies an audience or a purpose, that is the instruction; where it does not, hold the baseline.

**Length is what the job takes.** There is no expected length here, and nothing is padded to look substantial or cut to look brisk. A text that has done its job in two hundred words is finished at two hundred words.

**A title is written when the artifact has one.** Where the requested artifact is a document with a title, the title says what the text is about in the text's own terms, rather than posing a question the text does not answer or promising more than the text delivers. Where the artifact is a passage, a message, or a fragment, no title is invented for it.

## What this genre does not do

It does not stand in for a genre that exists. Where the request names a content type the Collection installs, that genre's contract is the one to load; this one is what applies when nothing was named and nothing in the request implies a named kind.

It adds no requirement the base contract does not already hold. Everything about register, structure, sentences, words, claims, openings, endings, formatting, and consistency is stated there once, and repeating any of it here would make one rule two things to keep true.

## The technique this genre is ordinarily written with

None. This is the contract a text gets when nobody named a kind of text, and a kind nobody named brings no arc with it. What puts a technique on a text here is a selection, and this genre is not one.
======== anti-slop.md
# The anti-slop catalogue

Prose that a language model produced, or that a human wrote after reading a great deal of it, fails in a small number of recognisable ways. This is the catalogue of those ways: what each one looks like on the page, why it costs the reader something, and the smallest change that removes it. It is a diagnostic document — a Skill reading it beside the base contract meets no target the contract does not already hold, and a Skill reading it alone has the patterns and their repairs and needs nothing else.

The examples are English because this document is, and they are patterns rather than strings. Apply each by what it does in the target language, and do not look for the English words in it. A word, a phrase, a punctuation convention, or a construction that only exists in one language belongs to that language's own anti-slop guidance instead, where a Skill loads it beside this file.

## The test before a pattern is recorded

Each pattern below says what a phrase looks like, and looking like one is not being one. Make the repair in your head before you record anything: where the text then asserts more, less or something else than it did, or a distinction it drew is gone, the pattern is not present and nothing is recorded. A hedge that marks genuine doubt, a repetition that lands, a contrast the reader really would otherwise draw wrongly — each of them fails that test, and each is left exactly as it stands.

The test comes before the finding rather than after it. A pattern written down reaches whoever repairs it as a defect somebody found, and a guard that only makes the repair careful arrives too late to save the phrase.

Repair with the smallest change that removes a pattern the test admits. The writer's vocabulary, their bluntness, their humour, their admitted uncertainty, and their rhythm are what makes a text theirs; a pass that tidies all of it into even prose has replaced one machine voice with another. Leave a strong sentence alone even where it is unusual.

## The seven patterns

**False contrasts.** A negation used as a run-up to the actual claim: *This isn't a tool. It's a way of working.* *The question isn't whether, but when.* *It's not just faster — it's cheaper.* The rejected half was never in play, so the sentence spends the reader's attention establishing a position nobody held. State the second half on its own: *This changes how the team works.* A contrast stays where the text really is correcting something a reader would otherwise believe.

**Empty openings.** A first sentence that announces the subject, asserts that the subject matters, or clears the throat before the text starts: *In today's rapidly changing landscape…*, *Communication has never been more important.*, *Here's the thing.*, *What most people get wrong is…* Each could sit in front of any text at all, which is the test: an opening that would survive being moved to a different article is carrying no information. Cut it and open on the material — a fact, a scene, a tension, the question the text answers. The second paragraph is very often the real opening.

**Importance inflation.** Adjectives and formulas that assert weight the material has not shown: *a pivotal moment*, *stands as a testament to*, *a game-changer*, *underscores the significance of*, *plays a vital role in*. Also the trailing participial clause that pretends to interpret — *…, highlighting the team's commitment to quality* — which adds a judgement no fact in the sentence supports. Replace the assertion with what actually happened: *The launch is the company's first paid product.* A reader who is given the fact will decide it matters without being told to.

**Vague attribution.** A claim credited to nobody: *Studies show*, *experts agree*, *industry reports suggest*, *it is widely believed*, *many argue*. Nothing here can be checked, and nothing can be contradicted, which is precisely what makes it comfortable to write. Name who found it, who said it, and how they know; where the text has no source, cut the claim rather than dressing it. Do not invent an attribution to satisfy the pattern — an unsourced sentence is removed, never furnished with a plausible source.

**Synonym cycling.** One thing given a new name every time it is mentioned, because repeating a word felt like poor style: *the agent* reviews the draft, then *the assistant* scores it, then *the tool* suggests fixes. A reader who has been introduced to three names reasonably assumes three things. Pick the clear name and repeat it. Repetition of a key term is how a reader knows it is the same term, and the fix is usually one word restored in two places rather than a rewritten paragraph. The pattern is present only where the names denote one thing: two words a text uses for two things are not cycling however often they alternate, and a text that named two things comes back naming two things.

**Robotic rhythm.** Sentences and paragraphs built to one template: the same length, the same shape, the same three-part list, each paragraph opening with its topic sentence and closing with its summary. Stacked short fragments used for drama — *That's it. That's the whole idea.* — are the same fault at the other extreme. Read a passage aloud in your head; where the breath falls in the same place every time, the shape is doing the writing. Vary length and construction where the meaning gives you a reason to, and merge or split only the sentences whose shape is actually the problem.

**Generic conclusions.** A final paragraph that restates what the reader has just read, reopens the introduction as a rhetorical question, or reaches for an uplifting abstraction: *In conclusion*, *Ultimately, the future belongs to those who…*, *one thing is clear.* The reader was there for the whole text and does not need it summarised back. Delete the paragraph and end on the last concrete point the draft already contains; where the job genuinely calls for a conclusion, a recommendation, or a next step, write that thing plainly and stop. Do not replace a weak closing metaphor with a better one — remove it.

======== writing-brief.md
# Questions to answer before you write

The Writing Brief template: each numbered question carries the guidance for answering it. This English reference is authoritative; render its questions and guidance in the user's working language while retaining every question, its number and its order. Its genre and technique examples explain the form, not what is installed: the Library directories supply those choices, and where a genre and this guidance differ, the genre governs.

How to use the template: answer the questions in full sentences and as concretely as you can. The details under the assignment may be short. You may fill in the questions in whatever order suits you, but the order below is the one the brief is read in. It runs from what the assignment gives you, through the context and the shape of the text, to a check that everything holds together. When every answer is there, you have a brief. If you cannot answer a question, you need to find out more before you start writing. Sources and evidence, on the other hand, can be added during the work. Refer to other answers by name, for example "the message" or "the hook", not by the question's number.

## 1. What is the assignment?

Here you gather what the assignment gives you. Most of it needs no thinking up, but it governs everything that follows.

- **Working title:** a name for the text, so that everyone immediately remembers what it is about. It need not be punchy, effective or even accurate. It only needs to be short and clear.
- **Topic:** what the text is about, in a few words, for example "remote work" or "Christmas baking".
- **Genre:** for example article, case study, column, opinion piece, press release, report, teaser or web copy.
- **Channel:** for example website, blog, web magazine, newsletter, LinkedIn or newspaper.
- **Language:** for example `sv` or `en_GB`; this is the future text's language, independently of the language the brief is written in. If the text is to exist in several languages, give the source language and how the other versions are produced.
- **Length:** often governed by genre and channel. Give roughly how many words or characters the text should have. The length decides how much the structure can hold. If you do not know yet, give a provisional range and narrow it once the sketch is done.
- **Search phrase:** if the text is to be found through search, give the keyword or phrase it should be visible for. If the text is deliberately written without one, say so.

## 2. Which brand is the sender?

Name the brand the text is published under. In a group of companies it is rarely the group itself. Then describe the brand. If the brand has a platform, a tone-of-voice guide or another description, it is enough to link to it and write out only what governs this particular text, for example distrust that needs handling or a limit to the mandate. The tone of this text you always describe.

- **Promise:** what the brand promises its audience and wants to be known for.
- **Image:** how the reader perceives the brand today, and whether there is trust to build on or distrust to handle.
- **Mandate:** what the brand can speak about with credibility.
- **Tone of voice:** how the brand sounds.
- **Tone in this text:** the brand's tone of voice and the genre's conventions must both be respected. Where they pull in different directions, choose a compromise that sounds natural to both. If they pull the same way, say so.

## 3. What does the sender want to convey with this text?

Every text has a reason, and the reason can nearly always be put as a message. The brand's general message belongs to the description of the brand and often lies close to the promise. Here the message is that of this particular text. It may lie close to the promise, but need not. Write the message as a claim in one sentence. The message need not appear in the text. It can be conveyed indirectly, through what the text is about and where it leads the reader. But you need to know what it is, because it decides which conclusion the text should land in. An example: a company selling a tool for video meetings writes an article about remote work. The article lands in the claim that remote teams work better with short daily check-ins than with long weekly meetings. The company's message is that its tool makes the short check-ins easy. That message may never appear in the text.

## 4. Who is the reader?

Describe the typical reader of content in the chosen genre and the chosen channel. What do they already know about the topic? What do they care about? What makes them want to read this kind of content? Is there something that makes the topic particularly pressing for them right now, for example a season, a piece of news, a decision or a new figure? What you write here about why now should also show in the structure.

## 5. What is the text's angle?

The angle is the particular perspective you choose on the topic. Choose only one. The most common mistake is trying to cover the topic from every direction so that the text becomes comprehensive. The result is a text that says a little about a lot and that the reader finds hard to take in. If you see several good angles, make one text of each. You then get more texts out of the same research, and each becomes clearer. Nor does a topic ever run out. Newspapers write about Christmas baking every year. One year it is about the classics, another about biscuits without sugar and a third about baking with children. Choose the angle that lies closest to the reader and that can carry the message. The angle governs the hook, the structure and the conclusion.

## 6. What is the hook?

The hook is what makes the text interesting to the reader. It is the tension that makes the reader want to read to the end: the question, the conflict or the surprise the text promises to resolve. The hook carries the angle. In the structure, the hook becomes ABT's But or PAC's premise. The hook is not the same thing as the opening. A text can begin with a scene or a detail that catches attention, but it is the hook that holds the reader. In short texts the two often coincide. The reader still needs to understand what is at stake before the hook arrives. In a press release, the hook is the news.

## 7. How should the text be structured?

Choose a technique, or follow the genre's own form, and then sketch the text. Begin with the whole text in one sentence, in the form below for the chosen technique. Then sketch it step by step: what each step says and which facts, figures and examples carry it. The sketch is the part of the brief the writer gets most use from. Make sure the sketch fits the length the assignment gives. The technique gives the text its arc but does not decide how the content sounds. An ABT text can explain and reason, and a PAC text can contain narrative examples.

- **ABT (And, But, Therefore)** suits a text that tells: a situation and what is at stake in it, what chafes and why, and what it leads to. The And and the But come quickly to what chafes. The Therefore is the rest of the text and is divided into sections with subheadings. The whole text in one sentence takes the form "[the situation] and if [...] then [...], but [the problem] because [...], therefore [...] by [...]", after Randy Olson's *Lincoln But Trump*.
- **PAC (Premise, Analysis, Conclusion)** suits a text that reasons: a question, an analysis of what the material means, and an answer. The premise comes quickly to the problem. The analysis is the largest part of the text and is divided into sections with subheadings. The whole text in one sentence takes the form "[the question or starting point], and [what the analysis shows], which means that [the conclusion]", after Gordon Harvey's *Elements of the Essay* and Skolverket's *Utredande text*.
- **The genre's own form** applies to genres whose structure belongs to the genre, for example the press release. You then choose no technique but follow the form the genre states. Where a genre names an ordinary technique of its own, that technique is its form. The whole text in one sentence gives the text's main point and how the text reaches it, in the order the reader meets them, with no fixed pattern.
- **Free structure** suits a text where none of the forms works. Begin with an opening that catches the reader and give the main point early. Then take one step at a time, in logical order, and close with an ending that ties the text together and points forward. Sketch the steps in the order they are to be read. The whole text in one sentence gives the text's main point and how the text reaches it, in the order the reader meets them, with no fixed pattern.

Sketch the ending too. It ties back to the hook and shows that it is resolved, and it carries the text's call to action, directly or indirectly.

Where a genre explicitly advises against a technique, as a report does against ABT and a press release against any arc laid over its news-first order, say so with the genre's reason. A genre's ordinary technique of none is not itself advice against an arc.

## 8. What should be left out?

State boundaries, side tracks you deliberately drop, and claims you cannot substantiate. Include also what lies outside the brand's mandate or what the brand for other reasons cannot or will not say. If a boundary has exceptions, write out where the line goes, for example something that may be reported but not argued for. Refer to the brand's writing guidelines if it has any. Many boundaries become visible only once you sketch the structure.

## 9. What should the reader have understood afterwards?

The answer is the text's conclusion. Write it as a claim in one sentence, not as a topic. "Remote work" is a topic. "Remote teams work better with short daily check-ins than with long weekly meetings" is a conclusion. The conclusion is what the structure lands in, that is ABT's Therefore or PAC's Conclusion, summed up in one sentence. With the genre's own form or free structure, the conclusion is the text's main point. If it cannot be summed up in one sentence, the sketch needs sharpening. The conclusion carries the message, either by stating it or by leading the reader to it. It lies within the brand's mandate and gives the reader something worth their time. What the understanding should lead to you answer in the next question.

## 10. What should the reader feel, think or do afterwards?

Describe the change in the reader the brand wants to bring about with the text. The conclusion is about what the reader understands. This question is about what that leads to.

- **Main effect:** choose one main effect and put it so concretely that it can be decided whether it has been reached. The effect can concern what the reader thinks or feels, for example "sees the brand as the obvious expert in the field". It can also concern what the reader does, for example "signs up for the webinar" or "contacts a salesperson".
- **The way there:** if the main effect is reached in several steps, describe what the reader first needs to see, understand or believe.
- **Call to action and measure:** what the reader is to do is the text's call to action. It can be direct, for example "contact our sales team". It can also be indirect: the text does not say it, but the reader is left with the feeling that they should act and knows how. If the effect can be measured, state also what shows it has been reached, for example sign-ups, clicks or enquiries. If the text deliberately has no call to action or no measure, say so, so that it shows as a choice and not a gap.

## 11. What makes the text RIV for the reader, and does the brief hold together?

This question checks that everything above serves the reader and holds together. Answer from what you have already written and state where in the structure each part is found. If something is missing there, add it now.

- **Relevant:** What makes the text relevant to the reader and their situation? Why should they read it at all? Is what makes the topic pressing right now part of the structure? The text must at least be relevant.
- **Interesting:** What makes the reader find the text interesting to read? Most often it is the hook.
- **Valuable:** What value should the text give the reader? What makes the reader feel the text was worth their time? Most often it is the conclusion and what carries it.

Then read the answers in one sweep and check that the chain holds:

- Does the reader follow from the sender and the channel?
- Does the angle lie close to the reader, and does the hook carry the angle?
- Does the structure resolve the hook's tension, and does it fit the length?
- Is the conclusion what the structure actually lands in, does it carry the message, and does it lie within the brand's mandate?
- Does what the reader understands lead to the effect you have described, and does that effect serve the brand's goals?
- Does anything in the structure conflict with what should be left out?

Write the check down: answer yes, no or partly to each question and give a short reason. Fix what does not hold together now. It is much easier than fixing it in a finished draft. Questions about the direction, that is the angle, the message and the conclusion, need to be settled before you write. Questions that need more research you write down as open questions and settle during the work.

## 12. What sources do you know of, and what do you need to find out?

A brief is often written before the research is done, and the research is often part of the assignment. Write down the sources you already know of or know you have access to, for example reports, statistics, earlier texts and interviews. Link also to the brand's platform or tone-of-voice guide, if you have not already done so under the sender. Link where you can and number the sources. If you already know that a claim in the sketch can be substantiated, refer to the source there. Add to the list as you find more, and note what you need to find out. It is no requirement that everything in the brief is substantiated. The requirement applies to the finished text. There every claim must be possible to substantiate with a source, an interview or your research, or be a generally accepted claim that the sender can make without risk.
