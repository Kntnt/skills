# Brief: the weekly receipt habit

Invented evaluation material. Larkspur Ledger and every person named are fictional and describe no real company or client.

## 1. What is the assignment?

- Working title: Ten minutes on a Friday
- Topic: keeping receipts in order through the tax year.
- Genre: general, a letter-length piece for the newsletter.
- Channel: Larkspur Ledger's monthly newsletter to sole traders.
- Language: en_GB, one version only.
- Length: roughly 300 to 450 words.
- Search phrase: none, deliberately; the newsletter is not found through search.

## 2. Which brand is the sender?

Larkspur Ledger, a bookkeeping app for sole traders in the UK. Promise: bookkeeping that takes minutes, not weekends. Image: known to its users as practical; most newsletter readers are on the free plan and have not tried the receipt scanner. Mandate: bookkeeping habits and its own app; it gives no tax advice. Tone of voice: plain, friendly, a little dry. Tone in this text: the same; a newsletter piece has room for the dry humour, so the brand and the genre pull the same way.

## 3. What does the sender want to convey with this text?

Larkspur Ledger's receipt scanner turns a weekly receipt session into a ten-minute job. The message need not appear in the text: the piece leads the reader to the weekly habit, and the reader, who already has the app, sees where the scanner fits.

## 4. Who is the reader?

Sole traders such as tutors, plumbers and designers who use the app's free plan and file their own Self Assessment return. They know the return is due on 31 January and that receipts matter, but most keep them in a drawer or an inbox until January. They care about their time and about not losing expenses they could have claimed. Why now: the newsletter goes out in early October, with the second half of the tax year starting and time left to change the habit before January.

## 5. What is the text's angle?

The weekly habit as what makes January short: what a sole trader gains by spending ten minutes on receipts every Friday instead of a weekend in January.

## 6. What is the hook?

The last weekend of January spent with a tin of faded receipts, and the expenses that go unclaimed because a receipt has faded to blank.

## 7. How should the text be structured?

ABT.

The whole text in one sentence: a sole trader keeps receipts through the year and files the return in January, and if the receipts are in order the return is a short job, but most leave them in a drawer because sorting feels like a job for later, and faded receipts and a lost weekend are the price, therefore the text shows that ten minutes every Friday makes the return short, by walking through one Friday session.

Sketch:

1. And: the return is due on 31 January, and receipts pile up in a drawer or an inbox until then.
2. But: till receipts fade within months, and expenses without a readable receipt go unclaimed. The survey: those who match receipts weekly report a median of 6 hours on the return, against 19 hours for those who wait until January (source 1).
3. Therefore, part 1: what a Friday session looks like: photograph the week's receipts, match each to a bank line, note anything odd.
4. Therefore, part 2: Priya Nand, a tutor, who moved to a Friday session in March (source 2).
5. The ending: back to the tin of receipts, which this January is empty.

## 8. What should be left out?

No tax advice, and no claims about which expenses are allowable. No comparison with other apps. No prices and no mention of the paid plan.

## 9. What should the reader have understood afterwards?

Ten minutes on receipts every Friday turns the January return from a lost weekend into a short job.

## 10. What should the reader feel, think or do afterwards?

- Main effect: the reader starts a weekly receipt habit.
- The way there: the reader first needs to believe that a weekly session is short.
- Call to action and measure: indirect. The text does not ask; the reader is left wanting to start this Friday and knowing the steps. Measure: the share of newsletter readers who scan a receipt in the week after it goes out.

## 11. What makes the text RIV for the reader, and does the brief hold together?

- Relevant: the October send-out with January ahead, in step 1 and the ending.
- Interesting: the hook, the January weekend and the faded receipts, in steps 1 and 2.
- Valuable: the steps of a Friday session in step 3, and Priya's example in step 4.

Chain check:

- Does the reader follow from the sender and the channel? Yes, the newsletter goes to the app's sole traders.
- Does the angle lie close to the reader, and does the hook carry the angle? Yes, every reader knows the January weekend.
- Does the structure resolve the hook's tension, and does it fit the length? Yes, five steps fit the length.
- Is the conclusion what the structure actually lands in, does it carry the message, and does it lie within the brand's mandate? Yes; the weekly habit is where the scanner fits, and a habit is within the mandate.
- Does what the reader understands lead to the effect you have described, and does that effect serve the brand's goals? Yes.
- Does anything in the structure conflict with what should be left out? No.
