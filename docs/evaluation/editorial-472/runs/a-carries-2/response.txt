# Ten minutes on a Friday

For four years, Priya Nand kept her receipts in a biscuit tin. She tutors maths in Leeds, and every January the tin came down from the shelf. "The last weekend of January was always gone," she says. The Self Assessment return is due on 31 January, and most of us spend the months before it the way Priya did, with receipts collecting in a drawer or an inbox until there is no more room for later.

The tin has two costs. The first is the weekend. The second is quieter. Till receipts are printed on thermal paper, and they often fade within months, faster in a warm kitchen. When Priya finally opened the tin one January, two receipts from the spring had faded to blank, and she could not claim them.

In March she tried something else: a session every Friday. "About ten minutes, with a cup of tea."

It works like this. She photographs each receipt when she gets it, while it is still new and readable. On Friday she sits down with the week's photographs, matches each one to a line in her bank account and makes a note of anything odd, such as a payment she does not recognise or a receipt with no bank line at all. The app reads the date, the supplier and the total from each photograph and suggests the bank line it matches.

Our users see the same pattern. In a survey this summer, 412 of them told us how long their last return took. Those who matched receipts at least once a week reported a median of 6 hours. Those who left receipts until January reported 19. The figures are what people told us rather than anything we measured, and they do not show that the habit caused the difference. But they point the same way as Priya's January.

"January this year was an evening," she says.

The tin is still on the shelf. These days it holds tea bags, and the receipts in her phone are all readable.

## Findings (all unresolved)

1. **The dates behind Priya's short January don't work (needs the writer).** The interview in `material.md` is dated September 2026. It says she started the Friday sessions in March 2026 and that "January this year was an evening." That January is January 2026, which is before she started. The text gives no year for March and presents the short January as the habit's result, and as agreeing with the survey.
   - *What the reader loses:* the piece's payoff, a short January caused by the Friday session, which the material does not support.
   - *To settle it:* the writer needs to check with Priya which January and which March she meant.
2. **"Priya's January" comes before the reader knows what it was.** The survey paragraph ends "But they point the same way as Priya's January." The quote that says what her January became ("January this year was an evening") only arrives in the next paragraph. At that point the reader knows only her lost January weekends, which point the other way.
   - *What the reader loses:* they can't tell what the survey is supposed to agree with.
   - *To settle it:* moving the quote ahead of the survey sentence would fix it.
3. **The scanner sentence makes Priya sound like a user of the app.** "The app reads the date, the supplier and the total from each photograph…" ends the paragraph about Priya's routine, so "each photograph" reads as her photographs. The interview doesn't say which app she uses, if any, and the support note describes the scanner only in general terms.
   - *What the reader loses:* Priya reads as a customer testimonial that the material doesn't back.
   - *To settle it:* the sentence has to leave her routine but still show where the scanner fits in a Friday session (brief question 3). My attempt at this failed; see below.
4. **Details about Priya that the interview doesn't give (needs the writer).** These are "every January the tin came down from the shelf", "such as a payment she does not recognise or a receipt with no bank line at all" and "The tin is still on the shelf. These days it holds tea bags". The interview gives the tin, the four years and "notes anything odd", and nothing more.
   - *What the reader loses:* made-up detail reads as reported fact about a named person.
   - *To settle it:* cutting the ending isn't the fix, because it would break the ending's link back to the opening.
5. **"most of us" (needs the writer).** The survey gives medians for two groups, not how many users wait until January. The brief's description of its reader doesn't count as evidence.
   - *What the reader loses:* a claim about a majority with nothing behind it.
6. **No reason to start now (brief question 4; needs the writer).** The text never says it is October, that the second half of the tax year is starting, or that there is still time before January. The only deadline it gives is 31 January, and "In a survey this summer" dates the piece only as after the summer.
   - *What the reader loses:* the reason to start this Friday rather than in January.
   - *To settle it:* the material has no timing facts to write this from.
