```markdown
# Ten minutes on a Friday

For four years, Priya Nand kept her receipts in a biscuit tin. She tutors maths in Leeds, and every January the tin came down from the shelf. "The last weekend of January was always gone," she says. The Self Assessment return is due on 31 January, and most of us spend the months before it the way Priya did, with receipts collecting in a drawer or an inbox until there is no more room for later.

The tin has two costs. The first is the weekend. The second is quieter. Till receipts are printed on thermal paper, and they often fade within months, faster in a warm kitchen. When Priya finally opened the tin one January, two receipts from the spring had faded to blank, and she could not claim them.

In March she tried something else: a session every Friday. "About ten minutes, with a cup of tea."

It works like this. She photographs each receipt when she gets it, while it is still new and readable. On Friday she sits down with the week's photographs, matches each one to a line in her bank account and makes a note of anything odd, such as a payment she does not recognise or a receipt with no bank line at all. The app reads the date, the supplier and the total from each photograph and suggests the bank line it matches.

We asked our users. In a survey this summer, 412 of them told us how long their last return took. Those who matched receipts at least once a week reported a median of 6 hours. Those who left receipts until January reported 19. The figures are what people told us rather than anything we measured, and they do not show that the habit caused the difference. But they point the same way as Priya's January.

"January this year was an evening," she says.

The tin is still on the shelf. These days it holds tea bags, and the receipts in her phone are all readable.
```

**Still open, for the writer**

1. **Priya's short January comes before her habit.** The text runs "In March she tried something else", then "But they point the same way as Priya's January", then "'January this year was an evening,' she says". That makes the short January look like the result of the Friday sessions. But the interview is dated September 2026 and she moved to Fridays in March 2026, so "January this year" is January 2026, before the habit began. The ending's payoff is a sequence the material contradicts. Check with Priya which January took an evening and when she started.
2. **The shelf, the tea bags and the phone aren't in the material.** This affects two passages:
   - "every January the tin came down from the shelf"
   - the whole ending: "The tin is still on the shelf. These days it holds tea bags, and the receipts in her phone are all readable."

   The interview only mentions a biscuit tin kept until January. The support note says a photo taken while a receipt is new stays readable. Nothing supports a shelf, tea bags, her phone, or all her receipts being readable, so the ending describes a scene about a real, named person that she never reported. Cutting it would leave the text with no ending that returns to the tin, and the material has nothing to replace it with.
3. **The examples of "anything odd" are invented.** The text says "such as a payment she does not recognise or a receipt with no bank line at all", but the interview only says she "notes anything odd". Readers will take the two examples as Priya's routine when the writer added them.
4. **The text implies Priya uses the scanner.** "The app reads the date, the supplier and the total from each photograph…" sits inside Priya's routine, and "each photograph" points back to her photos. The material doesn't say she uses the app. The support note only supports a general statement about the scanner.
5. **"most of us" is unsupported, and "us" means two groups.** In "most of us spend the months before it the way Priya did", the material gives no share, and the brief's description of the reader isn't evidence. Here "us" means sole traders, while "told us" and "We" in paragraph 5 mean Larkspur Ledger.
7. **The brief's "why now" (question 4) is missing.** Nothing says that months remain before 31 January or that starting now still helps. The reader gets no reason to start this Friday rather than later. I didn't write it in, because the brief's October send date is a requirement, not source material.
