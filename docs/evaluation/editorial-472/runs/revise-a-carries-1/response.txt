```markdown
# Ten minutes on a Friday

For four years, Priya Nand kept her receipts in a biscuit tin. She tutors maths in Leeds, and every January the tin came down from the shelf. "The last weekend of January was always gone," she says. The Self Assessment return is due on 31 January, and most of us spend the months before it the way Priya did, with receipts collecting in a drawer or an inbox until there is no more room for later.

The tin has two costs. The first is the weekend. The second is quieter. Till receipts are printed on thermal paper, and they often fade within months, faster in a warm kitchen. When Priya finally opened the tin one January, two receipts from the spring had faded to blank, and she could not claim them.

In March she tried something else: a session every Friday. "About ten minutes, with a cup of tea."

It works like this. She photographs each receipt when she gets it, while it is still new and readable. On Friday she sits down with the week's photographs, matches each one to a line in her bank account and makes a note of anything odd, such as a payment she does not recognise or a receipt with no bank line at all. The app reads the date, the supplier and the total from each photograph and suggests the bank line it matches.

"January this year was an evening," she says.

Our users see the same pattern. In a survey this summer, 412 of them told us how long their last return took. Those who matched receipts at least once a week reported a median of 6 hours. Those who left receipts until January reported 19. The figures are what people told us rather than anything we measured, and they do not show that the habit caused the difference. But they point the same way as Priya's January.

The tin is still on the shelf. These days it holds tea bags, and the receipts in her phone are all readable.
```

## Findings

1. **Repaired.** "But they point the same way as Priya's January." pointed ahead to something the text hadn't said yet.
   - At that point in the original, Priya's Januaries had appeared only as the lost weekend and the January her faded receipts couldn't be claimed.
   - The January that "was an evening" came in the next paragraph. Readers couldn't tell what the survey figures were being compared with, and "Our users see the same pattern" had no pattern of hers to match.
   - **Repair:** the paragraph "'January this year was an evening,' she says." now stands before the survey paragraph, word for word as it was.

2. **Left for the writer (brief questions 2 and 12): "most of us spend the months before it the way Priya did".**
   - The source material gives no share that would support "most". The survey reports medians for the two groups but not how many people were in each.
   - "Us" also puts Larkspur Ledger among the people filing a return, while "412 of them told us" uses "us" for the company.
   - Readers get a generalisation nothing supports, and can't tell who is speaking.

3. **Left for the writer (question 12): details about Priya that aren't in the interview.**
   - The details are "the tin came down from the shelf", "The tin is still on the shelf", "These days it holds tea bags" and "the receipts in her phone are all readable".
   - The support note says only that a photograph taken while the receipt is new stays readable.
   - The ending's resolution rests on details only Priya can confirm.

4. **Left for the writer (question 12): "such as a payment she does not recognise or a receipt with no bank line at all".** The interview says only that she "notes anything odd". Readers take the examples as her own practice.

5. **Left for the writer (question 12): "The app reads the date, the supplier and the total from each photograph and suggests the bank line it matches."**
   - The sentence sits inside the description of Priya's routine, and "each photograph" means her photographs, so readers learn that she uses the scanner.
   - The support note covers what the scanner does. The interview doesn't say she uses it.

6. **Left for the writer (question 12): the order of events, and what it implies caused her short January.**
   - Readers are led to take "January this year was an evening" as the result of the Friday habit ("In March she tried something else"; "But they point the same way as Priya's January.").
   - The interview is dated September 2026 and puts her move to Fridays in March 2026. The January she calls "this year" therefore came before the habit began.
   - The central example may not show what the text implies. The writer needs to find out which January she meant and what made it short.

7. **Left for the writer (questions 4 and 11): nothing says why now.**
   - Nothing in the text places readers in October, at the start of the second half of the tax year, with time left before January.
   - Readers lose the reason to start now rather than next January.
   - The send-out timing is in the brief but not in the source material, so writing it in would add a claim.
