What carries in the text's favour: the quotations are verbatim, the arguments and chronology survive, the whole results section is untouched, every sentence that limits what the text claims stands word for word, no fact was invented, and the reply refuses to invent a byline rather than fill the slot. Several repairs are genuine — the overclaiming headline and the ambiguous storage sentence were real defects on the page.

What fails it: two claims were changed outside the review's stated findings — the attribution filled out, and the bridge's reservation deleted — and neither is reported. That alone breaches the criterion, which asks that claims outside the findings be preserved and that every removal be reported. It is compounded by an affirmative and false statement of completeness: the reply declares that exactly one claim was removed and that nothing had its scope, certainty, source, chronology, cause or meaning moved, and then itemises which limiting sentences survived — an account precise enough that a reader would reasonably stop checking. A second removal sits behind that assurance. The harm to the delivered text is small, since the reservation survives inside the quotation; the harm to the account is not, because a review whose stated inventory is wrong cannot be relied on for the changes it did not think to mention. Set against this, the reply's genuine strength — five self-created defects named and attributed to the repairs that caused them — makes the omission harder to excuse, not easier: the same pass that caught those did not catch a deleted claim.
