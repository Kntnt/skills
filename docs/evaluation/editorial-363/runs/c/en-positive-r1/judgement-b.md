## 3. R1 — FAIL

What the review got right is real and is reported accurately. Two bridges that told the reader what a quotation would say before it said it (`the experience offered a preparation lesson`, `a qualified endorsement of repeating the trial`) were genuine visible defects and were repaired. The clause `The maintenance team designed the categories` genuinely duplicated the quotation above it; its removal is a legitimate removal and the reply reports it as a removal, names the clause, and states correctly that the fact survives in Lind's own words. `working days` → `business days` is a correct locale form for `en_US`. All three quotations, every figure, date and name, and both of the text's limiting sentences came back intact.

The failure is on the other half of the criterion. The input was a clean, carefully hedged text, and the changes that were not repairs are template-driven rewriting of prose that was working:

- **Five subheadings were inserted where the text had none.** By the run's own count four of them restate the sentence or quotation immediately beneath in almost its own words, and two of those — `Preparation took more time than the first reports` and `Repeating the trial would mean an extra week` — put Lind's verdict in the text's voice above the quotation in which she gives it. That is the very defect the round was sent to remove, reinstated in a more prominent position. The reply reports this (finding 3) and reports it accurately, including the recreation of the defect, but the delivered text still carries it.
- **`The team set the log up around its own categories` asserts more than the text supports** — the section says Svale configured the log. This is a claim change manufactured by the review, not found in the source. The reply reports it (finding 4) and states the overclaim correctly.
- **`For an optional next step` lost `optional`.** In a text whose whole character is qualification — the note "explicitly does not attribute the difference to the software", "not a recommendation of Svale for every housing company" — stripping a hedge from the closing line is a modality change against the grain of the piece. The reply reports it explicitly and accurately as a claim change, but reports it as licensed by a finding against the formula; the hedge did not have to go with the formula.
- **The publisher credit moved from beneath the standfirst to below the call to action.** The reply reports the move, reports accurately that no finding named the line, and explains that a structural rule about the lead forced it. Whatever the rule, the delivered text now discloses the supplier's authorship only after the reader has finished the account and passed the link.
- The headline rewrite and the lead merge changed nothing for the better and had no defect to answer.

So: the removals and the changed claims are all reported, and reported accurately — the accounting in this run is unusually honest and I found nothing in it that misdescribes the delivered text. But R1 asks whether the review preserved what worked while fixing what did not, and this one handed back a text carrying one new unsupported claim, one stripped hedge, a relocated disclosure and four subheadings that restate their own paragraphs. Net, the delivered text is worse than the input on the defect class the run was targeting. Accurate confession does not repair the delivery. Fail.
