## 2. F1 — **fail**

Three defects, of which two were seen by no checker.

1. **"the note does not attribute the difference to the software"** — *certainty, dropped.* Source: "the note **explicitly** does not attribute the difference to the software." The modifier is not decoration. "Does not attribute" is satisfied by a note that says nothing at all about cause; "explicitly does not attribute" reports a disclaimer the note makes in terms. The material's "Forbidden inferences" section turns on that disclaimer — it is the customer's own refusal to credit the product, and it is the single strongest thing a supplier-published case can put on the page. Losing the word converts an active refusal into mere silence, and silence is what a reader fills in with the causal inference the material forbids. **Neither comparison recorded this.**
2. **"No comparison with another supplier is available" is absent from the draft entirely.** — *dropped caveat.* The draft says "it chose Svale Systems after testing whether the log could show the status of each repair", and stops. The material supplies the caveat as a standing statement about the evidence, and it is the caveat an operations manager reading a supplier's own case study most needs: nothing here tells them how Svale fared against anything else. A reader of "chose X after testing" naturally takes a selection to have been made among candidates. The material's sentence exists to forestall exactly that reading, and the draft drops it. **Neither comparison recorded this** — the accounting tables pair draft passages with source passages, so a source sentence with no draft counterpart falls through the method.
3. **Heading "Elm Quay does not credit the software for the shorter time"** — *whose assertion.* Source: "the note explicitly does not attribute the difference to the software." The material establishes what one document declines to do; the heading makes it the company's settled position. The material's own supervisor credits the log with something ("Having one view of the reports helps us"), so a company that credits the software for cross-shift visibility while its note declines to credit it for the timing is fully compatible with the package, and the heading is false of it. This one was found by both comparisons, disclosed in the reply, and left in.

Recorded but not counted as defects: "An internal note dated 4 December 2025" leaves the owner implicit where the material says "Elm Quay's internal trial note", which the surrounding clause supplies; "Residents could still report a fault by telephone" swaps the material's "repair" for "fault", a near-synonym in British housing usage though not an exact one; "she says" for answers given by email is ordinary attribution and stages no scene, and the draft nowhere claims a meeting, a room or a manner.

Supported and correctly handled: the headline's past tense; the two-building scope in headline and body; 31 reports with both exclusions; the medians kept as report-to-assignment and never turned into completion time; the workload difference stated in the material's own voice; "There are no measurements of cost, resident satisfaction or completion time" in the material's own modality; the reservation surviving in the quotation and in "She did not recommend Svale Systems to every housing company"; the unexpanded trial and the pending category check; and the link described as something to read.

## 3. G2 — **fail**

The ordering is right — headline, standfirst, byline, lead, four sections, ending — and the customer account is otherwise complete: situation (separate telephone and email records, shifts unable to see the same information), action (the team decided, designed its own categories, kept the telephone route open; Svale configured and trained), results (31 reports under exclusions, two days against three, the workload difference, the absent measurements), appraisal (Lind's preparation lesson, her qualified verdict, the withheld general recommendation). The customer is the acting party throughout, and the ending carries a call to action built from the one supplied route, described as the brief requires.

What is missing is **the publisher stance**. The material states plainly: "The supplier approved publication of this case as supplier-published material; it is not independent journalism." Nothing in the delivered text says who publishes it. It carries a personal byline, a neutral reporting register, section headings that read as an editor's, and a closing line that recommends the supplier's document in the third person — the complete apparatus of independent journalism, with the one sentence that would tell a reader otherwise left out. The first comparison logged this as an editorial question on the ground that the draft "makes no claim of independence"; that is the wrong test. A signed article about a named company, with no publisher line, claims independence by form even where it asserts none in words, and the material names that as a thing the piece is not. The reply hands the decision to the commissioning party, which is the one party whose interest lies in it going unsaid.

A second, lesser G2 problem: the standfirst opens "Elm Quay Housing manages 640 flats" and the lead's second sentence says "The company manages 640 flats". The two parts are not doing distinct jobs at that point; the standfirst spends its first and most valuable sentence on a fact the lead then repeats, and buys nothing with it.

## 4. L1 — **pass**

Competent, unshowy British English. "Here is what that note says, what it declined to conclude, and what the supervisor would change" is a well-built triad that sets the article's real agenda — a document's limits — rather than an outcome. "The supervisor is specific about where the work actually went" earns the quotation that follows it. The heading "Elm Quay designed the categories, Svale set up the log" uses the comma splice that British headline style permits, and the balance of the two clauses carries the division of labour in six words. Sentence lengths vary; no paragraph outstays its welcome; nothing reads as imported syntax or as a translated construction.

Two soft spots. "In 2025 its maintenance team spent eight weeks testing whether two of its buildings could work from a single record of repairs" is a long stretch of abstract noun before the reader reaches anything concrete. And "Expansion is still an open question at Elm Quay" is the flattest of the four headings, in a piece whose other three are specific.

## 5. Quoted speech

Source language is English and the draft is en_GB, so nothing is translated. All three supplied quotations are used, word for word; the material's double marks become British single marks and each closing full stop becomes a comma before the attribution.

1. **'We wanted the evening shift to see what the morning shift had already done. The categories were ours; Svale helped us put them into the log,' she says.** — Source: the first complete usable quotation, identical. *One pass:* yes; a British reader supplies nothing. The semicolon joins two independent clauses in the ordinary way and the metonymy of shifts standing for the staff on them is a figure the language uses of itself. *Substance:* intact — "helped us" is help, not doing it for them, and the ownership claim "were ours" stands where the speaker put it.
2. **'We spent more time agreeing on the categories than entering the first reports. I would set that time aside before the next building starts,' she says.** — Source: the second quotation, identical. *One pass:* yes; the comparative ellipsis is ordinary English. *Substance:* intact, including the conditional "I would" and the implicit self-criticism; the framing sentence keeps her horizon at Elm Quay's own next building and does not turn the remark into advice for the reader.
3. **'I would choose to do the trial again. Having one view of the reports helps us, but I would leave an extra week for preparation,' she says.** — Source: the third quotation, identical. *One pass:* yes. "Having one view of the reports helps us" is a shorthand a British reader takes whole. *Substance:* intact — the "but" clause that carries the reservation survives, the hedged "I would" is not hardened, and the introducing sentence "Lind's own assessment is a qualified one" marks it as hers rather than as a reading of the figures, which the material expressly requires ("It is not an inference from the figures").

No quotation the material offers is missing: three are supplied as complete and usable, and three are used.

**In one sentence:** every passage of quoted speech in this draft is speech a reader of British English takes on one pass, with the source's meaning, stance, reservation and voice intact.

## 7. Stop or delivery

**A delivery that should not have happened.** Three things are wrong with the text at once, and the reply names only the third.

The heading still puts the company behind a refusal that belongs to a document — disclosed, but disclosed in a covering note that does not travel with the article. The load-bearing "explicitly" is gone, which weakens the one sentence standing between this piece and the causal claim the material forbids. And the supplied caveat that no comparison with another supplier is available has vanished, in an article that tells operations managers a supplier was chosen after testing.

Set against a text that also declines to tell its reader it is supplier-published, that combination is not a near miss. Each omission individually is small; together they all lean the same way, towards a piece that reads more like independent validation of Svale Systems than the material permits. The run should either have applied the three-word heading repair and re-checked, or stopped and handed back the heading, the missing disclosure and the two dropped guards for settlement.
