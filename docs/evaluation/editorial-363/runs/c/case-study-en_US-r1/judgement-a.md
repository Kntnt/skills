## 2. F1 — **Fail**

Not for anything the draft asserts. Sentence by sentence this is the most careful body prose of its kind: I could not find a single unsupported addition, changed term, changed subject, shifted date, raised modality or invented attribute in it. It fails on two things the material supplies and the draft leaves out, in an arrangement that makes both of them matter.

### Cited defects

**1. The non-recommendation is dropped, and the endorsement is left adjacent to the call to action.** The material states: "Lind did not recommend Svale to every housing company", and its judging section requires that "The customer's reservation must survive." The draft carries no version of that sentence. What it does instead is put her final quotation and the call to action in the same paragraph, in this order:

> "I would choose to do the trial again. Having one view of the reports helps us, but I would leave an extra week for preparation," Lind says. Operations managers weighing a similar test can read the [implementation checklist](https://example.invalid/svale/checklist).

The reservation that survives inside the quotation is about preparation time. The reservation that is missing is the one about *reach* — that she would not tell every housing company to do this. A reader of a supplier-published case meets "I would choose to do the trial again" and is moved, in the next clause, toward the supplier's document, with nothing between the two. The material supplies the sentence that stands between them, and the draft omits precisely it. Both checkers logged the omission as permitted on the ground that the draft "makes no recommendation claim on Lind's behalf"; that tests the sentences in isolation rather than the reader's path through them.

**2. The supplier-published disclosure is dropped.** The material states: "The supplier approved publication of this case as supplier-published material; it is not independent journalism." Nothing of this reaches the page. What does reach it is a personal byline over three sections of third-person reporting that ends by pointing at the publisher's own document. The reply is candid about this and gives its reason — "the anatomy has no slot for one" — which is an account of why it happened, not a reason the reader is better off. The first checker's claim accounting confirms the disclosure is absent; the second files it as "an editorial and compliance question about the format".

## 3. G2 — **Fail**

Headline, standfirst, byline, lead, three sections and an ending, in that order, and the top of the piece is well built. The headline states the bounded event; the standfirst does its own job and does it hard — "Eight weeks, two buildings and 31 repair reports" gives the scale in seven words, then names what the note records *and* refuses to attribute, so the causal guard is in the summary rather than parked three sections down; the lead does not restate the standfirst but supplies the situation under it (the date, the scale, the separate records, the motive). The middle section on the figures is exemplary: count with exclusions, medians with workloads and the explicit non-attribution, then "Assignment is not completion" and the three absences.

Two failures, both at the foot.

**No publisher stance.** For a customer account this part is required, and the material states it in terms the draft could have lifted. Its absence is not a missing nicety; it changes what the reader thinks they are reading. Everything else in the account inventory is present and separable — situation, action, results, appraisal — and the customer is unmistakably the acting party throughout (Elm Quay decides, tests, chooses, designs, keeps; Svale configures, trains and is named in the third person). That makes the missing line the one hole in an otherwise complete structure.

**The ending welds the appraisal to the call to action.** The last paragraph is Lind's verdict and the supplier's ask, in two consecutive sentences with no boundary between them. The reader cannot tell where the customer's voice stops and the publisher's business begins, and the endorsement effectively becomes the reason to click. An ending and a call to action can share a section; they should not share a breath, and in a piece the material insists is supplier-published they especially should not.

A third, smaller point: the call to action no longer says whose checklist it is. "Operations managers weighing a similar test can read **the** implementation checklist" uses a definite article for a thing never previously mentioned. The reader is offered something and not told what it belongs to. This came in as a repair, and I return to it in §6.

## 4. L1 — **Pass**

Idiomatic American English with no imported phrasing. The locale is held at the level of word choice and not just spelling — "apartments", "test" where a British draft would write "trial" as a verb, double quotation marks, "against three in the preceding eight-week period". Attribution is handled the way an American desk handles it, with the speaker identified after the first quotation ("says maintenance supervisor Maya Lind") and by surname thereafter ("Lind says").

The best writing is in the figures section, where the sentences get short on purpose and the shortest one does the most work: "Assignment is not completion." Three words, and the inference the material forbids is closed off. The standfirst's colon construction — "Eight weeks, two buildings and 31 repair reports: Elm Quay Housing tested…" — is a native news-summary move and lands cleanly.

One sentence is compressed past comfort: "The trial produced no measurement of how long repairs took to finish, and none of cost or resident satisfaction." The gapped "none of cost or resident satisfaction" parses, but the reader has to carry "measurement" across the comma and re-fit it, and the possessive-free "none of cost" reads oddly for a beat. Not an error and not a foreign construction; a tighter rhythm than the sentence can support.

## 5. Quoted speech

1. **"We wanted the evening shift to see what the morning shift had already done. The categories were ours; Svale helped us put them into the log," says maintenance supervisor Maya Lind.** — renders the material's first complete usable quotation, word for word, including the semicolon and the past perfect. An American-English reader takes it on one pass; nothing has to be supplied. Meaning, stance and voice are the source's: the credit to Svale is real and bounded, the ownership claim is hers.
2. **"We spent more time agreeing on the categories than entering the first reports. I would set that time aside before the next building starts," Lind says.** — the second supplied quotation, word for word. One pass: yes; the elliptical comparative is how English does this. The conditional stays a conditional and is not converted into advice the draft gives, and "the next building" is not widened into a rollout. The sentence before it ("The team designed those categories itself, and kept telephone reporting open for residents") sets it up without telling the reader what it means.
3. **"I would choose to do the trial again. Having one view of the reports helps us, but I would leave an extra week for preparation," Lind says.** — the third supplied quotation, word for word. One pass: yes. Inside the quotation marks nothing has moved: the reservation after "but" stays in the same sentence as the endorsement, "helps us" is not widened to housing companies in general, and no hedge is added or removed. What has moved is around it — the material's "Lind did not recommend Svale to every housing company" is not there, and the call to action follows immediately, so her bounded verdict is read as the warrant for the next click. The voice is hers; the use made of it is not the material's.

**Missing.** All three quotations the material offers and permits are used, each whole, each once, none welded, none split, none staged in a scene the material forbids. No fourth quotation appears.

**One sentence:** Every passage of quoted speech in this draft is speech an American-English reader takes on one pass with the source's meaning, stance, reservation and voice intact inside the quotation marks — though the third is placed where the missing sentence about not recommending Svale to every housing company should have stood.

## 7. Stop or delivery

**A delivery that should not have happened in this form.** The prose deserves better than this verdict. Within its own four corners it is the most disciplined of its kind: no causal claim anywhere, the workload caveat and "explicitly" both intact, assignment kept apart from completion in its own three-word sentence, every absence named, the comparison caveat carried in the same breath as the choice of supplier, the customer acting in every sentence that has an actor, and three quotations reproduced to the character.

But a supplier-published case that never says it is supplier-published, and that ends with the customer's "I would choose to do the trial again" sitting one sentence away from a link to the supplier's document — with the material's own "Lind did not recommend Svale to every housing company" nowhere on the page — is a piece whose omissions all run one way. Two sentences of supplied text, placed in the last paragraph, would have fixed both and cost nothing the brief cares about.

The reply is honest about one of the two. It raises the missing disclosure unprompted, names the material's sentence, and tells the user to add a line if house rules require it. It says nothing about the non-recommendation, because no comparison told it there was anything to say. The user is therefore left holding one problem they know about and one they do not.
