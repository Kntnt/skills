## 2. F1 — **fail**

Sentence by sentence this is the tightest draft of its kind: the figures are exact, the medians are named as medians and as report-to-assignment, "explicitly" survives in the body, the workload difference is in the material's own voice, "Assignment is not completion" is written in as a guard, the comparison caveat is carried, "not yet expanded" keeps its "yet", and the three quotations are verbatim. No individual assertion is false.

The failure is what is not there, and where the gap falls.

1. **"Lind did not recommend Svale to every housing company" is absent from the draft entirely.** — *Dropped caveat, with a consequent implication.* The material states this in its own voice, immediately before it explains that her final quotation "is her actual, qualified assessment. It is not an inference from the figures." The judging section then says, in terms: "The customer's reservation must survive."

   What survives in the draft is the preparation reservation inside the final quotation — "but I would leave an extra week for preparation" — which is a reservation about Elm Quay's own scheduling. The reservation about the *supplier* is gone. And it is gone from a text whose last two sentences are:

   > "I would choose to do the trial again. Having one view of the reports helps us, but I would leave an extra week for preparation," Lind says. Operations managers weighing a similar test can read the implementation checklist.

   The customer's endorsement, then the reader's next step, in one paragraph, with nothing between them. The material supplies exactly one sentence that blocks the reader from taking her "I would choose to do the trial again" as a recommendation extended to them, and that sentence is the one the draft leaves out. The implication the paragraph carries — that her experience is a warrant for other operations managers to act — goes beyond the supplied material, which records her declining to make that extension. Both checkers saw the omission and licensed it on the ground that "omission is permitted"; that rule holds for material a text merely has no room for, not for the one statement the material names as having to survive.

2. **Standfirst: "The trial note records a shorter median time from report to assignment, but does not attribute it to the software."** — *Dropped caveat, and a concessive frame.* The material pairs the non-attribution with the fact that makes it necessary: "The periods had different workloads, and the note explicitly does not attribute the difference to the software." The standfirst takes the non-attribution and drops both the confound and "explicitly". The concessive "but" then does the rest of the work: a reader meets a recorded improvement followed by a demurral, and the natural reading of a demurral without a stated reason is modesty. The body repairs this four paragraphs later, which is why I record it as contributing rather than as sufficient on its own — but it leans the same way as the first item.

Recorded and not counted: "The trial produced no measurement of how long repairs took to finish, and none of cost or resident satisfaction" narrows the material's unrestricted "There are no … measurements" to the trial, which is the safe direction. "This account records no comparison with another supplier" likewise narrows safely. "Says" for answers given by email attributes a statement without asserting a spoken occasion, and the draft stages no scene. "Check the categories on larger repairs" in the heading is restated verbatim from the material in the sentence below it.

Nothing forbidden is asserted: no causal claim, hedged or otherwise; assignment time is never turned into completion time, and the draft says so outright; no cost, satisfaction or rescue claim appears.

## 3. G2 — **fail**

Headline, standfirst, byline, lead, three sections. Most of the customer account is there and correctly assigned: situation (separate telephone and email storage, shifts unable to see the same information, 640 apartments), action (the team decided, designed its own categories, kept telephone reporting open; Svale configured and trained), results (31 reports with both exclusions, two days against three, the workload difference, the note's explicit non-attribution, the three absences). The customer is the acting party throughout and Svale appears only where the material has it act. The call to action is built from the one supplied route and describes it as a document to read.

Two required elements fail.

**No publisher stance.** The material states: "The supplier approved publication of this case as supplier-published material; it is not independent journalism." Nothing in the delivered text tells a reader who is publishing it. It carries a personal byline, neutral reporting syntax, an editor's section headings, and a closing recommendation of the supplier's own document — the full apparatus of independent journalism, minus the one line that would tell the reader it is not. The reply notices this and attributes it to the format ("the anatomy has no slot for one"), which explains the omission without excusing it: the material names the stance, and a piece that will not carry it is not the piece the material licenses.

**The appraisal is incomplete.** A customer's appraisal in this material has two halves — she would do it again, with a week more preparation; and she would not recommend the supplier to every housing company. The draft carries the first and not the second, so the appraisal a reader receives is warmer than the appraisal the customer gave.

A third, lesser point: there is no ending doing an ending's job. The last section's second paragraph is the final quotation with the call to action welded onto it in the same paragraph, so the account does not close — it stops and then sells. That adjacency is also what turns the F1 omission into an implication.

## 4. L1 — **pass**

Crisp, professional American journalism. The standfirst's inventory opening — "Eight weeks, two buildings and 31 repair reports:" — puts the scale of the evidence in front of the reader before any claim, which is the honest move and also the readable one. "Assignment is not completion" is a three-word paragraph opener that does more than a paragraph of hedging would. "The note counts 31 reports, and names what it leaves out" is the best heading in the piece: it makes the note's candour the subject. Sentence rhythm varies, no paragraph runs long, and quotations are attributed in the American manner with the attribution outside the quotation and the comma inside.

Two British constructions sit oddly in an American text. "Svale configured the log and trained **six staff** in two sessions" uses *staff* as a count plural, which is British; an American desk would write "six staff members" or "six employees". And "two working days during the trial, **against** three in the preceding eight-week period" is British comparative journalese where American usage reaches for "versus" or "compared with". Neither stops a reader, and "apartments", "test" and the attribution style are all correctly American, so the draft passes — but the two seams are visible.

## 5. Quoted speech

Source language is English and the draft is en_US, so nothing is translated; all three quotations are reproduced word for word.

1. **"We wanted the evening shift to see what the morning shift had already done. The categories were ours; Svale helped us put them into the log," says maintenance supervisor Maya Lind.** — Source: the first complete usable quotation, identical. *One pass:* yes. The shift-for-staff metonymy is a figure American English uses of itself, and the semicolon joins two independent clauses in the ordinary way. *Substance:* intact — "helped us" stays help, "were ours" keeps the ownership claim, and the speaker is introduced with the supplied job title and no more.
2. **"We spent more time agreeing on the categories than entering the first reports. I would set that time aside before the next building starts," Lind says.** — Source: the second quotation, identical. *One pass:* yes; the comparative ellipsis needs nothing supplied. *Substance:* intact — the conditional "I would" survives, and the sentence before it ("The team designed those categories itself, and kept telephone reporting open for residents") keeps the remark inside Elm Quay's own work rather than turning it into advice for the reader.
3. **"I would choose to do the trial again. Having one view of the reports helps us, but I would leave an extra week for preparation," Lind says.** — Source: the third quotation, identical. *One pass:* yes; "one view of the reports" is plain idiom. *Substance:* the words, the hedged "I would" and the "but" clause are all intact, and the draft does not derive the verdict from the medians, which the material forbids. But its **stance has moved by placement**: the material calls it "her actual, qualified assessment", and the draft gives it no framing sentence at all, drops it after the pending-decision paragraph, and follows it in the same paragraph with "Operations managers weighing a similar test can read the implementation checklist." A qualified personal assessment thereby becomes the warrant for the reader's next step — and the material's counterweight, "Lind did not recommend Svale to every housing company", is nowhere on the page.

No quotation the material offers is missing: three are supplied as complete and usable, and three are used.

**In one sentence:** every passage of quoted speech in this draft is speech a reader of American English takes on one pass with its meaning, certainty and voice intact, but the third's stance is moved by its placement immediately before the call to action, in a text that omits the material's statement that she did not recommend Svale to every housing company.

## 7. Stop or delivery

**A delivery that should not have happened.** Nothing in this text is false, and that is what makes the outcome worse rather than better: the distortion is achieved entirely by leaving two things out, so no comparison of draft passages against source passages could catch it, and none did.

What goes out is a signed, independently framed article that tells operations managers a customer would run the trial again, immediately hands them the supplier's checklist, never says the supplier published it, and never mentions that the customer declined to recommend that supplier to companies like theirs. Every sentence is supported. The piece as a whole is not.

The reply is candid about the missing disclosure and offers to add one, which is the right instinct arriving at the wrong moment — after delivery, in a covering note, framed as a house-rules question. The run should have stopped, or fixed both: one sentence restoring "Lind did not recommend Svale Systems to every housing company" before the call to action, and one line saying who publishes the piece.
