## 4. L1 — **Pass**

Idiomatic American English with no imported phrasing. The locale is held at the level of word choice and not merely spelling: "apartments", "phone reports" rather than "telephone reports", "staff members", "December 4, 2025", the serial comma in "No cost, resident-satisfaction, or completion-time measurements". Sentence rhythm varies, and the semicolon in "The trial has not yet expanded beyond them; Elm Quay will decide that after checking…" is used the way an American desk uses one, to bind two related states rather than to avoid a full stop.

The prose is at its best where the material is most dangerous. "What the note records is time to assignment, not time to completion" is a cleft that puts the distinction where the reader cannot miss it, in native syntax and without a hedge. "Thirty-one reports were entered, and the internal trial note is careful about what they prove" makes caution sound like a virtue rather than a disclaimer, which is the hardest register in the piece to find.

Two places where the sentence is looser than the rest. "how the work divided" uses *divide* intransitively without naming what it divided between; "how the work divided between them" is the natural completion, and a reader supplies it without stopping, but it is a beat short. And "Its supervisor would do it again" puts a possessive next to the wrong noun, as noted above — a cohesion slip, not a foreign construction.

## 7. Stop or delivery

**A valid delivery.** The draft earns it on the two things this material makes hardest.

The first is what a supplier-published case is tempted to leave out. This one leaves out nothing: the absence of a supplier comparison is stated in the material's own words rather than glossed, the workload difference and the note's explicit refusal to credit the software sit beside the two-day figure instead of three sections away from it, assignment is separated from completion in its own sentence, all three missing measurements are named, the non-recommendation is on the page, and the disclosure carries all three of its parts — who publishes, that they approved it, and that it is not independent reporting. Every one of those is a sentence that costs the publisher something, and every one of them is there.

The second is arrangement. The reservation precedes the endorsement; the disclosure precedes the call to action and sits in its own paragraph; the headline is in the past tense and the scope is on it. A reader who reads only the headline and standfirst is not misled, and a reader who reads to the end is told whose account they have been reading before they are offered the link.

What remains is small and honestly reported: a present-tense sentence in the closing section that rests on a single present-tense quotation of unknown date, a possessive with the wrong nearest noun in the standfirst, and a call to action that suggests when to read a document whose contents nobody has described. None of these needs a stop, and none of them would embarrass the piece in print.
