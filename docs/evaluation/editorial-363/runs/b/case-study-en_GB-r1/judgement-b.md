## 2. F1 — **fail**

The body is clean. All three failures are in the standfirst, and one of them is substantive.

1. **"Supervisor Maya Lind says what came of it, what the figures do and do not show, and where she would spend more time next time."** — *Changed subject / whose assertion.* All three complements hang off "Lind says". The material supplies her "complete usable quotations" and says they are complete; none of the three touches the count, the medians, the workloads or the exclusions. The material gives the reading of the figures to the document — "the note explicitly does not attribute the difference to the software" — and states of her closing remark: "Her final quotation is her actual, qualified assessment. It is not an inference from the figures." The standfirst therefore attributes to a named person an act of commentary the material expressly removes from her. The draft's own body confirms the misfit: the figures section quotes her nowhere. This is an unsupported attribution, not a stylistic loosening.
2. **"decided on its own to trial"** — *Unsupported manner/circumstantial claim.* Source: "its own maintenance team decided to trial a shared repair log in two buildings." There "own" marks the team as in-house, as against an outside party. The draft moves it onto the manner of the decision, asserting that nobody prompted it. The material is silent on whose idea the trial was; a supplier suggestion followed by an in-house decision is compatible with every supplied sentence. Minor but real.
3. **"to trial a shared repair log for eight weeks"** — *Modality/scope.* Source: "The trial lasted eight weeks", an elapsed duration. Inside "decided … to trial … for eight weeks" it becomes a term fixed at the outset. A trial begun open-endedly and stopped after eight weeks satisfies the source and not the draft. Minor.

Checked and found supported, for the record: the two-buildings scope in the headline; the undated supplier choice ("It chose Svale Systems…" carries no date in the draft either); "explicitly" retained; the workload difference stated in the material's own voice rather than as the note's claim; assignment time never turned into completion time; the absence of cost, satisfaction and completion-time measurements stated as absence of measurements, not as unknowns; the reservation surviving in the heading, in the closing quotation and in "She does not recommend Svale Systems to every housing company"; supplier narration in the third person throughout; the link described only as a document to read, at the exact supplied URL. "An internal note … counts 31 repair reports entered over the eight weeks" binds the count to the trial period where the material attaches no window; the note is the trial note and its exclusions are trial-relative, so this is defensible, not a finding. The present tense in "She does not recommend" renders a past report as a standing position; the material calls it "her actual, qualified assessment", which carries it.

No forbidden inference appears: nothing says or hedges that the software shortened anything, saved money or pleased residents, and the customer is never the rescued party.

## 7. Stop or delivery

**A delivery that should not have happened in this form.** The text was delivered carrying a defect the run itself had identified, stated plainly, and supplied a one-clause repair for: the standfirst tells the reader that Maya Lind says what the figures do and do not show, when the material's complete quotation set has her saying nothing of the kind and expressly detaches her assessment from the figures. That is exactly the kind of misattribution a supplier-published case study cannot afford, and it sits in the most-read sentence after the headline.

The disclosure is unusually good — the passages are named, the allegations quoted, the repairs given, and the reply says outright that it asserts no fidelity for those three passages. But disclosure in a covering note does not travel with the article; the standfirst does. Either the repair should have been applied and a further comparison accepted, or the run should have stopped short of delivery and handed back the three passages for settlement. Delivering an article whose second sentence misattributes commentary to a named interviewee is not a defensible outcome, however honestly it is flagged.
