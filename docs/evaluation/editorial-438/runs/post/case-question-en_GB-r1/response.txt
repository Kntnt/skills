# Fenwick keeps its bench schedule for new jobs

A brass and woodwind repair workshop began a six-week trial of a shared job schedule. Its trial summary and its manager's emailed answers show what staff had to agree first, what the trial established and why the backlog was held back.

By Thomas

Fenwick Instrument Repairs services brass and woodwind instruments, and used to list jobs awaiting parts and jobs ready for work on separate paper sheets. In January 2026 its team began a six-week trial of a shared bench schedule, choosing Benchline after checking that its software could tell those two statuses apart.

## Fenwick drew up its statuses; collection calls carried on

Benchline configured the statuses the workshop had defined, along with its choice of who would update them, and trained four staff in one online session. The team still discussed collection arrangements with customers by telephone.

Asked by email on 2 March 2026 what the team had to settle before the schedule was useful, workshop manager Priya Vale replied: ‘We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.’

## Trial summary leaves repair speed unmeasured

The workshop's trial summary, dated 23 February 2026, records 38 jobs entered in the schedule and says staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts. It does not measure repair duration, revenue or customer satisfaction.

Vale approved continued use for new jobs but postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions.

## Priya Vale's verdict on the trial

Vale was also asked: ‘Would you make the same choice again, and what would you change?’ The reply: ‘I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.’

A workshop weighing a shared schedule might begin with two things Fenwick decided for itself: what each status means, and who updates it.
