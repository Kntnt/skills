# Fenwick reports staff could see instruments awaiting parts

At Fenwick Instrument Repairs, a six-week trial of Benchline's shared bench schedule began in January 2026. Here is what the team had to agree first, what the trial summary did not measure and what its manager would change.

By Thomas

Fenwick Instrument Repairs services brass and woodwind instruments. Jobs awaiting parts and jobs ready for work once appeared on separate paper sheets. In January 2026 the team began a six-week trial of a shared bench schedule, using statuses the workshop defined itself.

## Fenwick defined the statuses, Benchline configured them

Before choosing Benchline, the team checked that it could tell those two statuses apart. The workshop defined the statuses and decided who would update them; Benchline configured those choices and trained four staff in one online session. Collection arrangements were still discussed with customers by telephone.

Asked by Benchline's case writer, by email on 2 March 2026, what the team had to settle before the schedule was useful, workshop manager Priya Vale replied: 'We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.'

## The summary leaves repair speed unmeasured

The workshop's trial summary of 23 February 2026 records 38 jobs in the schedule and says staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts. It does not measure repair duration, revenue or customer satisfaction.

The manager approved continued use for new jobs but postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions.

## Vale looks back on the trial

Asked in the same exchange, 'Would you make the same choice again, and what would you change?', Vale wrote: 'I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.'

Other managers can put the question Fenwick had to settle to their own teams: what does ready for work mean?
