# Fenwick reports staff could see jobs awaiting parts

Fenwick Instrument Repairs put a Benchline bench schedule on trial. Here is what the team had to agree first, what the trial established and what manager Priya Vale would change.

By Thomas

Jobs awaiting parts and jobs ready for work used to sit on separate paper sheets at Fenwick Instrument Repairs, which services brass and woodwind instruments. In January 2026 the team began a six-week trial of a shared bench schedule, choosing Benchline after checking that it could tell the two statuses apart. The workshop's trial summary and manager Priya Vale's emailed answers record how it went.

## Fenwick set the rules and Benchline configured them

Benchline trained four staff in one online session. It configured the statuses the workshop had defined, and the workshop decided who would update them. Collection arrangements were still discussed with customers by telephone.

Asked by Benchline's case writer what had to be settled before the schedule was useful, Vale wrote: ‘We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.’

## Summary covers what staff could see, not repair speed

The workshop's trial summary, dated 23 February 2026, records 38 jobs entered in the schedule. It says staff could see which instruments were waiting for parts, but it does not measure repair duration, revenue or customer satisfaction.

Vale approved continued use for new jobs but postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions.

## Vale looks back on the trial

Asked whether the choice would be the same again, and what would change, Vale replied: ‘I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.’

A workshop weighing a shared schedule can start with the question Fenwick's team had to answer: what ready for work means on its benches.
