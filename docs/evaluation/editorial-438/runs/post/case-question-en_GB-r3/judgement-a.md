## 2. F1 (source fidelity): fail, narrowly

Most of the draft follows the material closely. The no-measurement caveat is kept and stated plainly ("it does not measure repair duration, revenue or customer satisfaction"). The headline is hedged as the workshop's report ("Fenwick reports staff could see..."), not written as a fact about the schedule. Both quotations are verbatim. The division of labour (the workshop defined the statuses and who updates them; Benchline configured them and trained four staff) is correct. So are the dates (January 2026, 23 February 2026), the count (38 jobs) and the decision about the backlog.

Defects:

- **Invented attribution in the byline: "By Thomas".** The brief says "No author name … is supplied". The material names only an unnamed "Benchline's case writer". The byline attributes authorship to a person the material never mentions. The reply discloses this, but the published text still carries it.
- **Added chronology in the standfirst: "what the team had to agree first".** The source says only that the team had to settle this "before the schedule was useful". "First" suggests the agreement came before anything else, before the trial began. The material points the other way. The manager postponed the backlog "until the team had reviewed its status definitions", and Vale would "allow another week to check the status names". So the settling was still going on during and after the trial. This is a small change of timing, but it is not supported.
- **Borderline: the ending edges towards a general recommendation.** "A workshop weighing a shared schedule can start with the question Fenwick's team had to answer: what ready for work means on its benches." This is in the publisher's voice and hedged with "can". But it turns one workshop's reported experience into advice for other workshops. The material says the sources establish "not … a recommendation for every workshop". This does not fail on its own, because it recommends a question to ask, not the product. But it leans against a stated limit.

Terms checked in both directions:

- **"jobs awaiting parts" / "instruments waiting for parts":** the same thing in this context.
- **"the two statuses" / "these statuses":** faithful.
- **Heading "Fenwick set the rules":** "rules" is slightly wider than "defined the statuses and decided who would update them". A reader could imagine workflow rules beyond status definitions. This is acceptable in a heading, and the body states the exact scope.
- **"Collection arrangements were still discussed with customers by telephone":** the passive voice drops "the team" as the actor. It changes no fact.
- **Unknown kept apart from absent:** the draft does not claim the trial ran its full six weeks. It says the team "began a six-week trial". It adds no visit, spoken exchange or emotion.

## 3. G2 (structure) and L1 (language)

**G2: pass.** The parts come in the required order: headline, standfirst, byline, lead, three sections, ending.

- **Situation:** the paper sheets and what kind of workshop Fenwick is, in the lead.
- **Action:** the trial, the choice of Benchline, the statuses the workshop defined, the training.
- **Results:** 38 jobs recorded and staff could see what was waiting for parts, framed truthfully as a report with nothing measured.
- **Appraisal:** approval for new jobs, the backlog postponed, Vale's two quotations.

The customer stays the acting party: Fenwick defines, decides, approves and postpones, while Benchline configures and trains.

Weaknesses:

- **The standfirst is signposting** ("Here is what the team had to agree…, what the trial established and what … Vale would change"). It lists the contents instead of saying something, so it does its job only thinly.
- **The publisher's stance is only implied** through "Benchline's case writer". It is not untrue, but a reader is never told plainly that Benchline published the piece.
- **There is no call to action.** None could be built, because the brief supplies no offers, links or contact routes. The ending is a reflective close, not an invented offer. That is the right choice when nothing was supplied.

**L1: pass.** The prose is idiomatic British English: "agree" used without "on", single quotation marks, "used to sit on separate paper sheets", "record how it went". Minor points:

- "put a Benchline bench schedule on trial" brings the courtroom sense of "on trial" to mind. "Trialled a Benchline bench schedule" would be the natural British idiom.
- "Asked whether the choice would be the same again, and what would change" is stiff. The reader gets through it, but it reads as a converted question.

Neither point makes the prose sound translated.

## 4. Bridges into quotations

1. The bridge is "Asked by Benchline's case writer what had to be settled before the schedule was useful, Vale wrote:". The quotation is "‘We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.’"
   - **Class (b).** "Asked by Benchline's case writer", "what had to be settled before the schedule was useful" and "Vale wrote" give the attribution, the occasion (the question) and the subject. They do not give the answer.
   - **Second reading, (c):** "Benchline's case writer" and "before the schedule was useful" are facts the quotation does not carry. I chose (b) because these facts are the attribution and the occasion themselves, which (b) covers. They tell the reader nothing about what the quotation says.
2. The bridge is "Asked whether the choice would be the same again, and what would change, Vale replied:". The quotation is "‘I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.’"
   - **Class (b).** "Asked whether the choice would be the same again, and what would change" and "Vale replied" name the question and the speaker, not the answer.
   - **Observation, not a bridge:** the last paragraph of the previous section says "Vale approved continued use for new jobs but postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions." This largely anticipates the quotation, so the quotation lands partly as a repeat. A heading stands between that paragraph and the quotation, so it is not the quotation's bridge, and the quotation still adds "another week".
3. No quotation stands without a bridge.

Summary:

- **Counts:** class (a) 0, class (b) 2, class (c) 0.
- **Interviewer:** the draft invents no interviewer question or utterance. Both questions are faithful paraphrases of the two questions in the supplied extract, attributed to "Benchline's case writer" as the material does.
- **Unsupported bridge content:** none. "Wrote" and "replied" fit an email interview, and each paraphrased question matches its source.

## 4b. Subheadings over quotations

1. The subheading is "Fenwick set the rules and Benchline configured them". The quotation is "‘We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.’"
   - **Class: prepares.** "Fenwick set the rules" names the subject the quotation speaks to: defining the statuses.
   - **Second reading, pre-echo:** "set the rules" could be read as "had to agree what ready for work meant" said in advance. I chose prepares because the quotation's point does not appear in the subheading. That point is why the agreement was needed: a part in the building is not a part at the right bench. The subheading's other half ("Benchline configured them") is about something else.
2. The subheading is "Vale looks back on the trial". The quotation is "‘I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.’"
   - **Class: prepares.** "Vale looks back" names the speaker and the occasion (looking back), not the verdict or the change she would make.
   - **Second reading, neutral:** it says nothing about what she concludes. I chose prepares because it does name the speaker and the occasion.

**Counts:** pre-echo 0, prepares 2, neutral 0.
