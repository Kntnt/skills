## 2. F1: pass, with minor imprecisions

Almost every assertion traces to the material. This includes the six-week trial begun in January 2026, the separate paper sheets, choosing Benchline after checking the statuses, and the split between the workshop defining statuses and Benchline configuring them. It also includes training four staff in one online session, telephone for collection arrangements, the summary date of 23 February 2026, the 38 jobs, the three measures the summary does not take, the manager's approval for new jobs, and the backlog postponed pending review of the definitions. The two quotations are verbatim. "Asked by email" and "wrote" keep the medium right, and nothing adds a scene, a voice or a reaction. The headline attributes the finding to Fenwick ("Fenwick reports seeing"). It does not state it as a measured fact.

Points short of failure:

- **"This is one team's reported experience, not a measured result."** The source's caveat is narrower: "not a measured improvement in repair speed". Something falls under the draft's term but not the source's. The summary does record a count, 38 jobs, and the draft itself says "It records 38 jobs entered" under the heading "The summary counts jobs". Read as "no measured outcome", the sentence is true, because repair duration, revenue and satisfaction were all unmeasured. Read literally, it is broader than the source and sits uneasily beside the draft's own count. This is an imprecision in the cautious direction, not an invented claim.
- **Dropped caveat: "or a recommendation for every workshop".** The draft drops this half of the source's closing limit. It then ends with general advice: "A manager weighing a shared schedule might begin with what Fenwick had to settle: agreeing what each status means at their own benches." The advice is hedged ("might"), does not recommend the product, and rests on Vale's own lesson. So it does not become the recommendation the source rules out. It still extends one workshop's lesson to readers at large, and the caveat that fenced this off is no longer there.
- **"Would Vale make the same choice again, and change anything?"** The source's question is "and what would you change?", which presupposes a change. "Change anything" makes it a yes/no question. Vale's answer names a change, so the reader is not misled about her answer. Only the question's framing moves.
- **"By Thomas".** The brief says no author name is supplied. The name comes from the run environment and is disclosed in the reply. It is not a claim about the customer's story, and no interviewer is named, so the byline is not linked to the email interview. It belongs under G2 more than F1.
- **Standfirst, "put jobs awaiting parts and jobs ready for work on one shared schedule".** This is inferred, not stated, but it follows directly from "previously … appeared on separate paper sheets" together with the status check. Supported.

Unknown and absent are kept apart. The draft never implies a visit, and it says what the summary does not measure rather than suggesting a result.

## 4. Bridges into quotations

1. The bridge "Asked by email what the team had to settle before the schedule was useful, workshop manager Priya Vale wrote:" introduces the quotation ‘We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.’ **Class (b).** The words that put it there are "Asked by email" (the occasion), "what the team had to settle before the schedule was useful" (the subject, restating the question) and "workshop manager Priya Vale wrote" (the attribution). A second reading is (c), since "by email" and "workshop manager" are facts the quotation does not carry. I chose (b) because both are occasion and attribution, which (b) covers, and the bridge does not give the answer, ready for work and the right bench.
2. The bridge "Would Vale make the same choice again, and change anything?" introduces the quotation ‘I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.’ **Class (b).** The words that put it there are "make the same choice again, and change anything": they name the subject the answer addresses, not the answer. A second reading takes the lead-in to be the whole run-up, including the sentence before it, "Vale approved continued use for new jobs but postponed adding the backlog until the team had reviewed its status definitions". That sentence already says most of what the quotation says, which leans to (a). I chose (b) because the bridge as defined is the sentence immediately before the quotation, which is the question. The quotation also still adds something: "another week" and the retrospective "would".

No quotation stands without a bridge.

- **Counts:** (a) 0, (b) 2, (c) 0.
- **Interviewer:** the draft invents no interviewer question or utterance. Its one reported question, "Would Vale make the same choice again, and change anything?", paraphrases the supplied question but softens "what would you change" into "change anything". It is not attributed to anyone.
- **Unsupported assertions:** no bridge asserts anything the material does not support.

## 4b. Subheadings over quotations

1. The subheading "## The workshop set the rules and Benchline configured them" sits over ‘We had to agree what ready for work meant. A part being in the building did not always mean that it had reached the right bench.’ **Prepares.** "set the rules" names the subject, the workshop settling its own definitions. It does not give the quotation's point, that "ready for work" needed agreeing because a part on site was not always at the bench. A second reading is pre-echo, taking "set the rules" as "had to agree what ready for work meant". I chose prepares because the heading is about who did what (the workshop versus Benchline), which the quotation does not address. It does not state the difficulty the quotation reveals.
2. The subheading "## Fenwick's manager settles the next step" sits over ‘I would use it again for new jobs. I would allow another week to check the status names before adding the backlog.’ **Prepares.** "Fenwick's manager" names the speaker and "the next step" the subject. It does not say what the step is. A second reading is neutral. I rejected it because "the next step" does point to the domain the quotation answers.

- **Counts:** pre-echo 0, prepares 2, neutral 0.
