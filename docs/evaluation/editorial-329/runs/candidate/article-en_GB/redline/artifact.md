# What four weeks of school temperature readings can tell you

A short measurement trial can help municipal property managers identify when classroom temperatures need closer attention. The fictional Björkskolan trial shows both the value and the limits of that evidence: readings flag particular lessons, but cannot establish why a room was cold or how pupils felt.

In January 2026, Lervik’s municipal property office installed temperature sensors in six classrooms at Björkskolan. It wanted to find out whether complaints about cold air coincided with low temperatures during lessons. The sensors recorded a reading every five minutes for four weeks.

## A reading below 20°C is only part of the picture

The office’s report, *Mätförsök i Björkskolan*, dated 12 March 2026, records at least one reading below 20°C in 14 of the 120 lesson periods monitored. It does not say how long temperatures remained below that level or whether pupils felt cold.

The 20°C threshold was the office’s own working limit for the trial. It was not presented as a legal requirement. Nor does the report provide a benchmark for judging whether 14 out of 120 is a high or low count.

Reporting by lesson period helps keep attention on the time that matters to the enquiry. A whole-day average can conceal temperature variations during lessons. Even so, counting lessons with a reading below the threshold does not establish how persistent those conditions were.

## Air temperature does not describe the whole room

A sensor measures temperature where it is positioned. Two sensors were near external walls and four were on internal walls. Their positions were documented, but the trial did not experimentally compare placements, so it cannot establish what difference those positions made.

There is also a distinction between air temperature and the combined effect of air temperature and heat radiating from surrounding surfaces. The latter is captured by a measure called operative temperature. This trial measured air temperature alone.

Ventilation, draughts and perceived temperature were not measured. The findings cover only the six classrooms and four weeks observed; they do not describe conditions throughout the school or across the year.

## Connect the readings to classroom use

After the trial, operations technician Elin Rask reviewed the ventilation and heating schedules with the school caretaker. No effects of any adjustments have yet been measured.

‘We know when we need to take a closer look. We don’t yet know why it got cold at those particular times,’ Rask said.

A further trial is planned for November, with sensors in equivalent positions and notes on when rooms are used. Funding has not been decided. For managers considering changes to heating controls, Rask’s recommendation gives a practical starting point: connect the sequence of temperature readings to room-use times before changing the controls.
