# What four weeks of school temperature readings can tell you

A short temperature trial can help council property managers identify when to investigate complaints about cold classrooms. At Björkskolan, readings flagged lessons for closer attention, but left the cause unexplained. Understanding what was measured—and what was missing—is essential before using the results to change heating controls.

In January 2026, Lervik’s property office installed temperature sensors in six classrooms at Björkskolan. It wanted to find out whether complaints about cold air coincided with low temperatures during lessons. The sensors recorded readings every five minutes for four weeks.

## A lesson count does not show duration

The office’s report, *Mätförsök i Björkskolan*, dated 12 March 2026, records at least one reading below 20°C in 14 of the 120 recorded lessons. That threshold was the office’s own working limit for the trial; it was not presented as a legal requirement.

The count identifies lessons containing a reading below the threshold. It does not say how long temperatures remained below it, or whether pupils felt cold. The findings cover only those six rooms and four weeks.

Reporting by lesson keeps attention on teaching time. An average for the whole day can hide variations during lessons, which is why the report uses lesson counts instead of a single daily average. Even so, those counts cannot distinguish a brief dip from a longer period below the threshold.

## The reading belongs to the sensor’s location

A sensor measures the temperature where it sits. Two sensors were near external walls and four were on internal walls. Their positions were documented, but the trial did not experimentally compare the effects of placement. The results therefore do not establish what difference those positions made.

Air temperature also captures only part of the thermal conditions. A measure that accounts for both air temperature and heat radiating from surrounding surfaces is called *operative temperature*. This trial measured air temperature alone.

Ventilation, draughts and perceived temperature were not measured. Those gaps leave the trial unable to explain why the cold occurred or establish how the rooms felt to their occupants.

## Link readings to room use before changing controls

After the trial, operations technician Elin Rask reviewed the ventilation and heating schedules with the school caretaker. The effect of any adjustments has yet to be measured.

‘We know when we need to look more closely. We don’t yet know why it got cold at those particular times,’ Rask said.

She recommends linking temperature readings over time to room use before changing the controls. A further trial is planned for November, with sensors positioned in the same way and notes on when rooms are used, although funding remains undecided. For property managers, the supported next step is to connect the readings to occupancy before deciding what to change.
