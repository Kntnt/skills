# What a short temperature trial can tell you about a school

Four weeks of classroom readings can identify times that need closer investigation without explaining why it was cold. A trial covering six rooms at Björkskolan illustrates the distinction: its results record air temperatures during lessons, but do not establish how pupils felt or what caused the readings.

## Start with what was measured

In this fictional example, Lervik’s municipal property office installed temperature sensors in six classrooms in January 2026. It wanted to find out whether complaints about cold air coincided with low temperatures during lessons. The sensors recorded a reading every five minutes for four weeks.

A sensor measures the temperature where it is placed. Two sensors were near external walls and four were on internal walls. Their positions were documented, but the trial did not experimentally compare those positions.

The trial measured air temperature only. A different measure, operative temperature, takes account of both air temperature and heat radiation from surrounding surfaces. Ventilation, draughts and perceived temperature were not measured.

## Read the lesson count carefully

The report, *Mätförsök i Björkskolan*, dated 12 March 2026, states that 14 of the 120 recorded lesson periods included at least one reading below 20°C. That is a count of lessons containing a reading below the threshold, not a measure of how long the temperature stayed below it. The report does not give that duration or say whether pupils felt cold.

The property office chose 20°C as its own working threshold for the trial. It was not presented as a legal requirement. Nor does the material provide a benchmark for judging whether 14 out of 120 is high or low.

A whole-day average can hide temperature variation during lessons. The report therefore presents lesson periods instead of a single daily average. Its findings cover only the six rooms and four weeks measured.

## Connect readings to room use

After the trial, operations technician Elin Rask reviewed the ventilation and heating schedules with the school caretaker. The effects of any adjustments have yet to be measured.

‘We know when we need to take a closer look. We don’t yet know why it got cold at those particular times,’ Rask said.

A further trial is planned for November, using sensors in the same positions and notes on when the rooms are occupied. Funding has not been decided.

For property managers considering changes to heating controls, Rask recommends first linking the sequence of temperature readings to the times when rooms are used. The trial identifies when to investigate further; the cause remains unresolved.
