# What four weeks of classroom temperature readings can tell you

A short monitoring trial can show when classroom temperatures need closer attention. At Björkskolan, readings identified lessons with air temperatures below the property department’s chosen threshold. They did not establish why temperatures fell or whether pupils felt cold.

## Start with the question

In January 2026, the fictional municipality of Lervik’s property department installed temperature sensors in six classrooms at Björkskolan. It wanted to find out whether complaints about cold air coincided with low temperatures during lessons. The sensors recorded readings every five minutes for four weeks.

The department’s report, *Mätförsök i Björkskolan*, dated 12 March 2026, states that 14 of the 120 recorded lessons included at least one reading below 20°C. That figure counts lessons containing a reading below the threshold; it does not say how long temperatures remained below it.

The 20°C threshold was the department’s own working threshold for the trial. It was not presented as a legal requirement. The findings cover only those six classrooms and those four weeks.

## Know what the sensor measures

A temperature sensor measures the temperature where it is placed. Two sensors were near external walls and four were on internal walls. Their positions were documented, but the trial did not experimentally compare the effects of placement.

Air temperature is also only part of the picture. Operative temperature is a measure that takes account of both air temperature and heat radiating from surrounding surfaces. This trial measured air temperature alone. It did not measure ventilation, draughts or how warm or cold the rooms felt, and the report does not establish whether pupils felt cold.

For a property manager, those limits matter when interpreting a complaint: the readings describe air temperature at the sensors, while the pupils’ experience remains unmeasured.

## Look at lessons, then investigate

An average for the whole day can hide temperature variations during lessons. The report therefore presents its findings by lesson rather than as a single daily average, keeping the measurements tied to the periods the department wanted to examine.

After the trial, building services technician Elin Rask reviewed the ventilation and heating schedules with the school caretaker. The effects of any adjustments have not yet been measured.

‘We know when we need to look more closely. We don’t yet know why it got cold at those times,’ Rask said.

A further trial is planned for November, with sensors in equivalent positions and notes on when the rooms are used. Funding has not been decided. Rask’s recommendation gives property managers a practical starting point: match the temperature readings over time to room use before changing the controls.
