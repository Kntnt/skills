# What a channel costs, and where a plan name comes from

[ADR-0182](0182-how-a-model-is-chosen-and-how-the-choice-is-measured.md) settled that one number orders the frontier and that the number is what the tokens are worth. It left unsettled where that number comes from when the user does not reach the provider on the provider's own terms, and it left the vocabulary for *how do you pay for this* as hand-written prose in the interview that asks the question. Both were found the same day, on the machine this collection is maintained from, by the maintainer answering his own interview. What the rules now are is stated in [`docs/rules/routing.md`](../rules/routing.md) and in the Skill's own shipped files; this record argues.

## What was found

**The tiers a user could answer with were a table somebody typed.** Three rows of it, in a reference file, in a Skill whose own first rule is *never ask for a fact the catalogue holds*. Two of the three could not express the answer the maintainer had. The Anthropic row offered `Max 5x` and `Max 20x` with no product name against either; the OpenAI row named the consumer plans and carried no usage multiplier at all, so `ChatGPT Pro 20x` — what he actually pays for — was not offerable and was typed as free text. The same page then contradicted itself three ways about how a plan is even spelled: `Max 20x` in the table, `"plan": "Claude", "tier": "Max 20x"` in the example ten lines below it, and `"plan": "Claude Max", "tier": "20x"` in what the interview actually wrote to disk. Two profiles written a week apart would disagree about one subscription.

**A channel had nowhere to record what its tokens cost.** The interview's own words were that an API channel *means a rate card, which the catalogue already holds per model and token category*. The catalogue holds one list price per model, from the provider's own page. It is not the bill for a gateway. The maintainer reaches Grok through OpenRouter at effective rates of USD 0.9915 in and 6.174 out per million tokens; the catalogue carries 2.00 in and 6.00 out from `docs.x.ai`. The ratio alone settles it without any currency question: the catalogue's input-to-output is 1:3 and his is 1:6.2, so the *shape* of the cost was wrong, not merely its scale. Coding work is weighted heavily toward input and cache reads, so Grok came out systematically too expensive and was recommended too rarely. Rather than record it wrongly, he left the provider out of his profile altogether — and a provider left out is a provider never recommended, which is the same outcome the wrong number would have produced, reached honestly.

**Two fields were collected and read by nothing.** `monthly` and `currency` were written into every subscription channel, read back by the loader, and consumed by no decision anywhere: there is no renewal verb, and ADR-0182's promise that *what was actually paid is recorded, the renewal question needing it* was a promise about a verb that was never built. The interview also called the monthly price *a fetched fact* shown for correction. Nothing fetched it. One of the two figures on this machine had to be taken from a retired profile and the other asked for outright.

## The plans are world facts, and they live with the prices

**A provider's subscriptions go in the catalogue, carrying `source_url` and `retrieved` exactly as a price does.** They are the same kind of fact, they go stale for the same reason — a provider renames a plan or adds a multiplier — and they are refreshed by the same verb. Prose in a reference file cannot be refreshed, cannot say where it came from, and drifts silently; that is the whole of the argument, and it is the Skill's own stated rule applied to a fact it had exempted itself from.

**A refreshed provider replaces its whole list rather than merging entry by entry.** Models merge by id, because a model that existed still exists and a partial refresh should be additive. A plan is not like that: a provider retires one, and a merge by name would go on offering a retired plan to somebody choosing how they pay for as long as the Skill stayed installed. Offering a wrong list is the defect being fixed, so the merge rule is the one that can make an entry disappear.

**A plan is recorded under the whole name its provider markets it by, and there is no tier.** `Claude Max 20x` is one product name and `ChatGPT Pro 20x` is another. The split into a product and a level was a boundary neither provider draws, and every session drawing it drew it somewhere else. Removing the field removes the disagreement by construction, which is cheaper than a rule saying where to cut.

**A vocabulary longer than the asking tool can offer is asked whole, not trimmed.** The tool that puts a question to a person admits four options; Anthropic markets seven plans and OpenAI eight. The session that held this interview dropped Anthropic's free tier to fit, which is the same failure in miniature. Where the set does not fit, the complete list goes in the question itself and the answer is taken as typed.

**An answer that matched nothing offered is reported as such.** It arrives indistinguishable from a chosen one, which is how an interview loses the ability to notice that its own vocabulary fell short — the single fact that would have caught every defect above. It is still recorded: the person who saw the screen is the record of what they pay, and a catalogue that has fallen behind is not grounds for refusing an answer. What it earns is a note naming the verb that fixes the catalogue.

## A channel may carry its own rate card, and it wins whole

**Where the user has said what a channel charges, that is what the comparison is made on.** The catalogue's card is the provider's own and is right for a subscription seat and for a direct API channel; it is a different arrangement's bill for a gateway. So a channel carries an optional rate card, per token category, and it replaces the catalogue's card wherever it exists — in the ranking, and in the price written onto every measurement that ran through that channel. A store priced at a list price nobody paid measures somebody else's arrangement.

**It replaces the long-context card too.** Two vendors of three re-price a whole request past a context threshold, and ADR-0182 is right that ignoring the cliff under-prices the models this collection sends the most work to. But the threshold is a fact about the provider's own card. A gateway that quoted one rate quoted it for every size of request, and applying a cliff the user's source never mentioned would be this Skill inventing a price.

**What a subscription costs per month is no longer recorded at all.** The catalogue holds the list price, attributed and dated, and the interview shows it. Wiring the collected figure to something was the alternative, and the something would have been a renewal verb nobody asked for. An interview that collects a number changing no answer spends the user's time for nothing, so it stops asking.

## One currency, and a card in another is refused

**Every figure is USD, and nothing anywhere converts.** The catalogue is USD, the arithmetic is USD, and the answers are USD. This was put to the maintainer as a choice between three arrangements — an exchange rate fetched and dated like any other world fact, one he sets himself in the profile, or none at all with rates asked for in USD — and he chose the third.

**What changes is that the refusal is now audible.** Before this, a profile could carry `"currency": "SEK"` beside a bare USD figure and nothing said a word; the arrangement was already unconverted, and only the silence was new. A rate card in another currency is now refused by name, by the writer as a problem and by the loader as a reason the profile fell back. An unconvertible number added to a dollar bill is the one kind of wrong answer a person reading the output cannot see, which is what puts it among the states that invalidate a profile rather than among the ones quietly dropped.
