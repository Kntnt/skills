# A run asks at plan time or not at all

Three `/orchestrate` runs were started to run overnight, and the sessions that drove them were interviewed afterwards. The stops that needed a human sort into three piles. A ticket labelled `ready-for-agent` whose own body said *the maintainer decides* surfaced its question hours into the run — a question that would have cost the developer a minute at the start cost the night instead, because by the time it was asked, nobody was there to answer it. Builders that hit a mechanical hinder — a path slightly wrong, a directory missing, a number already taken — stopped and reported, and the orchestrator, whose step read *a builder that stopped and reported: record it failed*, had no licence to fix the condition and try again, so a trivial hinder was recorded as if the ticket could not be built. And orchestrators asked the developer things between waves — whether to file findings, whether to finish a ticket, which of two scopes to take — turning a report into a conversation with somebody who had gone to bed. The developer's stated requirement is the opposite shape: start the run in the evening, read one report in the morning, and be interrupted for nothing a competent session could have handled.

**So every question a run needs a human for is asked before the first ticket is claimed, in one batch — and after that the developer is not addressed again until the report.** Three mechanisms carry it:

**At plan time, the orchestrator reads each frontier ticket for a decision its text leaves open, and asks all of it at once.** The label promises there is nothing to ask — a ticket carrying `ready-for-agent` is supposed to be finished thinking — but the label is a claim, and the evidence is that triage sometimes gets it wrong. A ticket whose body defers a value, names a choice as the maintainer's, or leaves two designs open is caught before anything is dispatched, while the developer who just typed the command is still there. Under `--yes` there is nobody to ask and the flag cannot answer an open question — `--yes` answers yes/no questions, and *which default?* is not one — so such a ticket is parked instead: the question is written on the ticket as a comment and its `ready-for-agent` label is replaced with `needs-info`, which is the tracker saying truthfully that the thinking is not finished. The standing rule that a run triages no ticket narrows exactly this far and no further: a run still creates no ticket and decides no ticket's substance, but returning a mislabeled ticket to the human loop, with the question that belongs on it, is not deciding it — it is the claim mechanism's honesty applied to the label.

**Mid-run, a mechanical hinder is the orchestrator's to fix, once.** A builder or verifier that stops on a condition of the environment rather than a question of the requirement — the wrong path, the missing directory, the tool that needed one flag — has not found a decision; it has found a chore. The orchestrator repairs the condition and dispatches that subagent's brief once more. Once, for the same reason the amend and the rebuild are bounded to one (ADR-0069, ADR-0055): a hinder that survives its repair is recorded rather than retried through the night. What separates a hinder from a decision is whether fixing it decides anything about the work: a condition anybody would repair the same way is a hinder, and anything else is a decision.

**Mid-run, a genuine decision parks the ticket and the night continues.** The builder's brief already says to stop rather than guess; what changes is what the orchestrator does with that stop. It is not a failure — nothing about the work has been tried and found wanting — so it is not recorded as one. The ticket is parked exactly as at plan time: the question on the ticket, the label returned to `needs-info`, the claim released, and the wave carries on without it. The morning report names it with its question. The one stop that remains a stop is the one that was always correct: an integrated branch whose verification fails, where continuing would spend the night building on broken code.

**Between waves the orchestrator acts; at the end it reports once, leading with what is done and what the developer must do.** A question in the middle of a report — *do you want these as tickets?* — is the plan-time rule broken at the far end. What a run has standing to do on its own account, it does; what it does not, it states as the report's *for you* list rather than as questions awaiting answers.

**What this costs.** The batch of questions makes starting a run a minute longer on the nights there is something to ask. A parked ticket costs its night even where the orchestrator misread an open decision into settled text — but the wrongly parked ticket is a comment and a label swap to bring back, while the wrongly guessed requirement is, in the words already in the brief, the trust in every ticket the run reports done. And the one-repair bound means a flaky environment can still cost a ticket; what it cannot do any longer is cost the run.
