# American English

American English. Bare English resolves to British English, so this resource is reached by naming it.

## Composition

American general-interest prose is direct and front-loaded. The point comes first, sentences are short, the voice is active, and concrete nouns do the work that a British writer might give to an understated construction.

Collective nouns take singular agreement: *the team is meeting*, *the committee has decided*, *the band is on tour*. The plural agreement of British usage reads as a foreign import here.

The simple past covers a recent past: *I just saw the figures*, *did you eat?* The present perfect is available but is not the default in those positions.

*Gotten* is the standard past participle in the sense of acquiring or becoming: *it's gotten worse*, *we've gotten three replies*. *Got* is retained for possession: *I've got it*.

Prepositions: *on the weekend*, *in the hospital*, *different from* or *different than*, *write somebody*, *meet with somebody* in the sense of holding a meeting.

The serial comma is standard in most American house styles and omitted in news style. Pick per document and hold it.

The mandative subjunctive is alive in formal American prose and is not an archaism: *the board recommends that he be reinstated*, *it is essential that she attend*.

## Review

Read for British spellings and vocabulary that have drifted in: *colour*, *centre*, *organise*, *whilst*, *amongst*, *autumn* for fall, *lift* for elevator, *flat* for apartment.

Read for house-style drift within one document. Chicago and AP disagree on the serial comma, on title capitalization, on when numbers are spelled out, and on how times and states are abbreviated. A document that follows both has followed neither, and this is the most common structural defect in assembled American copy.

American business prose collapses into abstract nouns faster than most: *leverage*, *utilize*, *operationalize*, *impactful*, *actionable*, *learnings*, *bandwidth*, *alignment*. Ask in each case what somebody actually did, and write that.

Check headings for one capitalization system. Title case and sentence case both occur in American publishing; alternating between them within a document does not.

Watch for intensifier inflation — *incredibly*, *absolutely*, *literally*, *truly*, *hugely* — which American register tolerates more than British and which accumulates until nothing in the text is emphatic because everything is.

Check number style: whether numbers under ten are spelled out, whether percentages are written as digits, and whether the same rule holds across the whole document.

## Anti-slop

The reskinning tell in the other direction. Generated American English is often British English with the spelling changed, and the leftovers are formats and idiom rather than spelling: *color* and *organize* in a text that still writes *at the weekend*, *£*, *24 August*, and *Mr* without a period.

The corporate-American register a model defaults to when asked for American English: *leverage*, *unlock*, *supercharge*, *seamless*, *robust*, *best-in-class*, *move the needle*, *circle back*, *at the end of the day*, *double down*, *take it to the next level*. These arrive as a set rather than because any one of them was meant.

*Utilize* for *use*, *incentivize* for *encourage*, *operationalize* for *do*. The longer verb is not the more precise one, and its density is a tell rather than a register.

Em dash density. The unspaced em dash is correct American punctuation; what marks generated prose is one in every paragraph, used to attach an afterthought the sentence did not need — like this, repeatedly.

House-style inconsistency within a document — the serial comma present in one list and absent in the next, *p.m.* in one time and *pm* in another — is a generation artefact rather than a choice, and worth naming as such rather than silently normalizing, because it usually means the passage came from somewhere else.

The listicle reflex in running prose: every paragraph opening with an ordinal or a bolded label, and every idea presented as a numbered takeaway whether or not the argument has parts.

## Mechanics

Spelling: *color*, *behavior*, *favor*; *center*, *theater*, *meter*; single *l* before a suffix in *traveled*, *modeling*, *canceled*; *catalog*, *dialog* in the computing sense and *dialogue* otherwise; and *airplane*, *curb*, *tire*, *gray*, *aluminum*, *draft*, *plow*, *story* for a floor, *check*, *program*.

*-ize* and *-yze* throughout: *organize*, *recognize*, *analyze*, *paralyze*.

*License* and *practice* are spelled the same as noun and as verb; the British noun–verb distinction does not apply. *Defense*, *offense*, and *pretense* take *-se*.

Dates: *August 24, 2026*, with a comma after the year when the date sits inside a sentence — *on August 24, 2026, the board met*. Numeric form is *8/24/2026*.

Numbers: decimal point, comma as the thousands separator, currency symbol before the amount — *$1,250.00*, *1,000,000*. Percentages are written *50%* without a space, or *50 percent* in news style.

Times: *2:30 p.m.* in Chicago style, *2:30 pm* in others; one form per document.

Quotation marks: double outer and single inner. Commas and periods go inside the closing quotation mark whether or not they belong to the quoted matter; colons and semicolons go outside; question and exclamation marks follow the sense.

Abbreviations take periods where British usage omits them: *Mr.*, *Mrs.*, *Ms.*, *Dr.*, *St.* Initialisms are set without them: *FBI*, *NASA*. *US* and *U.S.* are both current; one per document.

Em dashes are set unspaced, *like—this*. En dashes take ranges: *pages 12–18*, *2020–2024*.

Where both forms are established — the serial comma present or absent, *toward* and *towards*, *gray* and *grey* — the text's own choice stands, and only inconsistency within one text is corrected.
