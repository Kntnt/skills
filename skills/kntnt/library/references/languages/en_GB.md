# British English

British English, and the resource bare English resolves to.

## Composition

British general-interest prose is understated. It states a strong claim plainly and lets the claim carry the emphasis, where American register would reach for an intensifier. Irony and litotes are available and are not read as evasion.

Collective nouns take plural agreement when the members are acting and singular when the body is acting as one: *the team are arguing about it*, *the committee has published its report*. Both are correct; what matters is that the choice is consistent within a passage.

The present perfect covers a recent past that still bears on now: *I've just seen the figures*, *have you eaten?* The simple past in those positions reads as American.

*Have got* is ordinary British usage for possession and obligation — *I've got the file*, *you've got to see this* — and is not an informality to be edited out of general prose.

Prepositions differ in a small, fixed set of places: *at the weekend*, *in hospital*, *different from* or *different to*, *write to somebody*, *meet somebody* rather than *meet with*. None of these is a preference; each is the British form.

Spelling is *-ise* or *-ize* by house style — both are correct British English, *-ise* being the majority convention and *-ize* the Oxford one — but a document uses one throughout. The *-yse* group is invariant: *analyse*, *paralyse*, *catalyse*, in Oxford spelling as in any other.

Quotation marks are single as the outer mark in most British house styles, with double inside; some publications invert this. Follow the document's own choice and make it consistent.

## Review

Read for American vocabulary that has drifted in: *gotten*, *math*, *fall* for autumn, *elevator*, *sidewalk*, *movie theater*, *diaper*, *vacation*, *check* for a restaurant bill. A single one is a slip; a scatter of them means the text was drafted in another variety.

Read for mixed formats in one document — dates written both *24 August 2026* and *August 24, 2026*, currency in both *£* and *$*, times in both *2.30pm* and *2:30 p.m.* Format drift is the clearest evidence of assembly from several sources.

Check *-ise* and *-ize* consistency across the whole document, not paragraph by paragraph. Generated British English is frequently American English with some suffixes changed, and the ones that were missed are where it shows.

British institutional and business prose slides into passive nominal style: *It is anticipated that a review will be undertaken*. Give it back its subject and its verb: *We expect to review it*.

Watch for hedges in chains — *somewhat rather more likely*, *it may perhaps be the case that* — where British understatement has stopped being restraint and become evasion. One hedge does work; three cancel each other.

Check that punctuation inside and outside quotation marks follows one convention. British practice places a mark inside the quotation only when it belongs to the quoted matter.

Check title capitalisation is one system throughout. British house styles differ over whether headings take sentence case or title case, and a document that alternates has chosen neither.

## Anti-slop

The reskinning tell. Generated British English is very often American English with a spelling pass over it, so the vocabulary and the format are what give it away rather than the spelling: *colour* and *organisation* in a text that still writes *gotten*, *2:30 p.m.*, and *$*.

*Whilst*, *amongst*, *utilise*, *endeavour*, and *commence* sprinkled through ordinary prose. Each exists; none is the neutral British choice, and their density is a model reaching for something that sounds British rather than something a British writer would write. *While*, *among*, *use*, *try*, and *start* are the words.

Suffix inconsistency inside one text — *organise* in one paragraph and *recognize* in the next — is a generation artefact rather than a style, and it is worth naming as such rather than silently normalising, because it usually means the passage came from somewhere else.

Punctuation carried in from American house style: the unspaced em dash *—like this—* where most British styles use a spaced en dash – like this – and double quotation marks throughout in a document whose own convention is single.

The apologetic opening: *It is perhaps worth noting that*, *One might argue that*, *It would be fair to say that*. British hedging turned into an opening formula that says nothing before the sentence starts.

## Mechanics

Spelling: *-our* in *colour*, *behaviour*, *favour*; *-re* in *centre*, *theatre*, *metre*; doubled *l* before a suffix in *travelled*, *modelling*, *cancelled*; *-ogue* in *catalogue*, *dialogue*; and *aeroplane*, *kerb*, *tyre*, *grey*, *aluminium*, *draught*, *plough*, *storey*, *cheque*, *programme* except for the computing sense.

Noun and verb pairs are spelled differently: *licence* the noun and *license* the verb, *practice* the noun and *practise* the verb, *advice* the noun and *advise* the verb. The noun–verb distinction is not optional in British English.

Both *-ise* and *-ize* are correct; only inconsistency within one document is an error. *Analyse*, *paralyse*, and their relatives are always *-yse*.

Dates: *24 August 2026*, or *24/08/2026* in numeric form. No comma between month and year. Times: *2.30pm* or *14:30*; both current, and consistency is what is corrected.

Numbers: decimal point, comma as the thousands separator, currency symbol before the amount — *£1,250.00*, *1,000,000*. Percentages are written *50%* without a space.

Quotation marks: single outer and double inner, or the reverse where the document's own convention is the reverse. Punctuation belonging to the quoted matter goes inside the closing mark; punctuation belonging to the surrounding sentence goes outside.

Abbreviations that end in the last letter of the full word take no full stop: *Mr*, *Mrs*, *Ms*, *Dr*, *St*. Truncations do: *Prof.*, *Jan.*, *ed.* Initialisms are set without points: *BBC*, *UK*, *NHS*.

Collective agreement is variation and not error. *The team are* and *the team is* are both correct; correct only a text that alternates within one passage.

Where both forms are established — *judgment* and *judgement*, *inquiry* and *enquiry*, the serial comma present or absent — the text's own choice stands.
