# The base contract

Every first draft this Collection writes is written against this document, and every review of a draft is read against it. It holds the outcomes a text has to reach — the whole of them, so that nothing normative has to be stated a second time somewhere else. It says nothing about how a Skill is invoked and names no option, because what differs between writing and reviewing is what you do about a failure, never what counts as one.

The examples here are English because this document is. They are patterns rather than strings: apply each one by what it does, in the target language, rather than by the words it happens to use. A rule that is only true of one language does not belong here at all — it belongs in that language's own resource, beside the guidance the language needs.

Two documents sit under this one and are loaded with it. The selected genre says what this kind of text owes its reader on top of what follows; the resolved language's composition guidance says what writing well in that language actually takes. Where a genre asks for something this document does not, the genre is the more specific instruction and wins.

## Register

Write idiomatic contemporary prose in the target language, with the restraint, concreteness, factual precision, and natural cadence of a well-edited general-interest newspaper or quality magazine. That is a floor for clarity rather than a house style: every sentence carries its weight, every claim carries its support, and the prose reads as though written by somebody who speaks the language rather than translated into it.

**Genre, audience, and purpose override that baseline.** A letter is a letter, an advertisement is an advertisement, a speech is written for the ear, and a personal essay may be as plain or as ornate as its subject deserves. None of them comes out as a news article because the baseline was stated first. What survives every override is precision: a text may be warm, funny, angry, or severe, and it may never be vague about what it is claiming.

## The reader and the job

A text does one job for one reader. Before anything else is settled, both are: what this reader is supposed to know, believe, feel, or be able to do afterwards, and what they already have. A draft that would serve two incompatible readers equally badly is not finished, however clean its sentences are.

Everything the reader needs is present, and nothing else is. Material that is interesting but does not serve the job is cut, however hard it was to gather. Assumed knowledge the reader does not have is either supplied or the sentence needing it is rewritten.

## Structure

Order by the reader's need, not by the order in which the writer found things out. What the reader needs first goes first, and a text that reaches its point in the last paragraph has kept its reader waiting for no reason the reader can see.

One movement to a paragraph. A paragraph advances the argument, the narrative, or the description by one step, and its first sentence tells the reader which step it is. Two unrelated movements in one paragraph is two paragraphs; a single sentence standing alone is a paragraph only when the emphasis it takes is earned.

Every transition is real. Each paragraph follows from the one before it by something in the text rather than by adjacency, and a connective claims only the relation that actually holds: *therefore* where one thing caused another, *but* where something genuinely cuts against what precedes it. A connective decorating a sequence that has no such relation is worse than none.

## Sentences

Vary length and shape, and let the variation do work: a short sentence after long ones lands, and a run of sentences built to one pattern reads as machinery. Read a paragraph aloud in your head; where the rhythm is flat or the breath runs out, the sentences are the problem.

Prefer the active voice, and use the passive where the object genuinely is the subject of the sentence or the actor is genuinely unknown. Keep the subject close to its verb. Put the emphasis at the end, which is where a reader's attention rests.

State a thing once. A sentence that restates its predecessor in different words, a summary that repeats what the reader has just read, and a closing paragraph that says the opening again all spend the reader's attention on nothing.

## Words

Concrete beats abstract. A number, a date, a name, an amount, an example: these are what a reader remembers and what makes a claim checkable. *Boardings fell by a fifth over the autumn* beats *ridership experienced a significant decline*.

Verbs beat nominalisations. *The council decided* beats *the council's decision-making process resulted in*. A sentence whose main verb is a form of *to be* with the action hidden in a noun is a sentence with its engine removed.

Use the plain word where one exists and the technical word where the field actually uses it. Jargon that a reader of this text uses daily is precision; jargon imported to sound serious is fog.

Cut intensifiers that add no information — *very*, *really*, *truly*, *incredibly*, *deeply* — and the throat-clearing that opens a sentence without saying anything: *it is important to note that*, *when it comes to*, *at the end of the day*.

Keep one name for one thing. Cycling through synonyms for a term the text has already established makes a reader wonder whether a new thing has been introduced. Repetition of a key term is not a fault; it is how a reader knows it is the same term.

## Claims

Every claim is supported by what the text or its material actually holds, and the strength of the wording matches the strength of the support. Evidence for a tendency does not license a law; one case does not license *always*; a correlation is not written as a cause.

Causality requires causal support. A sequence or correlation stays a sequence or correlation unless the material attributes one event to the other. A causal verb such as *caused*, *shortened*, or *led to* asserts that attribution, and a causal hedge such as *suggests that it shortened* still asserts it more tentatively rather than preserving chronology. The same boundary holds in the title, summary, headings, and body; no prominent or compressed part of the artifact receives a licence the body does not have.

Circumstantial detail about the case the text reports is a claim like any other. A duration, a manner, a motive, an absence — something that did not happen, a decision nobody took — and a state of affairs offered as background are each an assertion about the case, and each is either carried by the material or absent from the text. Detail of that kind is what a draft invents first, because it reads as prose rather than as an assertion and so survives the writer's own reading of it: *the few minutes before the meeting began* and *nobody thought to object* are inventions wherever the material gives no duration and records no such absence.

An evaluative characterisation is a claim beyond the fact it describes. A count is not *high*, *low*, *modest*, or otherwise relative unless the material supplies the comparison, target, capacity, or speaker assessment that makes it so. Without that support, state the count and its exclusions without classifying its size; an exact number does not make the unsupported adjective true.

Attribution is exact. Who said it, who found it, and how they know are part of the claim, not decoration on it. *Studies show*, *experts agree*, and *it is widely believed* attribute to nobody and can be neither checked nor contradicted; name the source or drop the claim.

Uncertainty is stated where it exists and not manufactured where it does not. A hedge on a fact the material states plainly is as inaccurate as a certainty on something the material leaves open.

Nothing is inflated. A change is not *a revolution*, a useful tool is not *a game-changer*, and an ordinary decision is not *unprecedented*. Importance that the material supports needs no adjective; importance it does not support is not made true by one.

A length that was asked for is a constraint on how much of the material to use, and never a licence to add to it. Where the material cannot fill it, the text is delivered at the length the material supports, and what further material would close the gap is named beside it. A text brought up to the requested number with detail the material does not carry has met the requirement that was easy to check and broken the one that mattered.

## Openings and endings

Open on the material. The first sentence carries information — a fact, a scene, a tension, a question the text will answer — rather than announcing the subject or explaining that the subject matters. *In today's fast-moving world* and *Communication is more important than ever* are openings that could precede any text at all, which is why they precede this one badly.

End where the text is finished. A closing that summarises what the reader has just read, restates the opening as a rhetorical question, or reaches for an uplifting generality is a paragraph the text does not need. Where the job calls for a conclusion, a recommendation, or a next step, the ending is that thing and says it plainly.

## Formatting

A list is for things that are genuinely a list, and prose that has been chopped into bullets loses the connectives that made it an argument. Headings are for a text long enough that a reader will navigate it. Bold is for the rare word or phrase a reader must not miss, and a page with six bold phrases has none.

Formatting the requested artifact needs — a title, a heading level, a table, a quotation block — is part of the text and is written properly. Formatting that is there to make a thin text look substantial is removed.

## Consistency

One text, one voice. Person, tense, level of formality, and the treatment of numbers, dates, and names stay the same from the first line to the last, and a shift happens only where the text has a reason a reader can see.

Terminology stays fixed once chosen. Where the material calls one thing by two names, the text picks one, says so if the other will be recognised, and then stays with it.
