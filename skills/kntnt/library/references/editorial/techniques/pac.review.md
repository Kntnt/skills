# PAC — review guidance

Diagnostics for the requirements the PAC technique's base half states, for a Skill reviewing a draft written against them. Nothing here is a requirement: every target a draft has to meet is in the base half, where the Skill that wrote the draft could see it, and a finding raised here is raised against a rule stated there. The examples are English and are patterns — apply what one does, in the language of the draft, rather than looking for its words.

A review reaches this document because PAC was the resolved technique for the draft. A text that happens to argue in this shape with no technique selected is not reviewed against it, and its resemblance is never a reason to raise a finding from this page.

## The Premise

**Test.** State the premise as the draft has it, then try to state its contradiction. If the contradiction is absurd or empty, nothing was at stake.

**Failure.** *Monitoring is important to reliability* — nobody argues the other side. *An assessment of our monitoring arrangements* — a topic, not a claim. A premise stated so late that the reader has already been given the evidence without knowing what it is for.

**Edge.** A premise may be a question rather than an assertion — *whether the second service earns its cost* — so long as the answer could go either way and the text says which question it is taking up.

**Ambiguity.** Where the material supports several readings of the question and the draft moves between them, the finding is that the premise is unfixed. Which reading to take is the text owner's choice, not the reviewer's.

**Minimum safe correction.** Sharpen the premise to the claim the analysis actually tests, taking the wording from the analysis rather than inventing a new claim for it. Where the analysis tests two claims, that is a finding of its own.

## The Analysis

**Test.** Sort the evidence in the draft into what supports the premise and what resists it. A column that is empty where the material has something to put in it is a finding.

**Failure.** Every paragraph confirming the premise while the material carries a stated counter-argument; the objection acknowledged in a clause and then passed over; a comparison whose unfavourable half is missing.

**Edge.** Material that genuinely contains no counter-evidence yields an analysis with none, and that is honest. The test is what the material holds, not whether the text feels balanced.

**Ambiguity.** Where evidence could be read either way, the analysis says so and says why, and a reviewer does not resolve it on the material's behalf.

**Minimum safe correction.** Restore the resisting evidence from the material in its own terms, next to the claim it bears on. Where restoring it changes what the conclusion may say, the conclusion is a second finding rather than something to adjust in the same edit.

## The Conclusion

**Test.** Cover the analysis and read the premise and the conclusion together. If the conclusion adds nothing the premise did not already contain, the text concluded nothing.

**Failure.** A conclusion restating the premise in new words; one asserting more than the analysis showed — *the second service is redundant* where the analysis showed it raised no first alert in twelve months and left the independence argument open; one that declines to conclude where the analysis did settle the question.

**Edge.** *The material cannot settle this* is a conclusion when the analysis showed why, and a finding when the analysis settled it and the text lost its nerve.

**Minimum safe correction.** Bring the conclusion back to what the analysis showed, using the analysis's own terms and its own qualifications. Never strengthen the analysis to justify a conclusion already written.

## Late entries

**Test.** Read the conclusion and mark every fact, consideration, and objection in it. Find each one in the analysis.

**Failure.** A cost, a risk, or an objection appearing for the first time in the closing paragraph; a condition attached to the recommendation that the analysis never examined.

**Edge.** A conclusion may restate something from the analysis compactly. Restatement of what was tested is not a late entry; a new consideration is.

**Minimum safe correction.** Move the item into the analysis where the material supports examining it, or cut it from the conclusion. Cutting is safe; leaving it in place with a hedge is not.

## Labels without relations

**Test.** Ignore the headings and ask where the claim is tested. If the section called analysis merely explains the premise, the labels are doing work the text is not.

**Failure.** Headings reading *Premise*, *Analysis*, *Conclusion* over three paragraphs of exposition; a text whose structure is the technique's vocabulary and whose content is a description.

**Edge.** Naming the parts is neither required nor forbidden, and a report may name its sections for what they hold without that being this failure.

**Minimum safe correction.** Raise the missing relation as the finding and leave the headings; renaming a section that holds the wrong thing hides the fault rather than repairing it.

## Proportion

**Test.** Ask how much of the text tests the premise. Where that is a small part of it, the text is mostly setup or mostly conclusion.

**Failure.** Six paragraphs establishing what the question is, one weighing it, and two drawing consequences from what the one paragraph did not show.

**Edge.** No ratio is prescribed, and a short premise with a long analysis is the ordinary shape rather than an imbalance.

**Minimum safe correction.** Cut from the setup what the analysis does not use. Adding to the analysis means going back to the material, and where the material has nothing more, the finding is that the analysis is as complete as the material allows.
