# Headlines — review guidance

Read under [Headlines](headlines.md). Read the text first and say its angle to yourself in one sentence. Then read the headline against that sentence, and each subheading against its own section and against any quotation standing under it, and ask four questions of each. Can every word in it be supported by the text? Is the core message there? Does the tone match the seriousness of the content? Would the reader feel misled afterwards?

## Avoid

Each of these in a headline or a subheading is a finding where you can name what a reader of that heading loses, and no finding where you cannot. A reader loses something where the heading claims more than the text, or something other than it; is louder in tone than the text; says again, in substance, what stands under it; gives away the point of a quotation under it; is a bare label or subject where the text has an angle, or a question where the text answers it; uses words the audience does not know; or leaves a reader who sees only the heading unable to tell what the text is about. Those are instances of the test and not the whole of it, so an item they do not name is still a finding wherever the test holds, as where a colon in place of a verb leaves the reader unable to tell who did what. A loss is something the reader gets wrong or does not get: what the text claims, how seriously it speaks, what it is about, a quotation's point met fresh. A heading that could be clearer, more specific, more complete or closer to the angle you would have chosen loses its reader nothing, and on a finished text it is left as it is:

- Claims, figures, names or conclusions not found in the text. A headline that sharpens, generalises or draws a conclusion the text itself does not draw is always a finding, however well it reads.
- Vague or mysterious wording that requires reading the text to understand the headline.
- A subject named with nothing said about it, where the text has an angle to state.
- Words, names and abbreviations the audience will not know.
- Partial quotes, colons in place of a verb, stilted headline-speak.
- Puns and references that fail for anyone who does not get the allusion.
- Question headlines. Where the text gives the answer, the headline gives it too. The exceptions are a text built as questions and answers, where the form is natural, and a text that truly leaves the question open, where the headline is deliberately kept from asserting anything.
- A tone more dramatic than the text's.
- The same words and phrasing in the headline and the standfirst or opening paragraph, or in a subheading and the first sentence under it. They complement each other; they do not repeat.
- A subheading that pre-spends the judgement, figure or concession of a quotation standing under it, so that the reader meets the quotation as the subheading said again. Read it against the quotation however far down the section the quotation stands. Repair it by naming the subject, the occasion or the speaker, or by stating what the rest of the section says, and leave the quotation as it is.

Name what the reader loses, and repair by rewriting the headline alone, from the text's angle and at the text's own strength. The text stays as it is. A headline or a subheading a round changed is reported with this finding — the part, and the defect that licensed the change — and, where the new one asserts something the old did not, with what it now asserts, so that a reader of the account can tell a repaired part from a rewritten one. A repair keeps the text's recurring words and figures and its meaning: a word or an image the text returns to comes back in the form the text gives it, and the heading names the text's point in the text's own word for it, never in a neighbouring word that widens or shifts what the text says.

## Leave alone

A working headline is left alone. Plain is no defect, and neither is a headline with no number or place where none carries the message, direct address in an instructional text, or an angle other than the one you would have chosen where the text supports it. A label on a reference or navigation text is correct as a label.

Read a heading as its reader meets it, and count its loss as [the base review](base.review.md) counts every passage's. A reader who sees only the heading, as a headline in a list or a search result, has nothing else, so a headline alone has to tell that reader what the text is about. A subheading is met after the headline and the standfirst, so a word or a name they have introduced is known to its reader, and a subheading that refers back to one is not unclear. Four kinds of heading are working, and no *Avoid* item makes one a finding by its wording alone. None of the four overrides the first *Avoid* item, a claim the text does not support as a reader would take it, or the item on a subheading that pre-spends a quotation standing under it:

- An allusion or an image that still tells a reader who sees only the heading what the text is about. It names the text's subject, and may name it in general terms where the text's first lines give the particulars. That reader is owed the subject, not its particulars: what the heading leaves for the text to fill in — what a contrast sets apart, what an image stands for, the figures and the findings — is the allusion at work and not a reference left unresolved. One that names no subject at all leaves its reader unable to tell what the text is about, however soon the body resolves it, and that is a finding.
- A figure of speech in the text's own register, or the writer's own voice in a signed text, first person included. A figure is an overclaim only where a reader would take it as a factual claim the text does not support, and there the first *Avoid* item decides; an *everything*, a *nothing* or a *never* that overstates in the way the figure means to is read as the figure it is.
- A subheading that states what its section says, where the section's point is not a quotation's judgement, figure or concession. A subheading that sums up its section says what the section says as a whole; that is its work, not a repeat, and it may name the section's limit or its setting rather than its most favourable result. Where the section's point is a quotation's judgement, figure or concession, a subheading that states that point pre-spends the quotation and stays a finding.
- A heading that shares names or topic words with the sentence under it while saying something that sentence does not. A repeat is a finding by what it says again, not by the words it shares.

Where you would word a working heading differently but can name no loss, record nothing against it: a note that it could be better is not a finding, and it licenses no change.

A subheading over a quotation that names the subject, the occasion or the speaker and leaves the quotation its point is working too, and not a subject named with nothing said about it.
