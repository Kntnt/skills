# Reviewing against the base contract

The base half says what a text has to reach. This half says how to tell whether it got there, what to do when the answer is arguable, and how much to change once the answer is no. It adds no requirement: everything a draft is answerable to is in [`base.md`](base.md), where the Skill that writes will see it, and anything asserted here that is not there would be a rule half the collection had never been told about.

The surface patterns of machine-sounding prose have their own catalogue in [`anti-slop.md`](anti-slop.md), which is read beside this file. What follows is the rest of the contract: the failures that are about what the text is doing rather than about the phrases it reaches for.

## What counts as a finding

A finding is a place where the text misses something the contract holds, stated so that somebody else could check it: where in the text, which requirement, and what the reader loses. *The third section is weak* is not a finding. *The third section argues from a single interview and the sentence claiming a trend is unqualified* is one.

Three things are not findings, however much they attract attention. A choice you would have made differently, where the text's choice also satisfies the contract. A house style the contract does not hold — a serial comma, a heading depth, a preference between two correct constructions. And a mechanical error, which is corrected by the mechanical pass at the end and never by a review; noting one in the findings makes it look unhandled when it is about to be fixed.

Where a passage is arguable, ask what the text is for and who is reading it. A digression that carries the argument is not padding, a rough sentence that sounds like the writer is not a defect, and a repeated word that is the same word for the same thing is the contract working rather than failing. When the honest answer is that it depends on something the text does not settle and you cannot, say so in the finding rather than deciding it silently.

Order findings by what they cost the reader. A text aimed at two readers at once, a section in the wrong place, and an unsupported claim each break something a sentence-level fix cannot repair; a flat paragraph rhythm does not.

## Reading for the reader and the job

Ask first what the reader is meant to know, believe, feel, or be able to do at the end, then read the draft as somebody who has only what the draft supplies. The failure is usually visible as a sentence that assumes a fact, a term, or a stake the text never gave, and as a passage that is interesting on its own and does nothing for the job.

The two-readers failure hides well because every individual sentence is fine. The tells are a definition offered for a term the text later uses as though everyone knows it, a register that moves between paragraphs, and a passage whose depth belongs to a specialist sitting beside one written for somebody meeting the subject. Name which reader the draft mostly serves and which passages belong to the other.

## Reading the structure

Read the first sentence of each paragraph in order, and nothing else. If that sequence is a coherent account of the text, the order and the paragraphing are sound; where it jumps, doubles back, or reads as a list of topics rather than a progression, the problem is in the arrangement and not in the prose.

Test every connective against the relation it claims. Cover the connective and ask whether the second thing really follows from, cuts against, or exemplifies the first. A *therefore* over a sequence, a *but* between two agreeing sentences, and a *for example* introducing a new argument are three of the same fault, and the repair is to state the real relation or to drop the word, never to swap in a milder connective.

Where a paragraph makes two movements, the second movement usually starts at a sentence you can point to, and splitting there is the whole of the fix. Where a paragraph makes none, it is either a sentence belonging to its neighbour or a paragraph the text does not need.

## Reading the sentences

Read a passage aloud in your head and mark where you run out of breath or where the rhythm goes flat. Those two marks find long tangled sentences and uniform ones far more reliably than counting words does.

For a suspect sentence, find the main verb and the actor. Where the actor is missing and could be named, where the action sits in a noun rather than the verb, or where the subject and its verb are separated by a clause the reader has to hold, the sentence has a specific fault with a specific repair. Where the passive is doing real work — the object genuinely is the subject, or the actor genuinely is unknown — it stays.

Repetition needs a diagnosis before a repair. The same information given twice is cut. The same term used twice for the same thing is correct. A closing that repeats an opening is an ending problem rather than a sentence problem.

## Reading the words

Concreteness is checkable: for each general claim, ask whether the text has the number, date, name, amount, or example that would make it verifiable, and whether that specific is present somewhere else in the draft and merely not used here. A general sentence sitting one line above the specific that supports it usually wants deleting rather than rewriting.

Jargon is judged by the reader, not by the word. Ask whether this reader uses the term daily; where they do, replacing it costs precision, and where they do not, the text either defines it once or uses the plain word throughout.

An intensifier is tested by removing it. If the sentence means the same, it was decoration; if it means less, it was carrying information and the underlying claim may be the thing to check.

## Reading the claims

For each claim, name the support and then compare their strengths. Evidence about a tendency under a sentence asserting a rule, a single case under a generalisation, and a correlation written as a cause are the three failures that matter most, and each is repaired by moving the sentence down to what the support holds rather than by adding a hedge in front of it.

Hedging is wrong in both directions and the review has to check both. A hedge on something the material states plainly makes a clear text sound unsure; a flat assertion over something genuinely open is the more serious of the two, because a reader cannot see what was removed.

## Reading the opening and the ending

Cover the first paragraph and read from the second. Where the text loses nothing, the opening was throat-clearing; where the second paragraph turns out to be a better first one, say so and let the deletion be the finding.

Do the same at the end from the other direction. Read the final paragraph and ask what a reader knows after it that they did not know before it. Nothing is the ordinary answer for a summary, a restated opening, or a closing generality, and the repair is to delete and end on the last concrete sentence — never to write a better final flourish.

## Reading the formatting and the consistency

A list is checked by turning it back into prose. Where the items need connectives to make sense, they were an argument that was chopped up; where prose written out is genuinely worse, the list belongs. Headings are checked by asking whether anybody would navigate a text this long, and bold by counting: past a few instances in a page, none of them is doing anything.

Consistency is read by scanning one thing at a time through the whole draft — person, tense, formality, and then the treatment of numbers, dates, and names. A shift found this way is either a mistake or a move the text has a visible reason for, and the review says which.

## The minimum safe correction

Correct with the smallest change that removes the finding, and touch nothing the finding does not concern. A deletion is safer than a rewrite, a rewritten sentence is safer than a rewritten paragraph, and a restructure is worth it only where the sequence itself was the fault.

Never repair by adding. A missing specific is supplied only if the draft already carries it somewhere; an unsupported claim is narrowed to what the text holds or cut, never propped up with a source, a statistic, or an example that came from you. A finding you cannot fix without inventing something is a finding that stays unresolved and is reported.

Preserve what the writer chose. Their vocabulary, their bluntness, their humour, their admitted doubt, and the passages that already work are not the review's to even out, and a correction that leaves the text tidier and less recognisable has cost more than it repaired.

After any correction, read the surrounding passage again rather than the changed sentence alone. Repairs break transitions, orphan a reference, strand a pronoun, and duplicate a fact that the deleted sentence used to carry, and each of those is visible only from the paragraph.
