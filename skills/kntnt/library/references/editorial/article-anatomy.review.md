# Article anatomy — review guidance

Read under [the anatomy](article-anatomy.md), and word each finding in the strength the statement it rests on has.

A requirement that fails is a finding. Count characters and words before reporting a limit, and give the count.

A part the text does not have is reported, and a part it has is repaired. Where a required part is absent — the headline, the standfirst, the byline, the lead, a section, or the ending with its call to action — its absence is a finding, and the part is not written: not from the text's own content and not from anywhere else, since the text is finished and a person decides what fills it. A part the text has that fails its own rule is repaired like any other defect, from the text's own content. A part in the wrong place is present, not absent, and is repaired by moving it into place: a byline standing at the end of the text is moved to its place in the order, although the measuring script reports the byline `absent`. Ending content the text has — closing paragraphs that show the lead's expectation met or call the reader to act — is a present ending that fails its rule where it stands inside the last section, or at the close of a text with no section at all; it is repaired from what the text already says, a vague call to action among it, and no section and no subheading is written to hold it. A text with no ending content at all has an absent ending, and a text that calls the reader to no action has an absent call to action; both are reported. A failure the script reports only because another part is absent is reported as that absent part and is not repaired by writing it: a lead of several paragraphs in a text with no subheading fails only because its sections are absent, so the finding is the absent sections, and no section and no subheading is written to make the lead one paragraph. Where the script takes the text's one opening paragraph as its standfirst and reports the lead absent, whichever of the two that paragraph is taken for, the other is absent and is reported.

A departure from a *should* is a finding only when following the norm was possible and the text is no better for having left it. A paragraph a little past the edge that holds one thought and reads well at that length has met the bar. A paragraph far past it, or paragraphs that run long throughout, cost the web reader something a finding can name. The anatomy's bands are its four *should* norms on a dimension: the headline's three to eight words and its at most 60 characters, a paragraph's at most 80 words, and a section's at most three paragraphs. A band is not by itself a defect. A headline, a subheading or a paragraph the text has that meets its requirements is not rewritten, split or merged only to come inside a band. A change the test above justifies is not made only to come inside one: it repairs what the finding names the reader losing, as a split of a paragraph far past the edge does. The limits the script reports as failures are requirements, not bands — the headline's 20–70 characters, a subheading's at most 70 characters, and the standfirst's one paragraph of at most 60 words — and a part that fails one is repaired. A subheading has no band, only its maximum. A subheading that describes clearly what its section is about meets its requirement, and is not rewritten from a label into a statement on that ground alone; one that does not describe its section fails that requirement and is repaired.

*Most* is read across the whole text, and no single paragraph or section fails it. A four-sentence paragraph is no finding. A paragraph holding two thoughts is one, and so is a text whose paragraphs are nearly all a single sentence or nearly all long. Name the pattern and what it costs the reader rather than the count.

Read the standfirst separately, then with the lead. Can it introduce the piece on its own, and does the lead start doing the promised work? Repeated orientation may be necessary; an opening that restarts without advancing is the defect. Then read the body with the standfirst covered. A pronoun, a definite form or a phrase such as *the reason* whose referent only the standfirst supplies is a finding, repaired by naming the referent where the body first needs it.

In a text under review, the author is whoever the text, its metadata or the invocation names. A byline nobody can name is reported as missing and left unfilled: the reviewer's user is not presumed to have written a text they brought for review.
