# Web copy — review guidance

Diagnostics for the requirements the web-copy genre's base half states, for a Skill reviewing a draft written against them. Nothing here is a requirement: every target a draft has to meet is in the base half, where the Skill that wrote the draft could see it, and a finding raised here is raised against a rule stated there. The examples are English and are patterns — apply what one does, in the language of the draft, rather than looking for its words.

## The surface

**Test.** Read the surface alone — the title, the headings, the first line under each of them, and anything set off from the prose — and then say what this page is, who it is for, and what it tells somebody. Whatever you cannot answer from that pass is a finding against the page, not against the sentence three paragraphs down that holds the answer.

**Failure.** A page whose headings are all sound and whose point sits in the fourth paragraph of the third section; a page that never says what it is, because the writer knew which site it was going on and the reader does not; an opening line spent warming up, so the most-read line on the page carries nothing; a heading followed by two sentences of preamble before the section says anything.

**Edge.** A page short enough to take in at a glance may carry no headings at all, and its opening lines are then its whole surface. Not everything has to be reachable from the surface either: detail that rewards somebody reading properly belongs where it is, and what fails is the page's own point sitting there.

**Minimum safe correction.** Promote the sentence that answers into the surface — usually into the heading above it or the first line beneath one — and leave the rest of the section as it stands. Do not repair by adding a part nobody asked for, such as a summary above the page or an introduction explaining what follows; the answer is already in the draft, in the wrong place.

## The headings

**Test.** Read the headings alone and write down, for each, what you expect the section to hold. Then read the sections. A heading you could predict nothing from, and a section that does not deliver what you wrote down, are both findings.

**Failure.** *The thing nobody tells you about procurement*; *And then it got interesting*; a question the section never answers; a heading whose sense arrives only once the section has been read, which is the tease in its quietest form; *Three things to check before signing* over a section that treats two of them and moves on to something else.

**Edge.** A declaration can be short, and a noun phrase naming what is there declares perfectly well. A heading that happens to be lively is not a finding as long as what it announces is what stands under it; the test is what a reader could skip on, never how plain the wording is.

**Minimum safe correction.** Rewrite the heading to what the section actually holds, in the section's own words. Where a section drifted away from an accurate heading, the heading is usually the true one: move the drifted material to the section it belongs in, rather than widening the heading until it covers both.

## The cold start

**Test.** Read the sections one at a time in an order nobody would read them in, each as somebody who has read no other, and mark every word that needs a section this reader did not read.

**Failure.** *As we saw above*; *this brings us to the second of them*; *the tool*, where the tool was named two sections up; a section opening on a contrast with something stated earlier on the page; a heading that only makes sense as the next item after the one before it.

**Edge.** A page still reads in sequence for somebody reading it in sequence, and a section that follows from the one above it is not a finding; what fails is a section that cannot be read without it. A term the page's audience uses daily is not an unintroduced term.

**Minimum safe correction.** Name the thing where the section needs it, taking the word from the section that introduced it, and delete the backward connective instead of rewriting it: two sections that meet without a bridge read perfectly well to the reader going down the page, who was the only reader the bridge ever served.

## The visual elements

**Test.** Take each element in turn and name the shape its content has: things comparable on the same dimensions, items that are genuinely co-ordinate, a term set against its definition, one sentence that carries standing alone, something secondary the reader can pass over. An element you cannot name a shape for is decoration, however much it does for the look of the page.

**Failure.** A callout because the page needed a break; a pull quote lifting a sentence that says nothing standing on its own; three items set as a table that one sentence would have carried; a table whose rows are not comparable on its columns; a disclosure folding away the thing the section exists to say, so the scan finds a heading and the answer is behind a click.

**Edge.** A page carrying several elements is not thereby decorated — the finding is per element and turns on its content, never on the count — and a page with none is not a page that failed to use them. Where the request or the surface the page renders on requires a particular element, that requirement stands, and this is not the place to argue with it.

**Minimum safe correction.** Put the content back into the prose it was taken from, or into the element whose shape it actually has. Never repair an element by supplying what its empty places would need: something short of the information its form implies is the wrong form rather than an unfinished one.

## Speaking rather than describing

**Test.** Read a stretch of the page and ask who is in it. Where the answer is a category — a role, a segment, an industry, *organisations today* — rather than the person reading, the prose is describing, however direct its sentences are.

**Failure.** *Decision-makers in the housing sector face increasingly complex demands*; a page whose every verb belongs to the organisation and none to the reader; a benefit written as a property of the thing rather than as something the reader will do differently; an opening paragraph explaining that the field is changing, which addresses everybody in it and nobody at the screen.

**Edge.** Naming the organisation is not the failure, and a page written throughout in the first-person plural can speak perfectly well; second person throughout is not by itself address. Where the page's subject genuinely is a group, describing the group is what the page is for, and what fails is describing the reader.

**Minimum safe correction.** Replace the category with the situation: take the particular the material actually holds — what goes wrong, when, and to whom — and put it where the abstraction stood. Do not repair by switching pronouns, which leaves the same description with the reader's name attached to it. Where the material carries no such particular, that is a finding about the material and not a licence to invent one.

## The page's own structure

**Test.** List the parts of the draft — the sections, whatever stands at the top, whatever it asks the reader to do at the end — and find each one in what was asked for. A part nobody asked for is a finding, however conventional it looks.

**Failure.** A closing invitation to get in touch on a page whose request named no such thing; the sections a page of this kind usually has, in the order it usually has them, imported rather than taken from the brief; a summary standing above the page because pages often carry one; a next step pointing somewhere the request never mentioned.

**Edge.** Where the request names the parts, they are the request's and are not findings here, whatever this genre would have chosen. A long page that has grown headings so a reader can navigate it has not invented a structure, and where the request names nothing, the arrangement the material implies is the writer's and is judged as ordinary structure rather than as invention.

**Minimum safe correction.** Delete the part nobody asked for; where it holds something the page needs, move that into a part the request does ask for. Do not replace an invented part with a better invented one, and do not raise the missing brief as a finding against the draft.
