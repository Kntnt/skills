# Article — review guidance

Diagnostics for the requirements the article genre's base half states, for a Skill reviewing a draft written against them. Nothing here is a requirement: every target a draft has to meet is in the base half, where the Skill that wrote the draft could see it, and a finding raised here is raised against a rule stated there. The examples are English and are patterns — apply what one does, in the language of the draft, rather than looking for its words.

## The angle

**Test.** Say what the article claims, in one sentence, using only what the draft actually says. If that sentence names a subject rather than something asserted about it, or if two sentences are needed, the angle is not settled in the text.

**Failure.** A draft that gives a paragraph to the history of the shared inbox, a paragraph to the team's tooling, a paragraph to the change, and a paragraph to what the team plans next is four beginnings of four articles. So is one whose opening promises *what we learned about queues* and whose body is a chronology of a migration.

**Edge.** An article may hold a second thread — a person, a running example, a comparison — without losing its angle, so long as the thread serves the claim rather than competing with it. Length is not the test either: a long feature has one angle and a short news piece has one angle.

**Ambiguity.** Where two candidate angles are both supported by the material and the draft serves both partly, the reviewer's finding is that the draft has not chosen, and the choice belongs to whoever owns the text. Where the request or the material names the angle, that one settles it and the finding is that the draft drifted from it.

**Minimum safe correction.** Cut what belongs to the other angle and let the surviving text stand, rather than rewriting the article around a new one. Where a passage is the only support for the angle that survives, keep it and cut the framing around it.

## Holding the reader

**Test.** Read from the end of each paragraph and ask what would make somebody continue. Where the answer is *nothing, but there is more*, the paragraph has not earned the next.

**Failure.** Background delivered in a block before anything is at stake; a paragraph explaining what the reader was about to be shown anyway; the same fact given twice because it was interesting both times.

**Edge.** A necessary explanation that no reader would seek out on its own is not a fault where the text has just made them need it. Interest is bought by the preceding sentence, not by the material's own importance.

**Minimum safe correction.** Move the passage to where the reader needs it, or reduce it to the clause the following paragraph actually uses. Deletion is safe where nothing after it depends on the passage; check that before cutting.

## Reporting rather than asserting

**Test.** For each conclusion the draft draws, find the material it stands on in the text above it. A conclusion whose support is only in the source material is a conclusion the reader is asked to take on trust.

**Failure.** *The change made the team dramatically more responsive* where the text has shown no figure, no incident, and no quotation; a summary of an interview in place of anything the person said.

**Edge.** A draft may state a conclusion the material supports without showing every element of the support, where the omitted part would not change what a reader believes. The failure is support that is absent, not support that is compressed.

**Minimum safe correction.** Bring the supporting detail up from the material into the text, or weaken the conclusion to what the shown material carries. Never manufacture a figure, an incident, or a quotation to support a sentence that is already written.

## People

**Test.** Where the material names somebody and says what they did or said, look for them in the draft. Where the draft names somebody, look for them in the material.

**Failure.** A named agent's account rendered as *some staff felt*; a composite *one team member* assembled from two people; a role attached to a name that the material does not give them.

**Edge.** Material that names nobody yields an article that names nobody, and that is correct rather than a gap to be filled. A person the material names in passing need not appear where they have nothing to do with the angle.

**Minimum safe correction.** Restore the name and role from the material, or drop the attribution to the impersonal form the material supports. Where a composite has been made, split it back to what each person actually said, or attribute nothing.

## The title

**Test.** Read the title, then read the last paragraph. If the title asks something the article never answers, or names something the article does not deliver, the title is the fault and not the article.

**Failure.** A title naming the subject — *Our support inbox* — where the article has an angle; a question the body leaves open; a promise of a general lesson from a text about one team.

**Edge.** Where the artifact is a passage, a message, or a fragment, no title is expected and none is invented for it.

**Minimum safe correction.** Rewrite the title to the sentence the angle test above already produced. Leave the body alone: a title is cheaper to change than the text under it.

## Length

**Test.** Where a length was stated, count. Within a small margin is a pass; twice the length is a finding, and so is half of it.

**Failure.** Length reached by restating what has been said, by a summary the text does not need, or by an introduction to a section that follows immediately.

**Edge.** Where no length was stated, there is nothing to measure and no finding to raise. A short article whose angle is complete is finished, not thin.

**Minimum safe correction.** Cut restatement, throat-clearing, and passages already found to belong to another angle, and stop when the count is inside the margin. Where the draft is short of a stated length, the finding is that material is missing; supplying it means going back to the source material, never inventing to fill.
