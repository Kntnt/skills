# Report

A document written for people who asked for it and will act on what it says — a team, a board, a client, a manager holding a budget. Its reader is known, has a decision or an obligation waiting on the answer, and will read it once and return to it for a figure.

## What this genre asks for

**The parts stand where a reader expects to find them.** A report is read once and then entered again in the middle, weeks or months later, by somebody after one thing, so the same few parts sit in the same few places every time. Beneath the title stands a short opening fixing what the report covers, what it was written to do, and the period it speaks to; a reader coming back to it later learns from that alone whether this is the document their question belongs to. The body follows. Where the report is long enough that nobody will read it end to end at one sitting — from about five thousand words, or wherever the reader has asked for one — a summary stands above the body and carries the whole of it, so that the reader takes in the report and then goes to the section they came for. Below that length the report carries none, since a summary above a document somebody reads in one go tells them what they are about to read. The threshold settles whether that part is there at all, and says nothing about how long any part runs.

**The question is stated, in the reader's terms.** A report exists because somebody asked something, so it says what it was asked and what it is meant to settle, in the words its reader would use, before it says anything else. A draft that opens with background has not yet said what it is for, and a draft that answers a question nobody asked is a report about the wrong thing however good its evidence is.

**The answer comes before the evidence, and the answer is the document's.** The reader may act on the first paragraph and read the rest only if they doubt it. The finding, the judgement, or the recommendation is stated plainly at the top, and everything that supports it follows in the order that supports it; a report that builds to its answer has kept the one reader it has waiting for a verdict they needed first. The rule fixes where the report's own answer to the question stands, and nothing about how a section beneath it arrives at what that section establishes: a section reaching its result at its own end has withheld nothing, because the answer the reader came for was given before they got there.

**Every figure carries its basis.** A number a reader may act on says what it counts, over what period, and where it came from, because a figure without its basis cannot be argued with, checked, or reproduced by the person whose decision rests on it. An estimate is labelled an estimate, a projection is labelled a projection, and a figure the material gives for one period is not quietly extended to another.

**The report is where the figures live.** Its reader opens it to find a number and expects it to be there rather than in the material behind it, so a report carries figures at a density another kind of text would find heavy: the counts, the amounts, the shares, the intervals, and the dates the material gives are given, not summarised into a description of themselves. A paragraph reporting that a cost came down, where the material holds both costs and the months between them, has sent the reader to the source for what they opened the report to get. Where the numbers are a comparison across categories, a series over time, or a set whose relations are the point, they belong in a table or a chart rather than in sentences asking a reader to hold six of them at once; the caption says what is shown, and the prose around it says what the report takes from it, so that the reader who studies the figure and the reader who passes over it are told the same thing. A table no sentence introduces, and a chart nothing in the text takes anything from, are material the report gathered and did not read.

**The strongest objection is put and then answered.** Where the material carries a counter-argument — an on-call lead's reason, a dissenting note, an obvious question the reader will raise in the meeting — it appears in the terms its holder would use, at its strongest, and the report then says what the material does and does not do to it. An objection weakened before it is answered is an objection the reader will raise anyway, now knowing that the report handled it badly.

**What the report cannot settle is part of the report.** The gaps in the material, the evidence that was not available, the conditions under which the answer would differ, and what would have to be known to settle the rest are written down, not left out. A limit stated is a report the reader can rely on; a limit discovered afterwards costs the report every claim it made.

**A recommendation says what to do.** Where the report recommends, it names the action, who takes it, by when, and what it costs or risks, as far as the material supports each of those. Where the material supports none of them, the report recommends nothing and says instead what would have to be established first, which is a usable answer where an unsupported recommendation is not.

**Sections are named for what they hold, and better for what they found.** A reader comes back for the twelve-month alert history or the cost of the second service, so the headings that carry those name them: *Cost over twelve months* finds what it holds and *Analysis* does not. A heading can do more than locate, and in a report it usually should: where the section reached a finding, the heading says it, so that *Thirty-five search terms explain the whole gap* is a label and a discovery at once and a reader running down the contents has met the case before reading a section of it. What a heading never does is withhold what the section holds in order to make a reader open it. The levels do the dividing and they carry it without numbers: the upper level takes the thematic area, the level beneath it takes the part of that area one section settles, and *1.2 Findings* places a reader in a scheme where a heading should have placed them in the report. A report reaching for a level below those two has an upper level doing the work of two reports.

**Every section is read by somebody who arrived at it directly.** A report is entered from a contents list, a link, or a search by a reader who wants one section and will read no other, so each section carries what it takes to be understood alone: the term it turns on named in it, the figure it rests on given in it or pointed to, and no pronoun, no definite form, and no *as shown above* whose referent sits in a section this reader did not read. The base contract already holds a body to standing without its paratext; a report is held to more than that, because a reader who came in at the middle is not holding the thread a reader who started at the top would have.

**The sentences are whole ones.** A report does not buy rhythm with grammar: no fragment, no one-word sentence, and no sentence opening on a conjunction to give a paragraph a beat. Those are licences a text takes when its reader may leave at any line, and this reader will not leave — they are checking, and a sentence shaped for its cadence is one they read twice to find the claim in. The variation the base contract asks for is made inside whole sentences, and a short declarative after two long ones does everything a fragment was reaching for while staying something a reader can quote, act on, and argue with.

## What this genre does not do

It does not persuade beyond what the evidence carries, and it does not perform. Rhetoric, an arresting opening, and a conclusion that reaches for significance all belong to texts whose reader has to be won; this reader is already obliged to read it, and what they need from it is that every sentence be accountable.

It adds no requirement the base contract already holds. Register, structure, sentences, words, claims, openings, endings, formatting, and consistency are stated there once, and a rule repeated here would be one rule made into two things to keep true.

## The technique this genre is ordinarily written with

PAC ([`pac.md`](../techniques/pac.md)). This reader is checking something rather than being carried anywhere, and that arc is the one built for a reader who wants to see the reasoning tested; what it asks of a section is stated there. ABT is not this genre's arc and is no fallback for it: a report told as a complication and its consequence hands a story to somebody who came for a result they can act on and argue with.
