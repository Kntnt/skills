# Case study

An account of what one named customer did, what it changed for them, and what they make of it — published by the supplier who did the work and told largely in that customer's own words. It is the one thing a supplier publishes that the supplier does not speak in: the customer carries the text, the supplier stands inside it as a third party, and what the reader is finally offered is a named person willing to say on the record that it was worth doing.

## What this genre asks for

**A reader meets the parts in the order a case study puts them.** Somebody reads one to find out whether it is about them, and they look for the same things in the same places every time. The headline names what the customer achieved rather than what the supplier delivered: *The housing company that stopped cutting new keys* is what a reader scanning for their own situation recognises, and *How our locking system helped a housing company* is an advertisement built from the same facts. A standfirst follows, carrying who the customer is, what was done, and what came of it; the same words are lifted from the top of the text to stand as its teaser wherever the case study is featured, so everything else a standfirst owes as a text belongs to the teaser genre ([`teaser.md`](teaser.md)), which states it once. Then the customer: who they are, what they do, and how large the operation is, in as much of it as the reader needs to see what was at stake. Then what set the work going. Then the work itself, which is the body of the case study and is carried by the customer's own words. Then the customer's recommendation. And last a close naming something the reader can do next: where the request gives somewhere for them to go, the close points there, and where it gives none, the close names the action and stops rather than sending the reader somewhere invented for the purpose. These are parts a reader recognises rather than sections to be labelled, and what the shape settles is which of them are there and in what order, never how much of the text any of them takes.

**The customer's voice carries the text, and the supplier is a third party in it.** A case study is published by the supplier and spoken by the customer, which inverts every other text the supplier publishes and is therefore the first thing a draft loses. The supplier is named and referred to as somebody else throughout: in the writer's voice it is never *we*, and what was built, installed, or delivered is never *our solution*. Which forms a language uses to do that is that language's business rather than this genre's; what the genre asks is that a reader who did not know who published the text would take it for the customer's.

**The interviewee's words carry the substance and the writer's prose the structure.** The judgement, the experience, and the assessment are in what the customer said — what they were worried about, what surprised them, what they would do differently — and the writer's prose puts those in an order, gives the reader what they need to follow them, and adds nothing of its own to them. A draft with a few attributed remarks set into prose that carries everything of substance has not met this: it reads as a supplier quoting a customer rather than as a customer speaking, and it is the easier draft to write, because summarising an interview costs less than finding the sentence inside it. Each quotation is also read for what it says about the person who said it, since a reader who has met them nowhere else judges them on it, and the person being judged is real and will read the text. A spoken quotation may be repaired within the boundary the writing Skill works under, which is stated there and not here. Where the material will not carry a text built this way, the shortfall is in the material and is reported as such.

**Without the customer's own recommendation the text is a project description rather than a case study.** Somewhere in it the customer says, in their own words, that it was worth doing: that they would choose the same again, that they would tell somebody else to, that they are glad they did. It is the one part the genre cannot do without, because everything else is an account of work that was carried out, and an account of work that was carried out is what the supplier could have published alone. It comes from the material like every other quotation. Where the interviewee did not say it, the text does not say it on their behalf and does not settle for the writer reporting that they were pleased; what is missing is named as missing.

**The situation is described from the customer's side.** What set the work going is told as the customer met it — what they were trying to do, what it was costing them, what they had already tried and what happened next — rather than as a problem the supplier found and put right. The framing that fails here sounds helpful, which is why it survives a writer's own reading: *the problem was that they could not keep track of their keys* casts a named organisation as helpless and the supplier as its rescuer, and publishes that about somebody who agreed to appear in the text. It also costs the text its evidence, because a reader who has watched a rescue being staged discounts the customer's own words when they finally arrive.

**The case is made by the customer's account and never argued by the writer.** The text is published by a party with an interest in what the reader concludes, and the reader knows that before the first sentence. What makes it work anyway is that everything claimed for the supplier is claimed by the customer: the results are the customer's figures, the assessment is the customer's assessment, and the writer's prose puts no adjective on either. A sentence in the writer's voice arguing the supplier's case — that the service was outstanding, that the system is unique — tells the reader whose text this really is and puts the customer's own words under suspicion in the same movement. The close is held to that restraint too: it names what the reader can do next rather than pressing them towards a purchase.

## What this genre does not do

It does not announce the supplier's news. A text whose subject is that the supplier has done something, written for somebody deciding whether to pass it on, is a press release; a case study's subject is what a customer did, and the supplier is in it because the customer had reason to mention them.

It does not explain the offering. A passage setting out how the supplier's product works, what it consists of, and what else it can do belongs on the page the reader can go to afterwards, and inside a case study it is the point at which the customer stops speaking. What the text says about the offering is what the customer says about the part of it they used.

It does not report from outside. An article's writer stands behind the material and reports it in their own voice; a case study's writer holds the text together while somebody else talks. The two can be built from the same interview, and what separates them is who the reader hears.

It adds no requirement the base contract already holds. Register, structure, sentences, words, claims, openings, endings, formatting, and consistency are stated there once, and a rule repeated here would be one rule made into two things to keep true.
