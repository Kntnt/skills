# Case study — review guidance

Diagnostics for the requirements the case-study genre's base half states, for a Skill reviewing a draft written against them. Nothing here is a requirement: every target a draft has to meet is in the base half, where the Skill that wrote the draft could see it, and a finding raised here is raised against a rule stated there. The examples are English and are patterns — apply what one does, in the language of the draft, rather than looking for its words.

## The shape

**Test.** Read the draft for its parts alone, ignoring how well any of them is written, and check them against the sequence the base half states: the headline, the standfirst, the customer, what set the work going, the work in the customer's words, the recommendation, the close. A part standing somewhere else in that sequence is a finding, and so is a part the material supplies and the draft leaves out. Read the headline by itself and ask whose achievement it names.

**Failure.** A headline with the supplier as its subject, or one naming the product rather than what came of it; a standfirst giving the result and never saying who the customer is; a text that opens on the supplier's offering before the reader has met the customer; background about the customer arriving after the work has been described, where the reader needed it to follow the work; a close that is an offer rather than an action, or that sends the reader to a destination the request never gave.

**Edge.** The parts are what a reader recognises rather than sections to be labelled: a standfirst owes no label announcing itself, and a part is not missing because it runs to a single paragraph. The recommendation and the close often stand together, and a close carrying the customer's own recommendation into the action is the shape rather than a departure from it. A part the material cannot supply is absent rather than invented, and that is a finding against the material.

**Minimum safe correction.** Move a misplaced part whole and leave its wording alone. Rewrite the headline from what the text already shows the customer achieved, and leave the body: a headline is cheaper to change than what stands under it. Where the close names a destination nobody gave, cut the destination and let the action stand.

## The customer's voice

**Test.** Read every sentence that is not inside a quotation and find each mention of the supplier. Where the writer's own prose speaks as the supplier rather than about them, that is a finding. Then read the draft as somebody who does not know who published it, and ask whose text they would take it for.

**Failure.** *Our team*, *our solution*, *we installed the system* in the writer's voice; a passage about what the customer did in which the supplier is the subject of every sentence and the customer the object; a standfirst written from the supplier's side of the transaction, so that the text announces a delivery before anybody has met the customer.

**Edge.** Inside a quotation the speaker's own person stands, whatever it is: a customer saying *they came out the same evening* is the customer's sentence and is corrected into nothing. A supplier's employee may be quoted where the material has them speaking, so the presence of the supplier's people is not the finding — the writer's voice is. Which forms realise person is the language's business, so the finding is that the prose speaks as the supplier and never that a particular word appeared.

**Minimum safe correction.** Put the supplier's name, or the ordinary third-person form, where the first-person one stands, and change nothing else in the sentence. Where a whole passage is written from the supplier's side, the repair is to find the customer's account of the same thing in the material rather than to turn the sentences around one by one.

## The carrying voices

**Test.** Mark every quotation, then read the draft with all of them struck out and ask what is left. Where the remaining prose still carries the judgement, the reasons, and the assessment, the quotations were garnish. Then read each bridge and the quotation after it as a pair.

**Failure.** Two attributed remarks in a text otherwise built from *the customer explains that* and *according to the property manager*; a bridge that states what the quotation then says, so the reader is told the same thing twice and the quotation earns nothing; a quotation spent on a fact anybody could have stated, where the customer's voice was the one thing that fact did not need; a quotation that reads as written rather than said.

**Ambiguity.** Where the material is a summary of what somebody said rather than their words, there are no quotations to be had, and a draft cannot meet this by manufacturing them: the finding is against the material, and what the draft owes meanwhile is exact attribution rather than quotation marks. Where the material holds a few real remarks and no more, the finding is the same, and what to do about it — go back to the interviewee, or deliver a text the genre's own terms call a project description — belongs to whoever owns the text.

**Edge.** A quotation may be shortened, or its spoken syntax repaired, within the boundary the writing Skill works under. What a review finds here is a quotation the material does not support, one that says nothing, or one that leaves the person who said it looking more careless, more grudging, or more foolish than the material does.

**Minimum safe correction.** Where a bridge and its quotation say the same thing, cut the bridge back to what the quotation does not carry — ordinarily who is speaking and when — and leave the quotation untouched. Where the writer's prose has taken the substance, look in the material for the sentence in which the customer says it and put that in its place. Never write the quotation the draft is short of.

## The recommendation

**Test.** Find the sentence in which the customer says it was worth doing. It has to be theirs and in their words. Where the nearest thing is the writer reporting that they were pleased, or the customer describing a result and going no further, the part is not there.

**Failure.** *The collaboration has worked well*, in the writer's voice, standing where the recommendation should be; a closing paragraph in which the writer recommends the supplier to the reader; a quotation about the outcome — *the keys stopped disappearing* — offered as the recommendation, which says the work succeeded and not that the customer would choose it again.

**Edge.** A recommendation need not be enthusiastic and need not be unqualified. A customer who says they would do it again and would ask harder questions about the timetable has recommended it, and the qualification stays in; softening it into an unbroken endorsement is a worse finding than the hedge would have been.

**Minimum safe correction.** Take it from the material's own words and put it where the shape has it. Where the material carries nothing of the kind, remove whatever is standing in for it and raise the finding: what is missing is one question to the interviewee, and a reader of the review can be told the text is a project description until it is answered.

## The customer's side

**Test.** Read the part about what set the work going and ask whose account it is and who is the subject of its sentences. Then check each characterisation of the situation against the material: a difficulty the customer described is theirs to describe, and a difficulty the supplier diagnosed is the supplier's.

**Failure.** *They had no control over their key management*, where the material has the customer saying they were changing locks twice a year; a passage listing the deficiencies the supplier found on arrival; a situation given entirely in the supplier's categories, so that what the customer lacked turns out to be the supplier's product; a first body sentence about the supplier's offering rather than the customer's situation.

**Edge.** A hard situation is not softened. Whose account it is settles this, not how bad the situation is allowed to sound, and a customer who says plainly that it was a mess has said so and is quoted saying it.

**Minimum safe correction.** Replace the diagnosis with the customer's own description from the material, which usually holds the specifics the writer's summary was laid over. Where the material carries only the supplier's account of the situation, keep what is factual in it and drop what evaluates.

## The unargued case

**Test.** Mark every evaluative word in the writer's prose — praise, scale, novelty, ease — and ask of each who said it. Then take each claim made for the supplier and ask whether the sentence carrying it is the customer's or the writer's.

**Failure.** *A seamless implementation*, *an outstanding level of service*, *the obvious choice* outside quotation marks; a paragraph explaining what else the offering can do; a final paragraph in which the writer draws the conclusion the reader was there to reach for themselves; a close that asks for a purchase rather than naming a next step.

**Edge.** A figure the customer gives is not an argument, however favourable it is, and neither is a plain factual description of what was installed. What makes a finding is the writer valuing any of it. A superlative the customer states inside a quotation is theirs and stays.

**Minimum safe correction.** Delete the modifier and leave the noun, which changes no fact where rewriting the sentence around the claim often does. Where a whole passage argues, cut it and check that nothing after it depended on it. Where the conclusion is one the reader should have, look for it in the material as something the customer said, and use that or nothing.
