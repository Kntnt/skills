# Teaser — review guidance

Diagnostics for the requirements the teaser genre's base half states, for a Skill reviewing a draft written against them. Nothing here is a requirement: every target a draft has to meet is in the base half, where the Skill that wrote the draft could see it, and a finding raised here is raised against a rule stated there. The examples are English and are patterns — apply what one does, in the language of the draft, rather than looking for its words.

## The parts and their order

**Test.** Read the draft for its parts alone, ignoring how well each is written: what names the text, what makes the reader want it, what tells them what they get by going. A reader scanning has to meet them in that order.

**Failure.** A draft opening on its call to action and explaining afterwards what the text is; a first line repeating the headline that already stands above it, which spends a third of the teaser on something the reader has read; an action written for a form that has nowhere to put one, so the teaser ends by telling a reader to click on where the text simply continues.

**Edge.** The parts are what a reader recognises rather than sections to be labelled, and a part is not missing because it is a clause rather than a sentence. Where the form supplies the naming line, the teaser having none of its own is correct and not a gap.

**Minimum safe correction.** Move the part and leave its wording alone; a part in the wrong place is rarely also badly written. Where the draft repeats a naming line the form supplies, cut the repetition and let the sentence after it stand first. Where an action was written for a form with no place for one, delete it rather than rewriting the close around it.

## The content declaration

**Test.** Cover everything but the teaser and say what the text on the other side holds. Where the answer names a field rather than what this text says about it, the declaration is empty however well the sentences read.

**Failure.** *A deep dive into the world of customer service*; *we have written something interesting about pricing*; a teaser whose every noun would sit equally well in a teaser for a different text; an opening sentence spent on why the subject matters in general.

**Edge.** A declaration may be specific and still withhold — naming what a text settles is not settling it — and a broad source is declared broadly, where *fourteen tools compared* is a narrow declaration of a wide text.

**Minimum safe correction.** Take the most particular thing the source actually holds — the number, the name, the outcome, the question it answers — and put it where the general sentence stood. Never sharpen past the source: a declaration made specific by invention breaks at the click, which is the one place the reader is certain to check it.

## The promise

**Test.** Read the teaser, write down what you now expect the source to give you, then read the source. Anything on that list the source does not deliver is a finding, whether or not a sentence of the teaser was false.

**Failure.** A teaser leading with the one dramatic figure the source mentions in passing; *the mistake everybody makes* over a text listing five ordinary considerations; a question the source never answers; an expectation carried by a word rather than by a claim — *finally*, *the real reason*, *nobody tells you*.

**Edge.** The promise is what a reader is led to expect and not what the words assert, so a teaser defensible line by line and misleading as a whole fails here. Where the source genuinely holds what the teaser claims, the claim stays however strong it sounds.

**Minimum safe correction.** Weaken the promise to what the source keeps, taking the wording from the source's own strongest supported line. Where the whole hook was unsupported, the repair is a different hook found in the source, not a hedge placed in front of the old one.

## The arc

**Test.** Ask what unsettles the reader and where the answer to it is. A teaser in which nothing cuts against anything is a description; a teaser that resolves what it raised is a summary.

**Failure.** Three sentences of situation and no turn; a turn delivered with its answer attached — *teams kept missing alerts, so they moved the rota into the calendar* — which leaves the click nothing to deliver; a sentence for the situation, a sentence for the problem and a sentence for the resolution, which is the form filled in rather than the arc compressed.

**Edge.** The turn need not be a contradiction put into words: a question the reader now has is the same movement. A teaser for a source that resolves nothing — an open question, a trial still running — promises the state of the question, and that is a complete arc rather than a missing answer.

**Minimum safe correction.** Cut the clause that gives the answer away and stop at the turn; that is usually the whole repair, and the sentence before it rarely needs touching. Where nothing in the draft turns at all, the complication is in the source and is found there rather than invented.

## The action

**Test.** Read the last line and ask what the reader is being offered. *Click* is not an offer.

**Failure.** *Read more*, *learn more*, *find out more*, *click here* — the machinery named where the delivery should be; an action naming something the source does not contain, such as a template it never provides; an action promising a shortcut the text does not give.

**Edge.** Not every form carries one, and where the source is something to read rather than something to take, the action names what reading it gives — *see what the audit found* — rather than manufacturing a thing to download.

**Minimum safe correction.** Replace the generic verb phrase with the delivery the declaration already names; the words are usually somewhere in the teaser already. Where the delivery named is not in the source, name what is there instead of softening the verb.

## Standing alone

**Test.** Read the draft as somebody who has never seen the source, the page around it, or anything next to it, and mark every word whose referent is elsewhere.

**Failure.** *The system solves the problem*; *this approach*; *as we wrote last week*; *she explains why it failed*; a first line that completes a sentence the headline began; a block that reads as the second of two because the first one supplied the subject.

**Edge.** A name the audience knows is not a dangling reference, and a definite form is sound where the noun itself introduces the thing, as in *the queue at a support desk*. What fails is a definite form standing in for something only the source names.

**Minimum safe correction.** Put the referent into the teaser in the fewest words, usually the noun the source uses restored to the first sentence that needs it. Never repair it by leaning on the headline or the surrounding page, which the form may change or move without the teaser being touched.

## The fixed forms

**Test.** Ask what renders this teaser and where it is cut, then read only what survives the cut and check that the declaration, the promise, and the action are all inside it. For a standfirst, read it twice: once as a reader already at the text, once as a reader meeting it in a feed.

**Failure.** A meta description whose sense arrives in a clause the search result will not show; a social post whose hook falls past the platform's fold; a module written to a length its template does not give it; a standfirst that works at the door of its text and says nothing to somebody who has not arrived.

**Edge.** The cut belongs to the surface, so the measure is the limit the placement or the request names and never a number brought from elsewhere. Where nothing names one, there is no cut to measure against and no finding to raise on length.

**Minimum safe correction.** Move what matters in front of the cut before cutting anything: a teaser overruns because its first sentence is throat-clearing far more often than because its last one is surplus. Where it still overruns, cut the part of the pull the reader can do without, never the declaration or the action.

## The source's voice

**Test.** Read the teaser beside the source's opening paragraphs and ask whether the same person wrote both.

**Failure.** A marketing register over an author's article — the exclamation, *excited to share*, a list of benefits; the writer's own voice over a case study whose source holds the client's; a formal summary over a plainly informal text, which mis-sells it exactly as the reverse does.

**Edge.** Compression changes cadence, and a teaser is more clipped than the text it points at without being in a different voice. Where the source has no settled voice of its own — a listing, a notice — there is nothing here to depart from.

**Minimum safe correction.** Replace the imported register's words with the source's own; the structure of the teaser rarely has to move. Where the voice is wrong throughout, write the teaser again from the source's opening rather than editing the draft towards it.
