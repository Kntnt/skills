# Column — review guidance

Diagnostics for the requirements the column genre's base half states, for a Skill reviewing a draft written against them. Nothing here is a requirement: every target a draft has to meet is in the base half, where the Skill that wrote the draft could see it, and a finding raised here is raised against a rule stated there. The examples are English and are patterns — apply what one does, in the language of the draft, rather than looking for its words.

## The pull

**Test.** Read the draft a paragraph at a time and ask after each one what makes somebody read the next: a fact not yet explained, a tension not yet released, a thing the writer has not yet said what they think of. Then ask where the point lands. A column whose point stands at the top and a column with no point at either end fail here for opposite reasons.

**Failure.** Three paragraphs of scene-setting with nothing at stake in them; a first paragraph naming the subject and announcing that the writer has thoughts about it; a piece that states its conclusion in the second sentence and then illustrates it, which leaves the rest of the column as evidence for something already conceded; an ending that stops because the words ran out rather than because the piece got somewhere.

**Edge.** A column may withhold for a long time when the withholding is doing work, and length alone is not the finding. What fails is a stretch that is only preparation. A reflection that ends in a question the writer genuinely cannot settle has arrived; the arrival is the honest uncertainty and not a missing verdict.

**Minimum safe correction.** Move the interest earlier rather than cutting the opening — the sentence with something at stake in it is usually already in the second or third paragraph, and putting it first repairs the opening without touching the arc. Where the point stands at the top, cut it from there and let the place it recurs at the end carry it alone.

## The visible shaping

**Test.** Mark every sentence that exists to manage the reader rather than to say something — a hinge, a signpost, a preview of what the piece is about to do. In this genre each of them is a finding.

**Failure.** *But let us step back for a moment*; *this brings me to my real point*; *there are three things worth noticing here* over a piece that is not a list; a paragraph whose whole content is that the subject is wider than it first appeared; a closing paragraph that names the move the column just made.

**Edge.** A real transition is not a signpost. A sentence that carries information and also turns the piece is doing both jobs and stays. What fails is the sentence with no content of its own.

**Minimum safe correction.** Delete the hinge and let the two paragraphs meet. Where they will not meet without it, the join is doing work the material should be doing, and the repair is a sentence of material rather than a better hinge.

## The author in the text

**Test.** Cover the byline and read a stretch from the middle. Ask whether anybody in particular is talking, and whether you could say what they think.

**Failure.** A stretch of neutral exposition in the middle of an otherwise personal piece — the passage where the writer explains the background and disappears into it; observations attributed to no one, as though the column had assembled itself; a piece whose author is present only in its first and last paragraphs, having gone away for the argument.

**Edge.** Visibility is not volume. A column can be plain, quiet, and formal and still be unmistakably one person's, and the writer need not appear in every paragraph. A ghostwritten column is the named author's voice and not the writer's, and it is judged as that person's presence rather than the writer's absence.

**Minimum safe correction.** Restore the person to the passage that lost them — usually one clause saying what they make of what they have just set out, taken from what the material has them thinking. Do not repair it by adding first-person asides throughout; the failure is a passage, not a rate.

## The freer language

**Test.** Count the departures — fragments, sentences begun on conjunctions, one-line paragraphs, a phrase deliberately repeated — and ask of each what it does to the pace at that point. A departure that would read the same as an ordinary sentence is doing nothing.

**Failure.** Fragments at a steady rate throughout, which is a texture rather than an effect; a one-line paragraph used for a sentence with no weight to take; a repeated phrase that returns unchanged and gains nothing on its returns; loose sentences excused by the genre — a clause dropped because the writer did not finish the thought, rather than because the drop lands.

**Edge.** Nothing obliges a column to use any of these. A column written in ordinary complete sentences fails nothing here, and the absence of a device is never a finding.

**Minimum safe correction.** Restore the surplus departures to ordinary sentences and leave the one that works. Where a repeated phrase is inert, cut its middle occurrences rather than rewriting the phrase; a return reads as a return when there is space between the returns.

## The headings

**Test.** Read the title and the subheadings alone and ask what they do to somebody who has not read the piece: pull them in, or file it.

**Failure.** *Introduction*, *Background*, *Conclusion* over a personal reflection; a subheading that summarises the paragraph beneath it, which spends the paragraph before it is read; a title naming the subject where the piece has a phrase of its own that would serve.

**Edge.** An oblique heading is not a defect here even where it would be in another genre, and a short column with no headings at all is complete. A heading that happens to be plain is fine; what fails is a heading that labels a piece nobody is navigating.

**Minimum safe correction.** Take a phrase from the passage the heading stands over and use that. Where a heading only labels, deleting it is usually better than replacing it, since a column rarely needs the signposting at all.

## The personal material

**Test.** Take every scene, anecdote, exchange, and remembered particular in the draft and find it in the material. Anything you cannot find is a finding, however small and however well it reads.

**Failure.** An opening morning, journey, or conversation the material never mentions; a quoted remark assembled from a paraphrase; a composite client standing in for several; a detail added for texture — the weather, what somebody was holding, what the room felt like — around an event the material records without any of it.

**Edge.** A ghostwritten column is held to this exactly as a first-person one is, and the writer's distance from that life makes invention more tempting rather than more permissible. Something the material states plainly is not invention because it is small. General knowledge that is not about the author — how an industry usually works — is an ordinary claim and is judged as one.

**Minimum safe correction.** Cut the invented material and open on something the material does carry; a column that opens on an observation instead of a scene is a complete column. Never repair an invented scene by making it vaguer — a blurred invention is the same claim with its checkability removed.

## The ending

**Test.** Read the last paragraph alone and ask what the reader is left holding, then ask whether the piece earned it.

**Failure.** A close restating the opening scene as though the return were itself the point; an exhortation in a register the column has not used anywhere else, arriving as though a different writer finished the piece; a final paragraph explaining the point the previous paragraph had already made; an ask the column never set up — a product, a subscription, a service the piece was not about.

**Edge.** A column may end on a small ask, and lightness rather than absence is the measure. It may also end on a plain statement with nothing asked at all, and that is complete.

**Minimum safe correction.** Cut back to the sentence where the point actually lands and stop there; the surplus is almost always after it rather than before. Where the ask is imported, replace it with one in the column's own terms or drop it, and do not rewrite the paragraph around it.
