# Teaser

Short freestanding text whose one job is to move a scanning reader to a text somewhere else: a standfirst, a meta description under a search result, a newsletter block, a teaser module on a website, a social post that exists to tease a linked piece. Its reader has decided nothing and is a second or two from deciding: they go to that text, or they pass on — so nothing in a teaser is there for its own sake, and the text on the other side is what every sentence is finally about.

## What this genre asks for

**A reader meets the parts in the order a scan expects them.** Somebody scanning takes a teaser in one movement, and what they expect to meet is a line naming the text, then the few sentences that make them want it, then the action. The naming line is more often the form's than the teaser's — the headline standing above it, the title beside a search result, the heading on a block — and where the form supplies it the teaser is written under it rather than saying it again. Where the form leaves the action nowhere to go, because the reader's next move is to read on rather than to follow a link, the teaser ends on the pull and nothing is written to fill the place. These are parts a reader recognises and not sections to be labelled: three sentences can carry all of them without announcing any.

**The reader is told what they get.** A teaser declares its contents, which means that from the teaser alone a reader can say what the text on the other side actually holds and whether it is theirs. That takes the subject narrowed to the one thing this text says about it, in the source's own particulars — the count, the outcome, the question it settles, the person who did the thing — because a teaser assembled from the general terms of a field would serve equally well for every other text in that field. *An interesting piece about pricing* asks the reader to guess what is there, and a scanning reader does not guess; they pass on.

**The promise is the source text's to keep.** Everything a teaser leads a reader to expect is a claim about a text they cannot see yet, and what makes such a claim true is not the material behind the teaser but the text on the other side of the click. The reader settles it on arrival, and settles it against what they were led to expect rather than against what was literally said: a teaser defensible sentence by sentence that leaves the reader expecting something the source does not deliver has broken the promise as squarely as a false one would. What breaking it costs is not the click, which has already happened, but the next one.

**The arc is the whole of the teaser.** A teaser has room for one movement and has to make it: the reader is given a footing, something unsettles it, and they are left in no doubt that a consequence follows and that the source has it. That arc is compressed into the whole of the text rather than laid over its parts — every sentence is doing more than one of those jobs at once — and a teaser that allots one sentence to the situation, one to the problem and one to the resolution reads as a form filled in rather than as a text. What the movement stops short of is its own resolution: what the reader is left holding is the pull of an answer that is not here.

**The action names what the click delivers.** The act is the same every time, so naming the act tells a reader nothing they did not know; what earns the click is knowing what is on the other side of it. *See what the audit found* and *take the checklist* name a delivery, where *read more* and *learn more* name the machinery. It is also the last thing read before the decision, which makes it part of the promise rather than an ornament on the end of one: it names something the source actually has.

**It stands entirely alone.** The reader has not seen the source and may never see it, so everything the teaser needs is inside the teaser — no pronoun whose antecedent is in the source, no definite form for something nobody has introduced, no reference back to a text the reader is assumed to have read, and no reliance on whatever happens to sit beside it in a feed, an inbox, or a page. *The system solves the problem* is unreadable to anybody who has met no system and no problem, however plain it is to a writer holding the source. This is stricter here than in any other genre the Collection installs: other texts have a body of their own in which to establish what they refer to, and a teaser has a few sentences, read once, by somebody who is not looking for the thread.

**A fixed form is cut, or placed, by something outside the text.** Several of these forms are not free. A meta description is rendered inside a search result that truncates it, a social post is truncated by the platform, and a newsletter block or a teaser module gets the room its template gives it and is scanned beside its siblings competing for the same second. The cut is made by the surface rather than by the writer, so a teaser whose declaration, promise, or action arrives after the cut was not read at all — and the limit that applies is the one the placement or the request names, never a number carried here. A standfirst is fixed the other way, by placement: it is read at the door of the text it introduces, and the same words are lifted from there to stand as that text's teaser elsewhere, so they have to work for a reader who is already there and for one who is not. Where such a part sits inside a text of a given kind is that genre's to say; what it owes as a text is here.

## What this genre does not do

It does not answer its own complication. A teaser carrying the resolution has handed the reader what the source was for, and a reader who has it has no reason to go anywhere; the same sentence withheld is the whole of what the click is worth. The failure is hard to see from the inside, because the version that answers itself is the most informative version of the teaser and reads as the most generous one.

It does not sell harder than the source can pay for. The constructions written to force a click — the withheld astonishment, the truth nobody will tell you, the error everybody is said to be making — promise a text that almost never exists, and a reader arriving at an ordinary useful piece has learned something about the publisher rather than about the piece. Where the source does hold something remarkable, saying so plainly is accuracy and not this failure.

It does not take a voice the source does not have. A teaser is the source's own first move towards somebody who has not met it, so it sounds like the source compressed: a teaser for one person's article written in the register of a marketing department has mis-sold the text before the reader reaches it, and a teaser for a case study written in the writer's own voice, where the source holds the client's, has done the same thing in the other direction.

It adds no requirement the base contract already holds. Register, structure, sentences, words, claims, openings, endings, formatting, and consistency are stated there once, and a rule repeated here would be one rule made into two things to keep true.

## The technique this genre is ordinarily written with

ABT ([`abt.md`](../techniques/abt.md)). The movement asked for above is that arc at the scale of the whole text — a footing, something that unsettles it, and a consequence the reader is in no doubt follows — so naming it here names what a teaser is already doing.
