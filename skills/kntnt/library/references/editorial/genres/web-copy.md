# Web copy

Text written for a page on a website and for the reader who scans it: somebody who arrived cold from a search result or a link, does not always know where on the site they have landed, and reads the headings and whatever else stands out from the prose before deciding what, if anything, to read properly. An article's reader chose the piece and means to read it through; a report's reader is obliged to it and will come back later for one figure; this reader has committed to nothing, is one click from somewhere else, and will read a fraction of what is on the page. Everything below follows from that reader.

## What this genre asks for

**The surface carries the page.** A scanning reader's first pass takes the headings, the top of what stands under each of them, and whatever is set off from the prose, and for most readers there is no second pass. So what this page is, who it is for, and what it says are reachable from that pass alone: somebody who does not know where on the site they have landed can answer from the surface whether this page is theirs, and somebody who reads nothing else has still been told the thing the page exists to tell them. A page whose substance can only be reached by reading it through has been written for a reader it does not have. This is the constraint the rest of the genre serves, and where another choice pulls against it, this is the one that stands.

**A heading declares what stands under it.** The scanner reads the headings to decide what to skip, so each one is a contract about the contents of its section: it says what the section holds, the section holds that, and a reader who skipped it lost nothing they were promised. A heading that withholds in order to make somebody open the section — the tease, the cliffhanger, the clever line whose point arrives two paragraphs down — gets the section skipped, because the reader deciding has the heading and nothing else to decide on. A declaration made and then broken costs more again: a section that goes somewhere other than where its heading said teaches the reader that the headings on this page cannot be trusted, and they read every later one on the page knowing it. The declaration stands at the front of the heading, where a reader running their eye down the page takes it and where it survives being cut by whatever renders it. What a heading never has to be is interesting; a plain one that says what is there has done the whole job.

**Every section is readable from a cold start.** A page is entered in the middle — by a search result pointing into it, by a link somebody sent, by an eye that landed halfway down — so each section is written for a reader whose reading of the page begins there. A connective reaching backwards, a pronoun whose antecedent is three sections up, a definite form for something named in a section this reader skipped: each works perfectly for somebody who started at the top and leaves everybody else holding a sentence they cannot complete. What carries a section instead is its own material — the term named again where the section turns on it, the figure given again where it rests on it — rather than a smoother bridge. The sections still follow one another for the reader who does read from the top; what they no longer do is depend on having been read in that order. This is the requirement that costs a writer most, because writing straight through is the habit that produces good prose everywhere else: by the fourth section the writer is holding three sections of context that the reader arriving there does not have, which is why a draft written linearly fails this in its later sections and not in its early ones.

**A visual element is the shape of its information.** A page has more ways to set text than prose and a list, and every one of them answers to the test the base contract puts to a list: a table, a pull quote, a callout standing outside the flow, a term set against its definition, a disclosure that folds away what is secondary. Each is used where the information genuinely has that form — things comparable on the same dimensions in the table, a sentence that carries on its own in the pull quote, something the reader can do without behind the disclosure — and never for what it does to the look of the page. The temptation here is not the one ordinary prose has: it is to reach for an element as relief, because the page looks dense and a reader who scans is assumed to want their eye rested. That defeats the thing it was reaching for. A scanner's eye stops at everything set off from the prose, and an element carrying no information is a stop that pays nothing, so a page decorated for scanability scans worse than the plain page it was made from.

**The reader is spoken to rather than described.** The failure this genre falls into is not a missing pronoun but prose sliding into an account of a category the reader happens to belong to: *decision-makers in the sector face growing complexity* is about somebody, and the reader recognises nobody in it, least of all themselves. What produces the sense of being addressed is verbs doing something the reader is actually doing, the situation they would recognise as their own set out in its own particulars, and a question that is genuinely theirs to answer as they read. What does not produce it is a pronoun. Which pronouns a language has and how a text reaches for them is that language's business rather than this genre's; what holds in all of them is that a page can address its reader without ever naming them, and a page that names them in every paragraph while describing throughout has addressed nobody.

## What this genre does not do

It does not settle what is on the page. Which sections a page carries, what stands at the top of it, and what it asks the reader to do belong to whatever asked for the page — the brief, the specification, or the kind of page it is — so a service page, a landing page, and a piece of pillar content take their parts from there and their prose from here. The absence of a shape is deliberate and not an omission: what a scanning reader recognises is how a web page reads, not which sections it has, and a shape written here would be a convention this Collection invented and then imposed on every web page it wrote afterwards. Where the request names no parts, the material and the job imply what the page needs, and nothing is manufactured to fill it out.

It is not an article with subheadings put into it. An article's reader chose the piece and reads it in the order it was written, so its prose may build, hold something back, and rest on what stood four paragraphs earlier; the same prose divided up afterwards has the appearance a scanner reads for and none of what they need underneath it. Where the text really is to be read through, because its reader chose it and means to finish, that is an article; where it is written for a named group to act on and return to, that is a report.

It adds no requirement the base contract already holds. Register, structure, sentences, words, claims, openings, endings, formatting, and consistency are stated there once, and a rule repeated here would be one rule made into two things to keep true.

## The technique this genre is ordinarily written with

ABT ([`abt.md`](../techniques/abt.md)). A page with one thing to say carries the arc across the whole of it. More often the reader meets it inside a section that has to stand alone for whoever entered there, and an arc at that scale is the nested one the technique states, subordinate on the terms stated there rather than a second arc of its own. Where a page is a set of facts somebody came to look up, the arc has little to do and does it in little space; a page is not released from a technique the run resolved because of what its material turned out to be.
