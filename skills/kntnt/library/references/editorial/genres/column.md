# Column

A personal reflection published where strangers will read it — a column in a magazine or a trade publication, a guest column on somebody else's site, a piece whose interest is that this particular person is the one thinking it. The author is visible and the text draws: it takes the reader along and reaches its point by arriving there, so that somebody who has followed the whole way ends up holding a thought rather than a conclusion they were handed.

## What this genre asks for

**The reader is drawn along, and the point arrives at the end.** A column holds its reader the way a story does rather than the way an argument does: something is set going in the first lines — an observation that does not add up, a small event, a thing somebody said — and each paragraph is worth the next one for its own sake. That is not an exception to ordering a text by its reader's need but an instance of it, because what this reader came for is the going: a point delivered at the top would be a claim they could take or leave, and a reader who took it would have no reason to stay. What a column may never do is make them wait for nothing. Early paragraphs that are only preparation spend the reader's patience on the writer's convenience, and whatever justifies the delay has to be working from the first lines.

**The shaping never shows.** A column goes somewhere and was built to go there, and the reader follows the piece rather than the frame it was built on. Where the joins show — a paragraph whose only work is to turn the piece, a sentence announcing that the reflection is about to widen, a passage visibly doing duty as setup — the reader stops reading the column and starts watching it work, and the sense that somebody is simply thinking aloud does not survive being seen through.

**The author is in the text.** A column is published because a particular person is thinking in it, so the writer is present as themselves: what they noticed, what they make of it, what they are prepared to be uncertain about. The reader is spoken to rather than written at, and how a language realises that address is that language's business and not this genre's. What breaks the genre is not a formal sentence but a stretch of neutral exposition: at the point where a reader can no longer tell who is talking, they are reading an article with a byline on it.

**The language is freer, and the freedom is for the ear.** A column may do what tidier prose does not — a fragment where a fragment lands, a sentence begun on a conjunction, a paragraph of one line, a phrase that returns through the piece and carries more each time it comes back. Every one of those is a device of pace: it holds the reader a beat longer, drops the floor from under a sentence, or makes a phrase's return mean something the phrase did not mean the first time. The freedom is not permission to write loosely, and it is spent only where it does that work — a draft in which every third sentence is a fragment has no fragments, only a texture, and the effect it was reaching for is gone.

**The title and the subheadings may draw rather than label.** In most kinds of text they help a reader navigate; here their first job is to pull somebody forward, so a heading may be oblique — a phrase lifted from the piece, an aside, a line whose point the reader gets only on reaching it. Nothing obliges a column's heading to declare what stands under it, and a heading that gives away the paragraph beneath it has spent that paragraph before anybody reads it.

**The personal material is the material's.** The column's characteristic opening is a scene — something that happened to the author, somebody they met, a thing that was said — which makes it the first thing a draft invents, since the genre invites one and the material often carries none. No anecdote, scene, exchange, or remembered detail enters a column unless the material carries it; where the material carries none, the column opens on something else it does have, and it is not made personal by supplying what a person of that sort plausibly would have experienced. This holds exactly as strictly for a column ghostwritten in somebody else's name, which is the case the rule exists for: the writer knows that life only through the material, and knowing less about it is not a licence to fill it in.

**The ending is the arrival.** The thought the column was for is delivered at the end, in the plainest words the piece has used, and it is delivered rather than explained: a column that lands its point and then unpacks it has taken back the recognition it spent the whole piece setting up. Where a column turns outward — asks the reader for something, points at something worth doing — it asks lightly and in the terms the column itself established, because a reader who has come the whole way with somebody thinking aloud hears an exhortation imported from elsewhere as the moment that person stopped being themselves.

## What this genre does not do

It does not argue. A column makes its point by taking the reader to it, so a draft that declares a position and then marshals a case for it has changed genre while keeping the byline. Where the material calls for the push rather than the pull, that is an opinion piece and is written as one.

It does not step back behind its material. An article's writer stands behind what they are reporting and lets it speak; a column's stands in front of it and is part of what the reader is there for. The two may take the same subject and the same facts, and what separates them is who is visible.

It fixes no sequence of parts. There is no opening a column owes and no part that has to stand in a particular place: the movement belongs to the reflection, and where it goes is the writer's to find in the material. A column assembled to a pattern reads as one, which costs it the only thing the genre has.

It adds no requirement the base contract already holds. Register, structure, sentences, words, claims, openings, endings, formatting, and consistency are stated there once, and a rule repeated here would be one rule made into two things to keep true.

## The technique this genre is ordinarily written with

ABT ([`abt.md`](../techniques/abt.md)). A column draws rather than argues, and what draws is a complication the reader follows to its arrival. Here the arc is subject to the genre's own rule above that the shaping never shows: it is what the piece is built on and never what the reader is watching.
