# Questions to answer before you write

The Writing Brief template: each numbered question carries the guidance for answering it. This English reference is authoritative; render its questions and guidance in the user's working language while retaining every question, its number and its order. Its genre and technique examples explain the form, not what is installed: the Library directories supply those choices, and where a genre and this guidance differ, the genre governs.

How to use the template: answer the questions in full sentences and as concretely as you can. The details under the assignment may be short. You may fill in the questions in whatever order suits you, but the order below is the one the brief is read in. It runs from what the assignment gives you, through the context and the shape of the text, to a check that everything holds together. When every answer is there, you have a brief. If you cannot answer a question, you need to find out more before you start writing. Sources and evidence, on the other hand, can be added during the work. Refer to other answers by name, for example "the message" or "the hook", not by the question's number.

## 1. What is the assignment?

Here you gather what the assignment gives you. Most of it needs no thinking up, but it governs everything that follows.

- **Working title:** a name for the text, so that everyone immediately remembers what it is about. It need not be punchy, effective or even accurate. It only needs to be short and clear.
- **Topic:** what the text is about, in a few words, for example "remote work" or "Christmas baking".
- **Genre:** for example article, case study, column, opinion piece, press release, report, teaser or web copy.
- **Channel:** for example website, blog, web magazine, newsletter, LinkedIn or newspaper.
- **Language:** for example `sv` or `en_GB`; this is the future text's language, independently of the language the brief is written in. If the text is to exist in several languages, give the source language and how the other versions are produced.
- **Length:** often governed by genre and channel. Give roughly how many words or characters the text should have. The length decides how much the structure can hold. If you do not know yet, give a provisional range and narrow it once the sketch is done.
- **Search phrase:** if the text is to be found through search, give the keyword or phrase it should be visible for. If the text is deliberately written without one, say so.

## 2. Which brand is the sender?

Name the brand the text is published under. In a group of companies it is rarely the group itself. Then describe the brand. If the brand has a platform, a tone-of-voice guide or another description, it is enough to link to it and write out only what governs this particular text, for example distrust that needs handling or a limit to the mandate. The tone of this text you always describe.

- **Promise:** what the brand promises its audience and wants to be known for.
- **Image:** how the reader perceives the brand today, and whether there is trust to build on or distrust to handle.
- **Mandate:** what the brand can speak about with credibility.
- **Tone of voice:** how the brand sounds.
- **Tone in this text:** the brand's tone of voice and the genre's conventions must both be respected. Where they pull in different directions, choose a compromise that sounds natural to both. If they pull the same way, say so.

## 3. What does the sender want to convey with this text?

Every text has a reason, and the reason can nearly always be put as a message. The brand's general message belongs to the description of the brand and often lies close to the promise. Here the message is that of this particular text. It may lie close to the promise, but need not. Write the message as a claim in one sentence. The message need not appear in the text. It can be conveyed indirectly, through what the text is about and where it leads the reader. But you need to know what it is, because it decides which conclusion the text should land in. An example: a company selling a tool for video meetings writes an article about remote work. The article lands in the claim that remote teams work better with short daily check-ins than with long weekly meetings. The company's message is that its tool makes the short check-ins easy. That message may never appear in the text.

## 4. Who is the reader?

Describe the typical reader of content in the chosen genre and the chosen channel. What do they already know about the topic? What do they care about? What makes them want to read this kind of content? Is there something that makes the topic particularly pressing for them right now, for example a season, a piece of news, a decision or a new figure? What you write here about why now should also show in the structure.

## 5. What is the text's angle?

The angle is the particular perspective you choose on the topic. Choose only one. The most common mistake is trying to cover the topic from every direction so that the text becomes comprehensive. The result is a text that says a little about a lot and that the reader finds hard to take in. If you see several good angles, make one text of each. You then get more texts out of the same research, and each becomes clearer. Nor does a topic ever run out. Newspapers write about Christmas baking every year. One year it is about the classics, another about biscuits without sugar and a third about baking with children. Choose the angle that lies closest to the reader and that can carry the message. The angle governs the hook, the structure and the conclusion.

## 6. What is the hook?

The hook is what makes the text interesting to the reader. It is the tension that makes the reader want to read to the end: the question, the conflict or the surprise the text promises to resolve. The hook carries the angle. In the structure, the hook becomes ABT's But or PAC's premise. The hook is not the same thing as the opening. A text can begin with a scene or a detail that catches attention, but it is the hook that holds the reader. In short texts the two often coincide. The reader still needs to understand what is at stake before the hook arrives. In a press release, the hook is the news.

## 7. How should the text be structured?

Choose a technique, or follow the genre's own form, and then sketch the text. Begin with the whole text in one sentence, in the form below for the chosen technique. Then sketch it step by step: what each step says and which facts, figures and examples carry it. The sketch is the part of the brief the writer gets most use from. Make sure the sketch fits the length the assignment gives. The technique gives the text its arc but does not decide how the content sounds. An ABT text can explain and reason, and a PAC text can contain narrative examples.

- **ABT (And, But, Therefore)** suits a text that tells: a situation and what is at stake in it, what chafes and why, and what it leads to. The And and the But come quickly to what chafes. The Therefore is the rest of the text and is divided into sections with subheadings. The whole text in one sentence takes the form "[the situation] and if [...] then [...], but [the problem] because [...], therefore [...] by [...]", after Randy Olson's *Lincoln But Trump*.
- **PAC (Premise, Analysis, Conclusion)** suits a text that reasons: a question, an analysis of what the material means, and an answer. The premise comes quickly to the problem. The analysis is the largest part of the text and is divided into sections with subheadings. The whole text in one sentence takes the form "[the question or starting point], and [what the analysis shows], which means that [the conclusion]", after Gordon Harvey's *Elements of the Essay* and Skolverket's *Utredande text*.
- **The genre's own form** applies to genres whose structure belongs to the genre, for example the press release. You then choose no technique but follow the form the genre states. Where a genre names an ordinary technique of its own, that technique is its form. The whole text in one sentence gives the text's main point and how the text reaches it, in the order the reader meets them, with no fixed pattern.
- **Free structure** suits a text where none of the forms works. Begin with an opening that catches the reader and give the main point early. Then take one step at a time, in logical order, and close with an ending that ties the text together and points forward. Sketch the steps in the order they are to be read. The whole text in one sentence gives the text's main point and how the text reaches it, in the order the reader meets them, with no fixed pattern.

Sketch the ending too. It ties back to the hook and shows that it is resolved, and it carries the text's call to action, directly or indirectly.

Where a genre explicitly advises against a technique, as a report does against ABT and a press release against any arc laid over its news-first order, say so with the genre's reason. A genre's ordinary technique of none is not itself advice against an arc.

## 8. What should be left out?

State boundaries, side tracks you deliberately drop, and claims you cannot substantiate. Include also what lies outside the brand's mandate or what the brand for other reasons cannot or will not say. If a boundary has exceptions, write out where the line goes, for example something that may be reported but not argued for. Refer to the brand's writing guidelines if it has any. Many boundaries become visible only once you sketch the structure.

## 9. What should the reader have understood afterwards?

The answer is the text's conclusion. Write it as a claim in one sentence, not as a topic. "Remote work" is a topic. "Remote teams work better with short daily check-ins than with long weekly meetings" is a conclusion. The conclusion is what the structure lands in, that is ABT's Therefore or PAC's Conclusion, summed up in one sentence. With the genre's own form or free structure, the conclusion is the text's main point. If it cannot be summed up in one sentence, the sketch needs sharpening. The conclusion carries the message, either by stating it or by leading the reader to it. It lies within the brand's mandate and gives the reader something worth their time. What the understanding should lead to you answer in the next question.

## 10. What should the reader feel, think or do afterwards?

Describe the change in the reader the brand wants to bring about with the text. The conclusion is about what the reader understands. This question is about what that leads to.

- **Main effect:** choose one main effect and put it so concretely that it can be decided whether it has been reached. The effect can concern what the reader thinks or feels, for example "sees the brand as the obvious expert in the field". It can also concern what the reader does, for example "signs up for the webinar" or "contacts a salesperson".
- **The way there:** if the main effect is reached in several steps, describe what the reader first needs to see, understand or believe.
- **Call to action and measure:** what the reader is to do is the text's call to action. It can be direct, for example "contact our sales team". It can also be indirect: the text does not say it, but the reader is left with the feeling that they should act and knows how. If the effect can be measured, state also what shows it has been reached, for example sign-ups, clicks or enquiries. If the text deliberately has no call to action or no measure, say so, so that it shows as a choice and not a gap.

## 11. What makes the text RIV for the reader, and does the brief hold together?

This question checks that everything above serves the reader and holds together. Answer from what you have already written and state where in the structure each part is found. If something is missing there, add it now.

- **Relevant:** What makes the text relevant to the reader and their situation? Why should they read it at all? Is what makes the topic pressing right now part of the structure? The text must at least be relevant.
- **Interesting:** What makes the reader find the text interesting to read? Most often it is the hook.
- **Valuable:** What value should the text give the reader? What makes the reader feel the text was worth their time? Most often it is the conclusion and what carries it.

Then read the answers in one sweep and check that the chain holds:

- Does the reader follow from the sender and the channel?
- Does the angle lie close to the reader, and does the hook carry the angle?
- Does the structure resolve the hook's tension, and does it fit the length?
- Is the conclusion what the structure actually lands in, does it carry the message, and does it lie within the brand's mandate?
- Does what the reader understands lead to the effect you have described, and does that effect serve the brand's goals?
- Does anything in the structure conflict with what should be left out?

Write the check down: answer yes, no or partly to each question and give a short reason. Fix what does not hold together now. It is much easier than fixing it in a finished draft. Questions about the direction, that is the angle, the message and the conclusion, need to be settled before you write. Questions that need more research you write down as open questions and settle during the work.

## 12. What sources do you know of, and what do you need to find out?

A brief is often written before the research is done, and the research is often part of the assignment. Write down the sources you already know of or know you have access to, for example reports, statistics, earlier texts and interviews. Link also to the brand's platform or tone-of-voice guide, if you have not already done so under the sender. Link where you can and number the sources. If you already know that a claim in the sketch can be substantiated, refer to the source there. Add to the list as you find more, and note what you need to find out. It is no requirement that everything in the brief is substantiated. The requirement applies to the finished text. There every claim must be possible to substantiate with a source, an interview or your research, or be a generally accepted claim that the sender can make without risk.
