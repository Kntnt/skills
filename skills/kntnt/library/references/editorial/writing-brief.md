# 13 questions before you write

The Writing Brief template: each numbered question carries guidance for eliciting its answer and the quality criteria for judging that answer. This English reference is authoritative; render its questions and guidance in the user's working language while retaining every question, its number and order. Its genre and technique examples explain the form, not what is installed: the Library directories supply those choices.

Answers belong in order, in full sentences and as concretely as possible. When every answer is there, they form a brief. An unanswered question identifies what needs to be found out before writing; a plausible inference is not an answer supplied by the writer.

## 1. What is the assignment?

- **Topic:** what the text is about, in a few words, such as remote work or Christmas baking.
- **Genre:** for example article, case study, column, opinion piece, press release, report, teaser or web copy.
- **Channel:** for example website, blog, web magazine, newsletter, LinkedIn or newspaper.

**Quality:** the topic takes a few words, the genre is installed (`general` is a genre), and the channel is a concrete choice.

## 2. Who is the reader?

Describe the typical reader of content in the chosen genre and channel. What do they already know about the topic? What do they care about? What makes them want to read this kind of content?

**Quality:** a defined group, rather than everyone or the general public, with its prior knowledge, interests and reason to read.

## 3. What makes the text RIV for the reader?

- **Relevant:** what makes the text relevant to the reader and their situation? Why read it at all? What makes it particularly relevant now, such as a season, news or a decision?
- **Interesting:** what makes the reader find the text interesting to read? What catches their interest?
- **Valuable:** what value should the text give the reader? What makes it worth their time?

**Quality:** R is specific to this reader and always present. I and V may be absent only as a deliberate choice, stated as such.

## 4. Which brand is the sender?

Name the brand the text is published under; in a group of companies this is rarely the group itself. Describe:

- **Promise:** what it promises its audience and wants to be known for.
- **Image:** how the reader perceives it today, with any trust to build on or distrust to handle.
- **Mandate:** what it can speak about with credibility.
- **Tone of voice:** how it sounds.

**Quality:** a named brand with at least mandate and tone of voice. A link to its brand platform suffices as an answer; it does not license inventing the contents of an unread platform.

## 5. What is the text's angle?

The angle is the particular perspective taken on the topic. Choose only one, closest to the reader. Covering every direction to make a text comprehensive commonly produces a little about a lot, which is hard to take in. Several good angles make several texts from the same research, each clearer. A topic never runs out: newspapers revisit Christmas baking yearly, with classics one year, sugar-free biscuits another, baking with children a third. The angle governs the message in the next question.

**Quality:** one angle. Where several are given, propose splitting them into separate texts.

## 6. What should the reader have understood afterwards?

This is the message the brand wants to put across: a claim in one sentence, not a topic. "Remote work" is a topic; "Remote teams work better with short daily check-ins than with long weekly meetings" is a message. The message lies within the brand's mandate and gives the reader the value described in question 3. The next question asks what this understanding should lead to.

**Quality:** a one-sentence claim within the brand's mandate, rather than a subject label.

## 7. What should the reader feel, think or do afterwards?

Describe the change in the reader the brand wants. Question 6 concerns understanding; this concerns its effect. Choose one main effect, concrete enough to tell whether it happened. A thought or feeling might be seeing the brand as the obvious expert in the field. An action might be signing up for a webinar or contacting a salesperson; an intended action is the call to action. Where measurable, state what indicates success, such as sign-ups, clicks or enquiries.

**Quality:** one main effect concrete enough to assess, distinct from the message in question 6.

## 8. How should the text be structured?

- **ABT (And, But, Therefore)** suits a text that tells: describe a situation, show what chafes, and land in what it leads to. It suits most articles, since nearly every article contains a situation, a problem and a solution, whether portraying a person, event, discovery, technique or decision. It also suits case studies, columns, teasers and web copy.
- **PAC (Premise, Analysis, Conclusion)** suits a text that reasons: put forward a premise, analyse it and draw a conclusion. It suits explanatory and analytical articles, columns, opinion pieces, reports and web copy.
- **Free structure** follows neither formula. Build on what nearly every genre shares: an opening that catches the reader, the message early, one step at a time in logical order, and an ending that ties things together and points forward. A press release uses this: the order of its parts is its whole structure.

**Library authority:** the resolved genre governs where this guidance and the Library differ. Suggest the genre's ordinary technique where it names one; where it names none, suggest from the tell-or-reason distinction above. A genre's ordinary `none` is not itself advice against an arc: choosing ABT or PAC there is legitimate. Where a genre explicitly advises against the chosen technique, mark the answer weak and quote the genre's reason. In particular, a press release's news-first order must not be displaced by an arc.

**Quality:** an installed technique or free structure, justified by genre and whether the text tells or reasons. A technique the selected genre advises against is weak. A deliberate choice to leave the technique to the genre is explicit; free structure means `none`, while leaving the choice to the genre leaves technique metadata absent.

## 9. What is the hook?

The hook makes I in question 3 concrete. It is the tension that makes the reader want to reach the end: a question, conflict or surprise the text promises to resolve. In ABT it is the But; in PAC it is the premise. It differs from an opening scene or detail that attracts attention: the hook holds that attention. In short texts the two often coincide.

**Quality:** a tension rather than merely an opening sentence, fitting both the angle and the technique.

## 10. What sources and evidence do you have?

Link or refer to everything known after initial research: facts, figures, reports, earlier texts, interviews and quotations. Include the brand platform or tone-of-voice guide where available so everything is in one place for writing. Mark what is still missing.

**Quality:** concrete sources with links or references, and missing evidence marked.

## 11. What should be left out?

State boundaries, side tracks deliberately dropped and claims that cannot be substantiated. Include what lies outside the brand's mandate and what it cannot or will not say for other reasons.

**Quality:** at least one stated boundary.

## 12. What is the working title?

This is a name that makes everyone immediately remember what the text is about. It need not be punchy, effective or even accurate as a finished headline; it only needs to be short and clear.

**Quality:** short and clear about the subject.

## 13. What frame applies to the text?

- **Language:** for example `sv` or `en_GB`; this is the future text's language, independently of the language of the brief.
- **Tone:** respect both the brand's voice from question 4 and the genre's conventions. Where they pull apart, choose a compromise natural to both.
- **Length:** roughly how many words or characters; genre and channel often govern it.
- **Search phrase:** if the text is to be found through search, state the keyword or phrase it should rank for.

**Quality:** a language and approximate length. For a web channel, a search phrase or a deliberate decision to have none.

## Check before you start writing

Read answers 2–7 in one sweep. Does the text give this reader at least R? Does the message follow from the angle? Does the understanding lead to the stated effect, and does that effect serve the brand's goals? Also check that the hook carries the angle. Fix what does not hang together while it is a brief, when doing so is easier than repairing a finished draft. Where an answer is still missing or a relationship unconfirmed, name the unresolved question rather than declaring the check passed.
