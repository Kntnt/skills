# The mechanics contract

Objective correctness, in the half of it that does not depend on which language a text is in. A Skill contracted to mechanical correction reads this document together with the resolved language's own mechanics guidance, and the two together are that run's rules. What is true of every language the Collection installs is stated here and stated once, so that it is not written again in each language, where the copies would be free to disagree with one another about what is an error.

Nothing here is a preference. A rule belongs in this document only where the text is wrong whatever the writer intended, and only where it would be wrong in the same way in any language. Spelling, inflection, agreement, the language's own punctuation, and its conventions for dates, numbers, currency, and quotation all turn on the language and belong to that language's resource. Where a language defaults more strictly or more loosely than a rule below, its own guidance says so, and what it says about itself wins.

The examples are English because this document is, and they are patterns rather than strings. Apply each by what it does in the target language rather than by the words it happens to use.

## Clause boundaries

Two independent clauses joined by a comma alone are an error where the second does not explain, specify, restate, or stand in direct contrast to the first. *The project will be expensive, it starts in the spring* puts two statements in one sentence that have nothing to do with each other beyond sitting there together: the reader takes the comma as a signal that the sentence continues, and meets a new subject and a new verb instead. Two clauses run together with no punctuation at the joint at all are the same error, and take the same correction.

The same joint is not an error where the second clause does explain, specify, restate, or contrast with the first. *The bug was not in the parser, it was in a silent retry* is the negative-positive contrast, where the comma is what holds the two halves together as one statement, and separating them would break a single thought into two. *We shipped it anyway, nobody had a better idea* specifies the first clause with the reason behind it. Both are established usage rather than tolerated looseness, and a pass that corrects them has changed how the sentence reads without being asked to.

Judge a joint by whether the clauses cohere, and not by their length, by how formal the text is, or by how the text punctuates elsewhere. Where the reading is genuinely open — where a careful reader could defend the joint — the comma stands, because what this rule reaches is the boundary that misleads and not the boundary that is merely informal.

Correct an erroneous joint at the joint and nowhere else: the comma becomes a period or a semicolon, and nothing else about the sentence moves. Do not reorder the clauses, do not merge them into one, and do not supply a conjunction — *and*, *but*, and *so* each assert a relation between the two clauses, and the writer asserted none. Neither half is rewritten, split, or tightened on the way past. Where more than one of a period and a semicolon would serve, the period is the safer of the two, since it claims nothing about how closely the statements are related.

## Quotation

Quoted matter reaches a reader in one of three settings, and the setting decides what the punctuation and capitalisation around it have to do. A quotation is run into a sentence, set off as a block of its own, or reported as speech, and a reader takes each of the three as a different promise about where the sentence ends, where the source's words begin, and who is speaking. The marks are the language's. Which glyphs enclose a quotation, which side of a closing mark a stop falls on, and how speech is conventionally set are stated in the resolved language's mechanics scope and not here, and the marks in the examples below stand in for whichever ones it names. What follows holds whatever they turn out to be — which setting a quotation is in, and what that setting then requires.

A run-in quotation is part of the sentence carrying it, and the joint between the two is punctuated by what the quoted words do there. Where the quotation is introduced as an utterance of its own, a stop stands at the joint and the quotation opens the way a sentence opens: *She replied, "I'll be there."* Where the quoted words are instead a grammatical part of the host sentence — its object, its complement, a fragment the sentence reads straight through — no stop stands at the joint and the quotation takes the case the surrounding syntax needs: *She called the proposal "completely unacceptable"*, not *She called the proposal, "Completely unacceptable"*. A stop at that joint promises the reader an utterance and then hands them a sentence with no room for one. Which of a comma and a colon introduces an utterance is a matter for the writer and for the language; whether a stop stands at the joint at all is neither.

The sentence around a run-in quotation still ends, and it ends once. A quoted sentence's own final stop and the host sentence's stop are one stop and not two, and where the quoted matter ends in a question or exclamation mark that mark stands alone and takes no comma beside it: *"Will you?" she asked*, not *"Will you?", she asked*. A reporting clause is bound to the utterance it reports rather than being a sentence in its own right, so it takes no capital and never stands behind a full stop: *"I'll come," she said*, not *"I'll come." She said.* Which side of the closing mark the surviving stop falls on is settled by the language and not here.

A block quotation is marked by being set off, and that setting is the whole of the mark. Marks enclosing a block state the quotation a second time, and they cost the reader the one thing the block was doing for them: inside a block, marks are the source's own and are preserved as the source wrote them. Because the block itself takes no level, a quotation inside one stands at the outermost level the language has rather than at the level it would take nested inside another.

The sentence introducing a block has finished by the time the block begins, and the block ends on the quoted matter's own punctuation, so the surrounding sentence's stop does not follow the block and no part of the host sentence is left hanging past it. A source named with a block sits outside the quotation and after it; set inside, the attribution reads as something the source wrote about itself.

Reported speech is punctuated as a run-in quotation is, and what the setting adds is the layout that says who is speaking. A turn belongs to one speaker and takes a paragraph of its own, and a second speaker beginning inside the first speaker's paragraph is read as the first speaker still talking, however plainly the words belong to somebody else. Where one speaker's turn runs across more than one paragraph, the continuation is marked as the same turn, because a reader shown nothing takes the second paragraph for somebody else's turn: a new paragraph is what a change of speaker looks like in this setting. How a language marks the continuation is its own — a turn left unclosed until its last paragraph, a speech mark opening every one of them — but that the reader can tell one continued turn from two is not the language's to settle.

A quotation promises that the words inside it are the source's, and three departures from that promise are each marked where they occur. Words added or altered so that the quotation fits its host sentence — a pronoun resolved, a tense harmonised, an initial capital raised or lowered to suit the surrounding syntax — are marked as the writer's rather than the source's: *"[T]he proposal was unacceptable"*. Words left out are marked at the gap, so that the reader is not shown a continuous sentence the source never wrote: *"the proposal … was unacceptable"*. An error the source made is kept and marked as the source's — *[sic]* — because quoted matter is not the text's own, and an error corrected inside a quotation makes the quotation false about its source. Unmarked, each of the three reads as the source's own words, which is the single thing the reader took the quotation to guarantee.

## Abbreviations spelled out

The capitals in an abbreviation are a property of the abbreviation and say nothing about the words behind it, so a spelled-out form is capitalised the way those same words are capitalised anywhere else in the text. *Search engine optimisation* and *hypertext markup language* are the forms; *Search Engine Optimisation* and *HyperText Markup Language* are the error, and it comes from reading the capitals backwards out of *SEO* and *HTML*. What it does to a reader is assert a name the text does not have: capitals in running prose mark a proper noun, and a reader meeting them takes the words for the title of a product, a standard, or an organisation rather than for a description of what the abbreviation stands for.

Where the words behind an abbreviation are a proper name, they keep the capitals the name has anyway: *North Atlantic Treaty Organization*, *World Health Organization*. That is the name's capitalisation and not the abbreviation's, which is why the abbreviation is no guide to it — *WHO* and *HTML* look alike, and the words behind them are not the same kind of thing. Correct at the spelled-out form and nowhere else: the abbreviation keeps its own capitals, and where and how the text introduces it is untouched.

## Lists

A list holds one convention for punctuation and capitalisation, and holds it in every item. Which convention it can hold follows from what the items are. Items that are complete sentences are punctuated as sentences, capital and terminal stop, because a sentence does not stop being one for sitting in a list; items that are fragments completing the sentence that introduces them are punctuated as parts of that sentence, and a list of fragments therefore has more than one correct treatment. What is wrong in either case is holding two at once, because the reader takes the change as meaning something: the fragment among sentences reads as truncated, and the one item ending in a stop reads as the end of the list.

Correct at the items that depart from the convention the rest of the list keeps, and nowhere else. The stop closing the last item of a list of fragments is the introducing sentence ending rather than a departure from the list's convention, and it stands. Where the items are genuinely of both kinds, what would have to change is an item and not its punctuation, and rewriting an item is past where a mechanical pass stops.

## Established variants

Where a language establishes more than one correct form, the text's own choice stands. A form is not an error because another form is more common, more modern, or the one the reader would have written, and the correction is never to normalise the text towards one of them. Only inconsistency within one text is corrected, and it is corrected towards whichever of the established forms that text already uses more.

That preserves nothing the rules in hand name as an error. A construction this contract or the resolved language's own mechanics guidance identifies as wrong is corrected however consistently the text commits to it, because consistency is what an established variant is judged by and never what makes something established. A text that is wrong the same way throughout is wrong throughout.
